# Seven: sheva / sheviis / shavua / shabbos / shevuah

## Bottom line

*Sheviis*, "the seventh," is the Sabbatical year, and it is the Mishnah's usual
word for it. *Shavua* is a **week**, but in several mishnayos, and seemingly
throughout Daniel, it is a **seven-year cycle**. *Shabbos* is the day and the
Sabbatical **year**, and by synecdoche the **week** the day closes (Ramban),
though Rabbi Mecklenburg objects that nowhere else in Tanach does it mean a
week, and reads it in Vayikra 23 as cessation. *Shevuah* is an **oath**; most of
the authorities Rabbi Klein brings tie it to *sheva*, though they differ on why.
*Sheva* itself is not always the number: Rasag renders it "much" at Bereishis
4:24 and "many" at Mishlei 24:16.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Does shabbos mean "week"?**

- In "you shall count seven complete *shabbasos*" (Vayikra 23:15-16), Ibn Ezra
  and Ramban hold that the word carries two senses within a few pesukim: the
  first two occurrences name a holy day, the first day of Pesach, and the last
  two mean weeks. Targum Onkelos reflects exactly this, rendering the first pair
  *yoma tava* and the second *shavua*. Ramban adds that *shabbos* for "week" is
  common in rabbinic speech (*Taanis* 4:7, *Megillah* 3:4, *Kesubos* 1:1,
  5:6-7, *Eduyos* 4:10, *Nedarim* 8:1, *Bava Metzia* 9:11, *Bava Basra* 5:10,
  *Sheviis* 2:6, *Niddah* 4:5), and calls the word a synecdoche: a day within
  the week and the week entire. By the same token the Sabbatical year is called
  *shabbos* (Vayikra 25:2, 25:4, 25:6, 26:34-35; Divrei HaYamim II 36:21), and
  so is the seven-year cycle that contains it (Vayikra 25:8), the name for the
  Sabbatical year highlighting its Shabbos-like rules requiring one to rest from
  working the ground.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg objects that nowhere else in Tanach does
  *shabbos* mean week, and reads it there in its plain sense of ceasing: between
  the Omer on the second day of Pesach and the Two Loaves on Shavuos no new grain
  is used in the Mikdash, so those weeks may be characterized as a cessation.
  The Torah says *shabbasos* in Parshas Emor to allude to that stopping of
  offerings from the new grain, and he says a *chiddush*: even new barley is not
  brought between the sixteenth of Nissan and Shavuos, which bears in practice
  on the *minchas sotah*.

**Shabbos or shavua, when both mean week.**

- Mecklenburg: *shabbos* is specifically the calendar week that runs from Sunday
  to Shabbos, which lends the day's name to the whole; *shavua*, from *sheva*, is
  any seven-day stretch and need not begin on Sunday.
- Rabbi Dr. Alter HaLevi Hilowitz presumes that *shavua* too means the calendar
  week, and draws a consequence: if the *shavuos* counted to the festival are
  calendar weeks, the count would always begin on a Sunday, which is the
  heretical Tzedoki position; on his account the name Shavuos lends credence to
  it, and that is why Chazal refrained from using it and called the festival
  *Atzeres* instead.
- Mecklenburg's own reading runs against Hilowitz's. Parshas Emor says
  *shabbasos* (Vayikra 23:15-16) for the cessation of offerings from the new
  grain; Parshas Re'eh says *shavuos* (Devarim 16:9) because from *shabbasos*
  alone one would have understood weeks that begin on Sunday, so there the Torah
  makes clear with *shavuos* that any kind of week is meant.

**Why an oath is a shevuah.** Six accounts:

1. **Sevenfold strengthening.** Rava rules that saying "no, no" or "yes, yes" is
   an oath (*Shevuos* 36a), which Rashi explains as strengthening by repetition,
   conceptually like an oath, which is meant to strengthen the force of what one
   says. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon joins the two
   senses of ש-ב-ע by defining the "swear" sense as "seven oneself, or bind
   oneself by seven things." And treaty oaths in Tanach were accompanied by seven
   animals (Bereishis 21:23-33) or a multiple of seven
   (Divrei HaYamim II 15:11-15), so that, Rabbi Klein concludes, it seems the
   number seven was somehow associated with affirming the oath taken, and all
   oaths came to be called *shevuah* in consequence. *Sefer HaChinuch* (mitzvah
   330) explains the connection between *shevuah* and *sheva*: repeating a
   thing seven times strengthens that speech act and symbolizes its everlasting
   value. Onkelos renders the oath verb at Bereishis 24:9
   with a cognate of *kiyum*, establishing and everlasting.
2. **Hashem is the Seventh.** Ibn Ezra suggests in several places, building on
   *Sefer Yetzirah* 4:2 that
   the first six numbers are the six directions and the seventh is Hashem at the
   center as Prime Mover: to swear is to tie one's claim to His existence, as
   though saying, just as Hashem is true, so is this. Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis
   21:31) and Ramban (to Bamidbar 30:3) both comment on the link cryptically
   without elaborating.
3. **Delivering oneself into His hand.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 21:23): since
   Hashem made the world in seven days, seven came to be associated with Him and
   to symbolize universally His role as Creator and Master of the world, so the
   one who swears actively places himself under His direct control, as if to say
   that if what he says is untrue he hereby delivers himself into Hashem's hand
   to be dealt with justly. Seven went on being a
   significant stock number in many ancient Near Eastern texts.
4. **Captivity.** Mecklenburg, Pappenheim, and Rav Hirsch all connect *shevuah*
   to *shevuyah*, a captive: the sworn man is bound and cannot move freely.
5. **Satiation.** Rabbi Moshe Elyakim Briah Hopstein: *shevuah* is akin to
   *seviah*, being satisfied, since swearing in Hashem's name is a way of
   clinging to Him, and the righteous are not satisfied until they cling to Him
   entirely.
6. **Tying the claim to This World's higher purpose.** Maharal (*Derech Chaim*
   to Avos 5:15) sets out three worlds, each with its number: This World, which
   we experience, is four; the Highest World, the Divine world of direct
   emanations from Hashem, is ten; and the Middle World, the celestial world of
   the *mazalos*, which joins This World to the Highest World as the conduit for
   Hashem's influx of *shefa*, is seven. Lying between four and ten, seven also
   connotes connecting This World to a higher purpose, a man using his free will
   to choose goodness and holiness, and following such connections reveals the
   true purpose of This World. Rabbi Eliyahu Menachem Margulies (*Emunas
   Eliezer*, *Yerach HaEisanim* ch. 9, and see ch. 18) notes that the Torah, in
   giving the etymology of the place-name Be'er Sheva, shows a connection
   between *sheva* and *shevuah*: "therefore he called that place Be'er Sheva
   [literally, the seventh well], because there they both swore" (Bereishis
   21:31). On the Maharal's basis he explains that one who takes an oath ties
   his undertaking or his truth-claim to the goals of This World that *sheva*
   represents.

Rabbi Yitzchok Avigdor Lubchansky of Milwaukee notes that the mitzvah
to swear in Hashem's name, as against the name of any other deity (Devarim
6:13, 10:20), is listed as the seventh positive commandment in Rambam's
reckoning of the 613, which illustrates the tie again between Hashem, swearing
and the number seven.

Tishrei is called the seventh month, and the Midrash (*Vayikra Rabbah* 29:9)
ties that ordinal to *shevuah*, since it was in that month that Hashem swore to
Avraham at the Akeidah.

**Is sheva always the number?**

- Rasag's *Tafsir* renders Lemech's "sevenfold has Kayin been avenged, and Lemech
  seventy-seven" (Bereishis 4:24) as "much... more and more," and *sheva yipol
  tzaddik vakam* (Mishlei 24:16) as "many"; Ibn Ezra and Ralbag read Mishlei the
  same way.
- Mecklenburg likewise intimates that the sevenfold punishments of Vayikra
  26:18, 26:24, 26:28 are recurring punishment rather than a count.

## Distinctions

### sheva (שבע) and sheviis (שביעית)
*Sheviis* is simply the ordinal, the seventh year of a seven-year cycle, and it
is Biblical for the Sabbatical year (Shemos 23:11; Vayikra 25:4, 25:20; Devarim
15:12; Nechemiah 10:32) as well as the Mishnah's standing term and the name of
the masechta.

### shavua (שבוע)
In Tanach usually a week of seven days (Bereishis 29:27-28; Vayikra 12:5;
Devarim 16:9), and so in the Mishnah (*Bava Metzia* 9:11, *Erachin* 2:1, and
throughout *Negaim*). But every time it appears in Daniel it seemingly means a
period of seven years (9:24-27,
10:2-3), and so in several mishnayos (*Sheviis* 4:7-9, *Bava Metzia*
9:10, *Sanhedrin* 5:1, *Makkos* 1:10, *Parah* 8:9), as Rashi repeatedly points
out (*Pesachim* 12a, 86a; *Moed Katan* 12a; *Sotah* 44a; *Bava Metzia* 110b;
*Sanhedrin* 97a). In Biblical Hebrew the plural of *shavua* as a seven-day
period is *shavuos*, and of *shavua* as a seven-year period *shavuim*; for some
reason, Rashi in his commentary to the Gemara sometimes uses *shavuim* for weeks.

### The root

- Mecklenburg traces both ש-ב-ת and ש-ב-ע to the two-letter ש-ב, whose sense is
  returning. The ayin is not one of Pappenheim's radical letters, but, Rabbi
  Klein argues, the Aramaic *shav*, seven, is *sheva* without it, which leaves
  ample room for the derivation. Hence, for Mecklenburg, a *shevuyah*, a captive,
  caught in a closed system that keeps returning to its start, and hence
  *shabbos* and *shavua* as cycles of time that restart on reaching seven,
  whether of days, weeks, years, or, as the medieval mekubalim have it,
  millennia.
- Pappenheim offers a similar explanation: one who has returned home no longer
  moves toward anything, so *shabbos* as cessation grows out of returning, and a
  captive is forced into that same stillness.

Mecklenburg holds that seven's cyclical importance is embedded in creation;
although the week answers to no astronomical cycle as day, month, and year do,
he points to the seven musical notes, and Rabbi Moshe Zuriel to the seven colors of the
rainbow, to which Rabbi Klein adds the seven planets visible to the naked eye,
the seven letters that take a *dagesh*, the seven orifices of the head (*Sefer
Yetzirah* 4:3), and perhaps the reputed seven-chambered uterus. Rabbi Klein then
qualifies this: the first examples are not foolproof and could be understood
as social constructs, since seven notes and seven colors of the rainbow may
reflect no more than a human consensus that fixed on seven, and in reality the
notes or the colors could be divided into any number, though we are not
conditioned to think of them so. The Shelah
writes that Hashem made the world in six days and rested on the seventh to
teach a man to use his seventy years (Tehillim 90:10) and waste none of them, and
the mitzvos built on seven, the seven days of *chametz*, the seven days of *sheva
berachos*, carry the same reminder.

## Rendering guidance

- *Sheviis* and *Shemittah* both name the Sabbatical year; keep whichever the
  source uses rather than standardizing, since the two words say different things
  about it (see the *shemittah* entry for the other half of this pair).
- Trap: English "week" absorbs *shavua* and *shabbos* both, and in Vayikra 23
  the same English word would then render *shabbos* in two different senses
  within four pesukim. Follow Onkelos: "the festival day" for the first pair,
  "weeks" for the second.
- Second trap: *shavua* in Daniel and in *Sanhedrin* 5:1 is seven years, not
  seven days. Check what is being counted before writing "week."
- *Shevuah* is "oath," not "vow"; *neder* is the vow (see the *neder*/*shevuah*
  entry). Where a derashah turns on *shevuah* and *sheva* together, keep the
  Hebrew, since no English pair carries it. Where *sheva* or *shivasayim* means
  "many," say "many"; writing "seven" imports an arithmetic the sources deny.
