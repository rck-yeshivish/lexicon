# Names of Shavuos: Shavuos / Atzeres / Chag HaKatzir / Yom HaBikkurim

## Bottom line

Three names are the Torah's: **Shavuos**, Festival of Weeks; **Chag HaKatzir**,
Festival of the Harvest; **Yom HaBikkurim**, Day of the First Fruits. Three more
are post-Biblical: **Yom HaChamishim**, the Fiftieth Day; **Chag HaTene**,
Festival of the Basket; and **Asartha**, the Aramaic of **Atzeres**. *Atzeres*
is the name of Chazal and the one the Mishnah and Gemara normally use, though the
Torah never applies it to this yom tov. Its sense is disputed,
**Assembly** on one reading and **Conclusion** on another.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether *Atzeres* means conclusion.**

- Rabbi Chaim Elazar Shapiro of Munkatch (1868-1937): *atzeres* means "stop," as
  Israeli stop signs read *atzor*, and here denotes "the end" of something, so
  Shavuos is the conclusion of Pesach as *Shemini Atzeres* is the conclusion of
  Succos, which is why Rabbinic literature sometimes calls Shavuos "the
  *Atzeres* of Pesach." Ramban (to Vayikra 23:36) offers a similar idea, and
  Rabbi Moshe Zacuto (1625-1697) adopts a related approach.
- The *Kedushas Levi*, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev (1740-1810), likewise
  makes both names one thought, each viewing the yom tov as completing a span of
  time: *Shavuos* completes the weeks leading up from Pesach, *Atzeres* is the
  festive finale of Pesach itself.
- Rabbi Dovid Tzvi Hoffmann (1843-1921) rejects this, arguing there is no
  evidence that *atzeres* is associated with conclusions; he and Rabbi Yaakov
  Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865) favor "gathering" or "congregating," pointing to
  Devarim 18:16, where the day the Torah was given is called *Yom HaKahal*, so
  that Chazal's name echoes a phrase the Torah had already used for that very
  day.

**Two further readings of *Atzeres*.**

- The *Kedushas Levi* also reads it from restraint: a spark of inspiration to
  love or awe of Hashem is easily lost unless it is contained in a worldly
  receptacle, and a mitzvah done straight after the inspiration serves as that
  vessel. At Sinai Yisrael had no mitzvos yet to serve so, except the command to
  refrain from touching the mountain, and by keeping that they fashioned a vessel
  in which the inspiration of Sinai could be stored forever. *Atzeres* therefore
  carries both senses at once, "stop" (refrain from the mountain) and "gather"
  (gather the inspiration into its container).
- Rabbi Yechezkel Abramsky (1886-1976) reads the name from the absence of a
  positive mitzvah: the two days the Torah calls *Atzeres*, the eighth of Succos
  (Vayikra 23:36, Bamidbar 29:35) and the seventh of Pesach (Devarim 16:8), are
  the only yamim tovim with no act commanded, only refraining from melachah,
  unlike lulav on at least the first day of Succos, matzah on the first day of
  Pesach, shofar on Rosh Hashanah and the fast of Yom Kippur. Shavuos had its own
  act, the two goats and two leavened loaves (Vayikra 23:16-20), while the Beis
  HaMikdash stood; once the First Beis HaMikdash was destroyed and that lapsed,
  Shavuos too was left with refraining alone, and in later times people began to
  call it *Atzeres*.
- The *Kedushas Levi* also writes that Shavuos is called *Atzeres* because it
  carries only the prohibition of work and no other mitzvah.

**Whether the name is warranted at all.** *Midrash Lekach Tov* (also called
*Pesikta Zutrata*), by the eleventh-century Rabbi Tuviah ben Eliezer, notes that
he examined the parshiyos of the moadim and did not once find *atzeres*
associated with Shavuos; the word does appear for other days, the eighth of
Succos and the seventh of Pesach. He nonetheless concludes that *atzeres* is a
fitting name, because Targum to Bamidbar 28:26 renders *b'shavuoseichem* as
*b'atzarteichon*, which shows that *atzeres* is somehow an Aramaic rendering of
*Shavuos*. In other places, though, the Targumim do render the word *Shavuos*
itself into Aramaic.

**Why the Torah's names fell out of use.** Rabbi Baruch Epstein (1860-1941): once
Yisrael was exiled from the Land and working it was no longer central to their
lives, *Chag HaKatzir* and *Yom HaBikkurim* fell away, those elements of the yom
tov having become impossible to fulfill. Why was *Shavuos* itself dropped, when that
name seemingly has nothing to do with agriculture as such? Some explain
that since *shavuos* means "weeks," the name may lend credence to the heretical
Sadducean view that the Omer count always begins on a Sunday and Shavuos
therefore always falls on a Sunday, after seven complete calendar weeks; Chazal
discontinued the name to avoid the appearance of supporting that view. Now that
the Sadducees are no longer a force, Rabbi Klein concludes, we can revert to the
Torah's own name.

## Distinctions

### Shavuos (שבועות)
*Chag Shavuos* at Shemos 34:22 and Devarim 16:10, *Chag HaShavuos* at Devarim
16:16 and Divrei HaYamim II 8:13. The Torah gives no calendar date, only a fixed
formula of seven weeks from Pesach; the commentators explain that at that season
many were camped out in their fields, away from the towns, and so would not know
when the Sanhedrin had declared Rosh Chodesh Sivan, while those in the field
certainly knew when Pesach fell and had only to count. *Shavua* means both a
seven-day period, plural *shavuos* in Biblical Hebrew, and a seven-year shemittah
cycle, plural *shavuim*; for some reason Rashi in his
commentary to the Gemara sometimes writes *shavuim* for weeks.

### Chag HaKatzir (קציר) and Yom HaBikkurim (ביכורים)
Shemos 23:16 names the yom tov for the harvest, which is why the people were in
the fields: Shavuos always falls when farmers are harvesting grain, wheat in
particular. Rashi to Yirmiyahu 5:24 notes that the harvest season for grain
lasted exactly seven weeks. Bamidbar 28:26 names it *Yom HaBikkurim* for the
first fruits that halachah requires be brought to the Beis HaMikdash (Devarim
26:1-11), and the Mishnah (*Bikkurim* 1:3) teaches that Shavuos is the first day
on which they may be brought.

### The three post-Biblical names
*Yom HaChamishim*, literally "the Fiftieth Day," is used by Josephus and other
Jewish-Hellenistic sources, fitting because Shavuos always falls on the fiftieth
day of Sefiras HaOmer; the English word "Pentecost" derives from it. *Chag
HaTene*, "Festival of the Basket," was apparently Philo Judaeus of Alexandria's
name for it, alluding to the baskets in which the bikkurim were brought.
*Asartha*, which Josephus records in *Antiquities of the Jews* (Book III) as
what the Jews customarily called the day, is the Aramaic of *Atzeres*, meaning a
festive gathering or a stoppage from work. The Mishnah and Gemara generally call
Shavuos *Atzeres* rather than Shavuos.

The Mishnah (*Eduyos* 7:6) calls Shavuos *chag*, as the Bartenura explains it.

## Rendering guidance

- Keep *Shavuos* untranslated; "Festival of Weeks" belongs in a gloss
  at first occurrence, and "Pentecost" nowhere.
- Where a Rabbinic text says *Atzeres* and means this yom tov, write "Atzeres"
  and gloss it rather than substituting "Shavuos"; the choice of name is usually
  the point.
- Trap: "Atzeres" also names Shemini Atzeres and the seventh day of Pesach, and
  English "Assembly" absorbs *Atzeres*, *kahal* and *asifah* alike. Where a
  passage sets *Atzeres* beside *Yom HaKahal*, keep the Hebrew for the first.
- *Chag HaKatzir* takes "Festival of the Harvest," not "of the Reaping";
  *bikkurim* takes "first fruits," transliterated where it names the mitzvah.
