# Silence: cheresh / shetikah / dom / chashah (hass)

## Bottom line

*Cheresh* is thinking silence: **holding one's peace** while turning something
over, and never merely quiet. *Shetikah* is the **quieting down** that follows
a commotion. *Dom* is **falling silent**, silence as a state that comes over a
person, and at root simply stoppage. *Chashah* is **keeping still**, an inward
stillness or a held-back answer. *Hass* alone is transitive: to **hush**
others. The trap is that English "be silent" flattens all five; where a passage
turns on which one it is (Mordechai's *hachareish tacharishi*, Esther 4:14),
the choice of word is the whole point.

## The article's question and its answer

The essay begins from Mordechai's words to Esther, "for if you shall surely be
silent at this moment, redemption and salvation will arise for the Jews from
another avenue" (Esther 4:14; see *Esther Rabbah* 8:6), and asks why he chose
cognates of *cheresh* over the other words for silence. It works through
*shetikah*, *dom*, *chashah* and *hass* first, then turns to *cheresh*.

Its answer comes in two parts. On Bedersi's and Pappenheim's reading, *cheresh*
is the silence of a man weighing what to say next, so Mordechai was telling
Esther not merely to think silently about the threat facing the Jews but to go
out and do something, stressing that there was no time left for silent
deliberation. Then, on Rav Hirsch's reading that *cheresh* is the stillness of
one who is asked to speak or act and behaves as though he never heard, the column
adds a further posit of its own, that Mordechai deliberately used the loaded
*hachareish tacharishi* to tell Esther not to ignore his call as though she were
deaf and heard nothing but silence, but to be spurred into action and say to
Achashverosh what was needed to save her nation. The column marks that second
part as its own suggestion, "we may posit."

## Where the authorities disagree

**What dom means, and what happened with Aharon.** Vayikra 10:3, *vayidom
Aharon*. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (1230-1300): *dom* is what he calls a
"natural state" of silence, and Aharon, confronted with the death of his two
eldest sons, was so overwhelmed by that painful development that he could do
nothing in reaction but stand in silence, unable even to think. Rabbi Yaakov
Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865): *dom* is deliberate silence, a person quiet because
he consciously and intentionally decides to be, so *vayidom Aharon* means Aharon
purposely disconnected himself from the matter by refusing to comment on it.
Still others explain *dom* as a general word for stoppage, as when Yehoshua
stopped the sun at Givon, *shemesh b'Givon dom* (Yehoshua 10:12); Chazal expound
that incident as the sun falling silent from singing Hashem's praises, but the
literal sense does not refer to silence at all, and since a man who is quiet has
stopped his lips, stoppage and silence are quite related. Ramban writes that
Aharon first cried and then fell silent; Mecklenburg disagrees on lexical
grounds, arguing that were that the reality the Torah would have used a cognate
of *shetikah* and not *dom*. The Bottom line keeps "fell silent," which survives
both Bedersi and Mecklenburg, and leaves the chiddush to a note.

**What chashah means.** Bedersi does not explain this word. Mecklenburg: a
reflective, introspective sort of quiet, similar to Bedersi's understanding of
*cheresh*. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866-1935): the silence of one who
holds himself back from answering another even though he has what to answer.
Wertheimer's reading is the more useful where someone is being addressed;
Mecklenburg's where he is alone.

**What cheresh means in Tanach as against Mishnaic usage.** In Mishnaic Hebrew
*cheresh* is one who can neither hear nor speak (Niddah 13b). Rabbi Pappenheim
explains that a deaf-mute is called a *cheresh* because he is the paragon of
quiet, silence surrounding him on every side: he neither breaks the silence with
his own speech nor hears anything other than silence. The column suggests there
may even be biblical precedent for that usage, at Shemos 4:11, where Hashem
answers Moshe at the burning bush, "Who put a mouth for man, or makes a person
mute (*ilem*) or deaf (*cheiresh*)…" If *cheiresh* merely means silent, how does
it differ from *ilem*? Because of that, some commentators explain that *cheiresh*
there means deaf and mute together, exactly as Chazal use the word; others that
it means deaf but not mute (Tosafos to Chagigah 2b with *Maharsha* and *Hagahos
Rashash* there, and Ibn Ezra to Shemos 4:11). Rabbi Shlomo Algazi (1610-1683)
splits it the other way: in Rabbinic Hebrew *cheiresh* is a deaf-mute, while in
Biblical Hebrew it is one who can hear but cannot talk. The column calls that
somewhat problematic, since it would leave the term meaning exactly what *ilem*
means.

## Distinctions

### cheresh (חרש)
Bedersi: an introspective silence, in which the silent party considers ideas but
does not verbally reveal those thoughts, the silence practiced by wise men and
experts; the column notes in support that in Akkadian *charash* means "wise" or
"intelligent." In related contexts a certain type of craftsman is a *charash*
(Shemos 35:35), and Targum calls Pharaoh's *chartumim* the *charshei* (to Shemos
7:22), such men being experts who silently think out how best to do what they do,
the artisan in particular tending to be quiet while he concentrates on his work;
digging into the depths of one's mind is conceptually similar to plowing
(*charishah*), which is why the two words are related in Hebrew. Pappenheim
(1740-1814) builds the whole root out of *charishah*, plowing, which prepares
land for agricultural use: from there the root expanded to any preparing or
manufacturing of tools out of wood, stone or metal (a smith is a *choresh*), from
there to anybody who deliberately ponders his actions, and finally to anybody who
is quiet. He stresses that the silence of *cheresh* still belongs to the root's
primary sense, being the contemplative silence in which one incubates his
thoughts before working out what to say: as plowing readies a field for sowing,
this silence readies a man for future speech, so *cheresh* is most fitting where
somebody is quiet while considering what to say next. *Chorshah*, a forest
(Shmuel I 23:15, Divrei HaYamim II 27:4), is another derivative, a quiet,
insulated place because of its thick foliage. Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 28:1): the
stillness of a man asked to speak or act who instead ignores the request, acting
as though he were deaf and had not heard it. In some places the word attaches
especially to evildoers (Mishlei 3:29 and Iyov 4:8, with Rashi). A cognate
appears at Shemos 14:14, "Hashem will fight for you, and you will be silent"
(*tacharishun*), meaning that Hashem will take care of the Egyptian army while
Yisrael sit silently on the sidelines contemplating how He wages war on their
behalf.

### shetikah (שתיקה)
Bedersi: the silence that comes after a commotion has been quelled. The root
appears only four times in Tanach, two of them where Yonah tells his shipmates
that throwing him overboard will make the stormy sea calm down (Yonah 1:11 to
12). Outside Tanach its cognates are the Targum's Aramaic renderings of
*cheresh*-based words (the article gives Bereishis 24:21 and Bamidbar 30:5, 3:8;
the last appears to be a slip for 30:8, the parallel pasuk about a husband).

### chashah (חשה)
The word of Koheles 3:7, *eis lachashos v'eis l'daber*, "a time to be silent and
a time to speak." See the dispute above. Mecklenburg also theorizes that
*nichush*, divination, and *choshen*, the Kohen Gadol's breastplate, derive from
this root.

### hass (הס)
Not a way of being silent but of making others so, and, the article says, its
etymology might be an onomatopoeic adaptation of the sound used to quiet others.
Bedersi does not mention it either. Pappenheim and Mecklenburg: the verb is
usually employed where one silences others so they may listen to somebody else,
or to show honor and awe to somebody else. Mecklenburg proposes that *hasket*,
"listen" (Devarim 27:9), is a portmanteau partly derived from *hass*, in the
sense of being quiet in order to hear what somebody else has to say.

## Rendering guidance

- Esther 4:14, *hachareish tacharishi*: "if you indeed hold your peace," not "if
  you are silent", which loses the point. Mordechai is telling Esther that this is
  no moment to sit and think out what to say; on Rav Hirsch's reading, that she
  must not act deaf to the call. So too Shemos 14:14, *v'atem tacharishun*, where
  Yisrael stands by watching how Hashem wages their war.
- Vayikra 10:3, *vayidom Aharon*: "and Aharon fell silent." Do not write "was
  quiet"; *dom* is a stopping, not a mood.
- Trap: "be silent" in English will absorb *cheresh*, *dom*, and *chashah* alike.
  Reserve "hold one's peace" for *cheresh*, "fall silent" for *dom*, and "keep
  still" for *chashah*. *Hass* is never "be silent" but "hush"; it takes an
  object.
