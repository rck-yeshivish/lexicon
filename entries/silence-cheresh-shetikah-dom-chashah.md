# Silence: cheresh / shetikah / dom / chashah (hass)

## Bottom line

*Cheresh* is thinking silence: **holding one's peace** while turning something
over, and never merely quiet. *Shetikah* is the **quieting down** that follows
a commotion. *Dom* is **falling silent**, silence as a state that comes over a
person, and at root simply stoppage. *Chashah* is **keeping still**, an inward
stillness or a held-back answer. *Hass* alone is transitive: to **hush**
others. The trap is that English "be silent" flattens all five; where a passage
turns on which one it is (Mordechai's *hachareish tacharishi*, Esther 4:14),
the choice of word is the whole point.

| הס | hass | hush | transitive: silencing others so they may listen or show honor (Pappenheim, Mecklenburg) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What dom means, and what happened with Aharon.** Vayikra 10:3, *vayidom
Aharon*. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini: *dom* is silence that simply settles on
a person, and Aharon was so overwhelmed that he could do nothing but stand there,
unable even to think. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: *dom* is chosen silence, and
Aharon consciously withdrew from the matter by refusing to comment. Others take
*dom* as a general word for stopping, as in *shemesh b'Givon dom* (Yehoshua
10:12); *Chazal* expound that as the sun falling silent from its song, but the
plain sense is that it stopped moving, and since being quiet is a stopping of the
lips the two senses run together. Ramban writes that Aharon first wept and then
fell silent; Mecklenburg rejects that on lexical grounds, since the Torah would
then have written a form of *shetikah*. The Bottom line keeps "fell silent",
which survives both Bedersi and Mecklenburg, and leaves the *chiddush* to a note.

**What chashah means.** Mecklenburg: a reflective, introspective quiet, close
to his reading of *cheresh*. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: the silence of a
man who has an answer ready and holds it back. Bedersi does not treat the word.
Wertheimer's reading is the more useful where someone is being addressed;
Mecklenburg's where he is alone.

**What cheresh means in Tanach as against Mishnaic usage.** In Mishnaic Hebrew
*cheresh* is one who neither hears nor speaks (Niddah 13b). On Shemos 4:11, where
*ilem* and *cheiresh* stand in one pasuk, some read *cheiresh* in that Mishnaic
sense, deaf and mute together; others as deaf but not mute (Tosafos to Chagigah
2b with *Maharsha* and *Hagahos Rashash*; Ibn Ezra to Shemos 4:11). Rabbi Shlomo
Algazi holds the reverse, which leaves *cheiresh* meaning exactly what *ilem*
means and is the weakest of the three.

## Distinctions

### cheresh (חרש)
Bedersi: the silence in which a man considers ideas without letting them out,
the silence of the wise and of experts. So a craftsman is a *charash* (Shemos
35:35), and Targum calls Pharaoh's *chartumim* the *charshei* (to Shemos 7:22),
men who work quietly while they concentrate. Pappenheim builds the whole root
from *charishah*, plowing: from breaking ground, to shaping tools of wood, stone,
or metal (a smith is a *choresh*), to weighing one's actions, and finally to
being quiet, because this silence readies a man for what he will say next just as
plowing readies a field for sowing. *Chorshah*, a wood (Shmuel I 23:15, Divrei
HaYamim II 27:4), belongs here as a place whose foliage makes it still. Rav
Hirsch (to Tehillim 28:1): the stillness of a man asked to speak or act who
behaves as though he never heard. The word also attaches to the scheming of the
wicked (Mishlei 3:29 and Iyov 4:8, with Rashi).

### shetikah (שתיקה)
Bedersi: the quiet that comes once a commotion has been quelled. The root
appears only four times in Tanach, two of them in Yonah, where throwing Yonah
overboard will make the raging sea grow calm (Yonah 1:11 to 12). Outside Tanach
its Aramaic cognates are the Targum's standard rendering of *cheresh* words
(Bereishis 24:21; Bamidbar 30:5).

### chashah (חשה)
The word of Koheles 3:7, *eis lachashos v'eis l'daber*. See the dispute above.
Mecklenburg also derives *nichush*, divination, and *choshen* from this root.

### hass (הס)
Not a way of being silent but of making others so, and plausibly an imitation of
the sound itself. Pappenheim and Mecklenburg: it is used where one silences a
crowd so that they will hear another speaker, or out of honor and awe for him.
Mecklenburg reads *hasket* (Devarim 27:9) as built in part on *hass*, being quiet
in order to hear.

## Rendering guidance

- Esther 4:14, *hachareish tacharishi*: "if you indeed hold your peace," not "if
  you are silent", which loses the point. Mordechai is telling Esther that this is
  no moment to sit and think out what to say; on Rav Hirsch's reading, that she
  must not act deaf to the call. So too Shemos 14:14, *v'atem tacharishun*, where
  Yisrael stands by watching how Hashem wages their war.
- Vayikra 10:3, *vayidom Aharon*: "and Aharon fell silent." Do not write "was
  quiet"; *dom* is a stopping, not a mood.
- Trap: "be silent" in English will absorb *cheresh*, *dom*, and *chashah* alike.
  Reserve "hold one's peace" for *cheresh*, "fall silent" for *dom*, and "keep
  still" for *chashah*. *Hass* is never "be silent" but "hush"; it takes an
  object.
