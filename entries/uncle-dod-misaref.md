# Uncle: dod / misaref

## Bottom line

*Dod* is not the English "uncle." It is specifically **father's brother**: Rashi
states flatly that Scripture never uses *dod* for a mother's brother. Render it
**paternal uncle** wherever the kinship is load-bearing, and plain **uncle**
only where nothing turns on it. *Misaref* is a *hapax legomenon* (Amos 6:10) and
is disputed: **maternal uncle** (Ibn Kuraish, Ibn Janach, Radak in *Sefer
HaShorashim*), a generic **kinsman** (Rashi, Ibn Kaspi), or not a kinship term
at all but a verb of **burning** (Targum Yonasan, Radak in his commentary). Do
not translate it confidently; give one reading and footnote the others. A
mother's brother in Tanach is normally spelled out as *achi imo*, never *dod*.

## Distinctions

### dod (דוד), dodah (דודה)
The Torah forbids marrying one's *dodah* because it exposes the nakedness of
one's *dod* (Vayikra 18:14, 20:20), and *Toras Kohanim* there fixes this on the
father's brother's wife; the mother's brother's wife is forbidden only by
Rabbinic decree (Yevamos 21a). A father's sister or mother's sister is called
just that, "his father's sister," "his mother's sister" (Vayikra 18:12-13,
20:19), not *dodah*. The one exception is Yocheved, called Amram's *dodah*
because she was his father's sister (Shemos 6:20), and the exception holds only
for the feminine *dodah*, never for *dod*.

Rashi (to Yirmiyahu 32:12) rules the point authoritatively: *dod* is always the
father's brother. Targum Onkelos (Vayikra 18:14, 20:20) renders *dod* as
*achvuhi*, which Rabbi Rafael Binyamin Posen reads as a fusion of *ach*
("brother") and *avohi* ("his father"). In Talmudic Aramaic *achvuh* is shortened
to *chaviv*, and Rashi (to Makkos 3b) explains that Rav called Rav Chiya
*chavivi* because Rav Chiya's brother was Rav's father, not because his sister
was Rav's mother (Sanhedrin 5a, Pesachim 4a), exactly because *chaviv* answers
to *dod*. Rabbi David Cohen and Rabbi Yochanan Sofer make this point.

The semantic range later widened to "beloved, companion," as throughout Shir
HaShirim. The article attributes that to the frequency of marriages to an uncle
and holds that the core sense of *dod* did not change.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *dod* to the single letter ד, "separation" or
"protrusion": a *dad* ("breast") protrudes from the body, and a *dod* protrudes
from the straight line of the family tree as a branch off to the side.

Rabbi Moshe Kunitz argues from Esther 2:15 ("Esther daughter of Avichayil, *dod*
of Mordechai") that Esther was Mordechai's cousin on his father's side, since
the Megillah chose *dod* rather than *misaref*, and adduces Yevamos 54b, which
limits the prohibition of one's *dodah* to the father's side. Rabbi Yosef Chaim
of Baghdad accepts that the sugya proves *dod* is paternal, but denies it proves
anything about *misaref*.

### misaref (מסרף)
The word occurs once: "and his *dod* and his *misarfo* shall carry him and bring
the bones out of the house" (Amos 6:10). Three readings:

1. **Maternal uncle.** Ibn Ezra (to Amos 6:10) in the name of Rabbi Yehuda Ibn
   Kuraish; Ibn Janach, *Sefer HaShorashim* s.v. ס-ר-פ; Radak, *Sefer
   HaShorashim*. On this view *dod* and *misaref* are the two kinds of uncle.
   Against it: the mother's brother appears several times in Tanach (Bereishis
   29:10, Shoftim 9:1, 9:3) and is always "his mother's brother," never
   *misaref*.
2. **Kinsman, cohort.** Rashi there reads it as a relative with no specified
   degree. Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi takes the same line and says outright that our
   grasp of Hebrew is incomplete and the exact relationship is unknown.
3. **A verb of burning.** Targum Yonasan and Radak in his commentary to Amos
   read *misarfo* as "the one who burns him," the samech standing for a sin, so
   the root is ש-ר-פ. On this reading the form is unique in Tanach.

Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit synthesizes 1 and 3: love for the mother's
brother "burns" especially strong (compare the fire imagery of Shir HaShirim
8:6-7), and Chazal say most children resemble the mother's brothers (Bava Basra
110a). Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer derives *misaref* instead from *seraf*
("tree sap," Orlah 1:7, Shabbos 26a): descendants are their parents' "sap,"
coming from within them, and *misaref* refers specifically to unwanted offspring
who drip out of their parents as casually as sap runs from a tree whether the
tree wills it or not. He links this to *sar'af*, "branch" (Yechezkel 31).

## Rendering guidance

- *Dod* in a halachic or genealogical passage: **paternal uncle**, or "his
  father's brother" where the English needs to be unambiguous. In Shir HaShirim
  it is **beloved**, and "uncle" is simply wrong there.
- *Dodah*: **aunt**, meaning the father's brother's wife, except at Shemos 6:20
  where it is the father's sister.
- *Chavivi* in the Gemara, said of a sage: **my uncle**, and the same paternal
  restriction applies.
- Trap: English "uncle" covers four relationships and will quietly absorb both
  *dod* and *misaref*. If a passage is arguing from which word the pasuk chose
  (Rabbi Kunitz on Esther, Rashi on Rav and Rav Chiya), the whole point dies the
  moment both become "uncle." Split them: "his father's brother" against "his
  mother's brother," and flag the second as disputed.
- When both stand in Amos 6:10, the safest English is "his uncle and his
  kinsman," with a note that *misarfo* is a *hapax* read variously as the
  maternal uncle or as "the one who burns him."
