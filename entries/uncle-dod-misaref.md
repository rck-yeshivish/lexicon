# Uncle: dod / misaref

## Bottom line

*Dod* is not the English "uncle," which covers four relationships. In Biblical
Hebrew it primarily means **father's brother**: Rashi asserts that Scripture
never uses *dod* for a mother's brother. In Shir HaShirim it seems to mean
**beloved**. *Misaref* is a *hapax legomenon*
(Amos 6:10) and is disputed: **maternal uncle** (Ibn Kuraish, Ibn Janach, Radak
in *Sefer HaShorashim*; so too the Karaite Yefet ben Ali), a reading Rabbi Klein
entertains ("seems to mean one's mother's brother") and then questions; a generic
**kinsman** (seemingly Rashi, with the Septuagint, the Peshitta and Ibn Kaspi); a
verb of **burning** (Targum Yonasan, Radak in his commentary); or a word from
*seraf*, sap or resin (Professor Gary Rendsburg after Yechezkel Kutscher, "to
smear with resin"; Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer, unwanted offspring). A mother's
brother in Tanach is spelled out as "his mother's brother," never *misaref*, and
never *dod*.

## Distinctions

### dod (דוד), dodah (דודה)
The Torah forbids marrying one's *dodah* because it exposes the nakedness of
one's *dod* (Vayikra 18:14, 20:20); the Torah's wording makes clear that the
Biblical prohibition is the father's brother's wife (see *Toras Kohanim* there),
while the mother's brother's wife is forbidden by Rabbinic decree (Yevamos 21a).
A father's sister or mother's sister is typically called just that, "father's
sister," "mother's sister" (Vayikra 18:12 to 13, 20:19), not *dodah*. The one
exception is Yocheved, called Amram's *dodah* because she was his father's sister
(Shemos 6:20), and the exception holds only for the feminine *dodah*, never for
*dod*.

Rashi (to Yirmiyahu 32:12) asserts that *dod* is never found in Scripture for a
mother's brother; it always means the father's brother. Targum Onkelos
(Vayikra 18:14, 20:20) renders *dod* as *achvuhi*, which Rabbi Rafael Binyamin
Posen explains as a portmanteau of *ach* ("brother") and *avohi* ("his father").
The Targumic *achvuh* was later shortened in Talmudic Aramaic to
*chaviv*/*chabib*. Rashi (to Makkos 3b) explains that Rav called Rav Chiya
*chabibi*, "my uncle," because Rav Chiya's brother was Rav's father. The Gemara
elsewhere (Sanhedrin 5a, Pesachim 4a) says that Rav Chiya's sister was Rav's
mother; Rabbi David Cohen of Gvul Yaavetz and Rabbi Yochanan Sofer, the Erloi
Rebbe, explain that Rashi nonetheless focuses on the brother because *chaviv*,
the Aramaic of *dod*, means specifically the father's brother (a mother's brother
would be *misaref*, the column adds). How both of Rav's parents could be Rav
Chiya's siblings, as Rashi to Eruvin 12b and Chullin 32a and Rashbam to Bava
Basra 41b note, the column answers: one was related to Rav Chiya through his
mother and the other through his father, so they were not related to each other
and could marry.

The semantic range of *dod* later expanded to "lover" or "companion," as the word
seems to mean throughout Shir HaShirim. The column chalks that up to the
regularity of marriages in which a woman married her uncle, and holds that the
core sense of *dod* did not change; it adds, "however, see Rabbi Zev Hoberman's
*Zeev Yitraf, Pesach* ch. 90."

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *dod*/*dodah* to the single letter ד,
"separation" or "protrusion": as a *dad* ("breast") protrudes from the body, a
*dod* protrudes from the straight stem of the family tree as a separate branch.

Rabbi Moshe Kunitz argues from Esther 2:15 ("Esther daughter of Avichayil, *dod*
of Mordechai") that Esther was Mordechai's cousin on his father's side, since the
pasuk says *dod* rather than *misaref*, and brings proof from Yevamos 54b, which
limits the prohibition of one's *dodah* to the father's side, one's father's
paternal brother's wife. Rabbi Yosef Chaim of Baghdad agrees that the sugya
proves *dod* is only the paternal uncle, but notes that it does not prove that
*misaref* is the maternal uncle. (By Biblical law one may marry the ex-wife or
widow of one's father's maternal brother, but the Rabbis forbade it, Yevamos
21a.)

### misaref (מסרף)
The word occurs once, in Amos's prophecy of destruction: "and his *dod* and his
*misarfo* shall carry him and take out the bones from the house" (Amos 6:10). As
a *hapax* it resists precise definition. The readings:

1. **Maternal uncle.** Ibn Ezra (to Amos 6:10) cites the early grammarian Rabbi
   Yehuda Ibn Kuraish: *dod* is the paternal uncle, *misaref* the maternal uncle.
   So too Ibn Janach, *Sefer HaShorashim* (entry שׂ-ר-פ), and Radak's *Sefer
   HaShorashim*; the reading was also popularized in Karaite scholarship by the
   early Karaite commentator Yefet ben Ali. On this view *dod* and *misaref* are
   the two kinds of uncle. Against it: the mother's brother appears several times
   in Tanach (Bereishis 29:10, Shoftim 9:1, 9:3) and is always "his mother's
   brother(s)," never *misaref*, which would suggest that *misaref* does not mean
   mother's brother. Moreover, Professor Gary Rendsburg wrote to Rabbi Klein that
   Ibn Kuraish and Ibn Janach may have been influenced by their native Arabic,
   which has two words for uncle, *em* (paternal) and *khal* (maternal). The column's sentence closes "and there is no reason to assume that the same
should not be true in Hebrew," which leaves unclear whether that clause concedes
the parallel or was meant as part of the objection.
2. **Relative, cohort.** Rashi (there) seems to explain *misaref* as a generic
   term for a relative, not a specific kinship. The Septuagint and the Peshitta
   translate the verse the same way, and Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi also seems to
   follow this approach, lamenting that our understanding of Hebrew is incomplete,
   so that we do not know the exact relationship *misaref* denotes.
3. **A verb of burning.** Targum Yonasan and Radak (to Amos 6:10) read *misarfo* as
   a verb of burning: though spelled with a samech, samech and sin often
   interchange, so the root is שׂ-ר-פ, to burn or incinerate. If so, this is a
   form of the root found nowhere else in Tanach (see also *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah*
   to Vayikra 21:11).
4. **From *seraf*, sap or resin.** Professor Gary Rendsburg, following Yechezkel
   Kutscher, explains *misarfo* as a verb, "to smear with resin," relating it to
   Aramaic and Hebrew *seraf*, sap or syrup. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer
   similarly derives *misaref* from *seraf*, tree sap (Orlah 1:7; see also Shabbos
   26a), though that word is spelled with a sin: descendants are one's "sap,"
   coming from one's own flesh as sap comes from within the tree, and *misaref*
   refers specifically to unwanted, wicked or wayward, offspring, who drip down
   from their parents almost involuntarily, as sap flows from a tree whether it
   wants or not. He notes that this lines up with *sar'af*, "branch" (Yechezkel
   31), the same root with an extra ayin.

Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit synthesizes readings 1 and 3: love for the
maternal uncle "burns" especially strong (see the fire imagery of love in Shir
HaShirim 8:6 to 7), alluded to in Chazal's saying that most children resemble
the mother's brothers (Bava Basra 110a).

It has been argued that English *syrup* is related to *seraf*, but the
etymologists cited by the *Oxford English Dictionary* derive *syrup*, *sorbet*
and *sherbet* from Arabic *sharba*/*sharab*, "drink." In Hebrew the root ש-ר-ב
means thirst or dryness (in Modern Hebrew a heat wave), the opposite of its
Arabic cognate. *Shoresh Yesha*, by the interchange of peh and beis, connects
*saraf* with *sharav*: dryness comes from heat, as burning does (see also Malbim
to Yeshayahu 35:7).

## Rendering guidance

- *Dod* in a halachic or genealogical passage: **paternal uncle**, or "his
  father's brother" where the English needs to be unambiguous. In Shir HaShirim
  it is **beloved**, and "uncle" is simply wrong there.
- *Dodah*: **aunt**, meaning the father's brother's wife, except at Shemos 6:20
  where it is the father's sister.
- *Chavivi* in the Gemara, said of a sage: **my uncle**, and on the reading of
  Rabbi David Cohen and Rabbi Yochanan Sofer the same paternal restriction
  applies.
- Trap: English "uncle" covers four relationships and will quietly absorb both
  *dod* and *misaref*. If a passage is arguing from which word was chosen (Rabbi
  Kunitz on Esther; Rabbi Cohen and Rabbi Sofer on Rashi's account of Rav and Rav
  Chiya), the whole point dies the moment both become "uncle." Split them: "his
  father's brother" against "his mother's brother," and flag the second as
  disputed.
- When both stand in Amos 6:10, the safest English is "his uncle and his
  kinsman," with a note that *misarfo* is a *hapax* read variously as the
  maternal uncle, a relative, "the one who burns him," or a word from *seraf*,
  sap.
