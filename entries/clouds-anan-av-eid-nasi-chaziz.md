# Clouds: anan / av / eid / nasi / chaziz

## Bottom line

*Anan* is the plain, general word for **cloud**, found close to a hundred times
in Tanach. *Av* is the **thick cloud** that darkens the sky. *Eid* is **rising
vapor** or mist. Rabbeinu Bachya takes it as water vapor, and Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim as clouds formed from water that has evaporated. *Nasi* is the **rain
cloud**; Rabbi Pappenheim takes it either as the cloud that carries water or as
one light enough to be lifted. *Chaziz* is the **thundercloud** that comes with
lightning (Rabbi Pappenheim). Chazal count these five as the words for cloud.

## Distinctions

The Yerushalmi (Taanis 3:3), *Midrash Tehillim* 135 and *Bereishis Rabbah*
12:12 assert that Hebrew has five words for clouds: *avim*, *eidim*, *ananim*,
*nesiim* and *chazizim*. Several of the five they explain by what rain does to a
market.

### anan (ענן)
The unmarked term: *anan* and its forms come close to a hundred times in Tanach,
seventeen of them in Behaaloscha, more than in any other parashah.
- Chazal: clouds are *ananim* because the rain they bring makes people *anavim*,
  humble with one another, since in a surplus people tend to deal fairly where
  scarcity makes them compete; alternatively because it makes them *aniyim*,
  poor, in the sense that uneven rainfall leaves everyone dependent on barter
  and nobody self-sufficient.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the root is the single letter ayin, denoting
  movement, the cloud being what the wind sets moving.
- *Sefer HaChochmah* (printed with *Peirush HaRokeach*) instead makes *ananim*
  the clouds that block sunlight.

### av (עב)
Shemos 19:9, Shoftim 5:4, Shmuel II 22:12.
- Chazal: from *me'avev*, because a heavy cloud darkens the sky or the land by
  blocking the sun.
- Pappenheim: the root ע-ב is "especially thick" (see Rashi to Taanis 6b), a
  cloud dense with water. (Rabbi Klein compares the English "thick clouds.")
- *Sefer HaChochmah*: thick and full of water.

For how *arafel* stands to *avim*, see Rashi to Shemos 19:9 and 20:18.

### eid (אד)
Bereishis 2:6.
- Chazal read it through the two senses of א-ד, "breaking" (Targum to Devarim
  32:35) and "festival" (Avodah Zarah 1:1): clouds break the holiday of the
  price-gouger, since rain means produce, produce means supply, and supply
  brings prices down.
- Pappenheim: the root is the single letter dalet, extraction and separation, so
  *eid* is clouds formed from water that has evaporated, drawn out of a larger
  body of water. This complements Rabbeinu Bachya, who takes *eid* as water vapor, and it is what the word plainly means in Rabbinic
  and Modern Hebrew.
- Ibn Ezra: a smoky cloud.
- *Sefer HaChochmah*: the cloud that comes with thunder.
- *Midrash Lekach Tov* (*Pesikta Zutrasa*) links *eid* to *nod*, a flask, clouds
  carrying water as a flask does; Pappenheim does not make that link, though he
  does derive *nod* from dalet by way of נ-ד, *nadud*, being separated from
  home, *nod* being the gear one takes along.

### nasi (נשיא)
Literally "prince."

- Chazal: clouds play a sort of kingmaker, since whose field gets rain decides
  who prospers; because they appear to choose the "princes," Tanach sometimes
  calls them *nesiim* (Tehillim 135:7, Mishlei 25:14).
- Pappenheim: the root is ש-א, carrying or lifting, either because these clouds
  carry rainwater from place to place, or because they are light enough to rise,
  as against heavy clouds that tend to come down like fog.

### chaziz (חזיז)
Iyov 28:26, Zecharyah 10:1.
- Chazal: from *chazon* and *chezyonos*, sight, since the flora that grow from
  the rain might be colored differently; others, because Hashem sends such
  clouds as signs in Heaven for man to read.
- Pappenheim: the root ח-ז is comprehension through sight or imagination, and
  *chazizim* are specifically the clouds that come with thunder, tied to sight
  because they flash lightning.
- *Sefer HaChochmah*: colorful clouds, at which Rabbi Klein registers open
  surprise.
- The Zohar draws the parallel between rain, a physical Divine influx, and
  nevuah, a spiritual one.

### the three beyond the five
Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino, a sixteenth-century Italian scholar, writes in *Ohel
Moed*, a lexicon of Hebrew synonyms, that there are two more than the five. He
adds *nivlei shamayim* (Iyov 38:37),
explained as clouds by Rashi, Ibn Ezra and others; Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes*) ties
the phrase to *neivel*, a jug, and Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) and Rabbi
Yehudah Ibn Balaam (*Sefer HaTzimud*) spell out why: clouds are jugs that pour
water out. *Kapayim* (Iyov 36:32, Eichah 3:41) is taken as clouds by many
commentators, Rashi among them, and
by *Midrash Chuppas Eliyahu Rabbah*, which counts six synonyms; *Metzudas Tzion*
explains that clouds are a *kippah* over one's head. Pappenheim and others
dissent, taking *kapayim* as the sky as a whole. Rabbi Yaakov Chaim Sofer points
to an eighth possible synonym: *Peirush HaRokeach* on Bereishis 49:25 reads *shadayim* there as
clouds emptying out water the way a nursing mother's breasts empty out milk.

## Rendering guidance

- Where the Midrash is being quoted, keep all five words transliterated and gloss
  each once. The passage is a list of five names; translating each as "cloud"
  deletes the very thing being counted.
- In ordinary prose: *anan* "cloud," *av* "thick cloud" (plural "thick clouds"),
  *eid* "mist" or "vapor," *nasi* "rain cloud," *chaziz* "thundercloud."
- Bereishis 2:6, *v'eid yaaleh min ha'aretz*: "a mist would rise from the earth"
  follows Rabbeinu Bachya and is the safe rendering. "Cloud" here
  would be defensible only when quoting the Yerushalmi's list.
- Trap: English "cloud" absorbs *anan*, *av*, *nasi* and *chaziz* without
  complaint. When two of them appear in one pasuk or one derashah, force the
  modifier ("thick cloud," "rain cloud"); when one stands alone in narrative,
  plain "cloud" is right.
- *Nesiim* in Mishlei 25:14 (*nesiim v'ruach v'geshem ayin*) needs "clouds and
  wind but no rain"; do not let the literal "princes" in, and do not gloss it
  "princes" in the body even though that is the word's other sense.
