# Clouds: anan / av / eid / nasi / chaziz

## Bottom line

*Anan* is the plain, general **cloud** (close to a hundred times in Tanach).
*Av* is the **thick cloud** that darkens the sky. *Eid* is the **rising vapor**
or mist that clouds are formed from. *Nasi* is the **rain cloud** that carries
and lifts water. *Chaziz* is the **thundercloud** that comes with lightning.
Chazal count these five as the words for cloud; the trap is that four of them
are not interchangeable descriptions but different states of cloud, so English
"cloud" for all five flattens a Midrash that is counting five names on purpose.

## Distinctions

The Yerushalmi (Taanis 3:3), *Midrash Tehillim* 135 and *Bereishis Rabbah*
12:12 name five words and read each as a comment on what rain does to a market.

### anan (ענן)
The unmarked term. Chazal: clouds are *ananim* because the rain they bring makes
people *anavim*, humble with one another, since a surplus makes men deal fairly
where scarcity makes them compete; alternatively because it makes them *aniyim*,
poor, in the sense that uneven rainfall leaves everyone dependent on barter and
nobody self-sufficient. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the root is the single letter
ayin, denoting movement, the cloud being what the wind sets moving. *Sefer
HaChochmah* (printed with *Peirush HaRokeach*) instead makes *ananim* the clouds
that block sunlight.

### av (עב)
Shemos 19:9, Shoftim 5:4, Shmuel II 22:12. Chazal: from *me'avev*, because a
heavy cloud darkens the sky or the land by blocking the sun. Pappenheim: the
root ע-ב is "especially thick" (see Rashi to Taanis 6b), a cloud dense with
water; the English "thick clouds" runs parallel. *Sefer HaChochmah*: thick and
full of water. For how *arafel* stands to *avim*, see Rashi to Shemos 19:9 and
20:18.

### eid (אד)
Bereishis 2:6. Chazal read it through the two senses of א-ד, "breaking" (Targum
to Devarim 32:35) and "festival" (Avodah Zarah 1:1): clouds break the holiday of
the price-gouger, since rain means produce, produce means supply, and supply
brings prices down. Pappenheim: the root is the single letter dalet, extraction
and separation, so *eid* is water drawn up out of a larger body. This fits
Rabbeinu Bachya, who takes *eid* as water vapor, and it is what the word plainly
means in Rabbinic and Modern Hebrew. Ibn Ezra: a smoky cloud. *Sefer
HaChochmah*: the cloud that comes with thunder. *Midrash Lekach Tov* (*Pesikta
Zutrasa*) links *eid* to *nod*, a flask, clouds carrying water as a flask does;
Pappenheim does not make that link, though he does derive *nod* from dalet by
way of נ-ד, *nadud*, being separated from home, *nod* being the gear one takes
along.

### nasi (נשיא)
Literally "prince." Chazal: clouds play kingmaker, since whose field gets rain
decides who prospers, so Tanach can call them *nesiim* (Tehillim 135:7, Mishlei
25:14; the article prints Tehillim 137:7, but the locus with *nesiim* is 135:7).
Pappenheim: the root is ש-א, carrying or lifting, either because these clouds
carry rainwater from place to place, or because they are light enough to rise,
as against heavy clouds that come down as fog.

### chaziz (חזיז)
Iyov 28:26, Zechariah 10:1. Chazal: from *chazon* and *chezyonos*, sight, since
the rain produces flora of many different appearances; others, because Hashem
sends such clouds as signs in Heaven for man to read. Pappenheim: the root ח-ז is
comprehension through sight or imagination, and *chazizim* are specifically the
clouds that come with thunder, tied to sight because they flash lightning.
*Sefer HaChochmah*: colorful clouds. The Zohar draws the parallel between rain,
a physical Divine influx, and nevuah, a spiritual one.

### the three beyond the five
Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (*Ohel Moed*) adds *nivlei shamayim* (Iyov 38:37),
explained as clouds by Rashi and Ibn Ezra; Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes*) ties
the phrase to *neivel*, a jug, and Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) and Rabbi
Yehudah Ibn Balaam (*Sefer HaTzimud*) spell out why: clouds are jugs that pour
water out. *Kapayim* (Iyov 36:32, Eichah 3:41) is taken as clouds by Rashi and
by *Midrash Chuppas Eliyahu Rabbah*, which counts six synonyms; *Metzudas Tzion*
explains that clouds are a *kippah* over one's head. Pappenheim and others
dissent, taking *kapayim* as the sky as a whole. Rabbi Yaakov Chaim Sofer points
to an eighth: *Peirush HaRokeach* on Bereishis 49:25 reads *shadayim* there as
clouds emptying out water the way a nursing mother's breasts empty out milk.

## Rendering guidance

- Where the Midrash is being quoted, keep all five words transliterated and gloss
  each once. The passage is a list of five names; translating each as "cloud"
  deletes the very thing being counted.
- In ordinary prose: *anan* "cloud," *av* "thick cloud" (plural "thick clouds"),
  *eid* "mist" or "vapor," *nasi* "rain cloud," *chaziz* "thundercloud."
- Bereishis 2:6, *v'eid yaaleh min ha'aretz*: "a mist would rise from the earth"
  follows Rabbeinu Bachya and Pappenheim and is the safe rendering. "Cloud" here
  would be defensible only when quoting the Yerushalmi's list.
- Trap: English "cloud" absorbs *anan*, *av*, *nasi* and *chaziz* without
  complaint. When two of them appear in one pasuk or one derashah, force the
  modifier ("thick cloud," "rain cloud"); when one stands alone in narrative,
  plain "cloud" is right.
- *Nesiim* in Mishlei 25:14 (*nesiim v'ruach v'geshem ayin*) needs "clouds and
  wind but no rain"; do not let the literal "princes" in, and do not gloss it
  "princes" in the body even though that is the word's other sense.
