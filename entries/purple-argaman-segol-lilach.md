# Purple: argaman / argavan / segol / lilach

## Bottom line

Classical Hebrew has no word for purple. *Argaman* is not a color word at all:
all commentators agree it is **wool dyed** a certain color, and the shade the
traditional sources describe is a **red**, whether crimson, orange, or a
yellowish red; none of them makes it a mixture of red with blue or green. (One
academic reading, Professor Athalya Brenner-Idan's, does
stretch the term as far as violet; see below.) The English word "purple" once
covered any deep red. *Segol* and *lilach* are Modern Hebrew flower-names
pressed into service as color words, with no standing in Tanach.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Is argaman one color or many?**

1. **One shade of red.** Rambam (*Klei HaMikdash* 8:13): *argaman* is wool dyed
   red. In his commentary to the Mishnah (*Kilayim* 9:1) he defines it with the
   Arabic *laca*, the red resin behind the English shellac and lacquer, and
   Bartenura (there) uses the same word and states outright that it was wool dyed
   red. Rabbi Avraham Maimuni (to Shemos 25:4), Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (to
   Daniel 5:7), and *Toras HaMinchah* (*Tetzaveh*) follow. Rabbi Yechiel Michel
   Epstein (*Aruch HaShulchan HaAsid*, *Klei HaMikdash* 28:12) proves Rambam's
   view from the Midrash that *argaman* resembled the gold of the *kapores*
   (*Shir HaShirim Rabbah* 3:16, *Bamidbar Rabbah* 12:4), which had a reddish
   cast (*Yoma* 45a).
2. **Several colors woven together.** Raavad, Rambam's famed interlocutor,
   disagrees: *argaman* is something made of two or three colors woven (*arug*)
   together. Rabbi Yosef Kurkis and Rabbi Yosef Karo clarify that he read the
   word as a portmanteau of א-ר-ג, weaving, as in *arigah*, with *min*, species,
   so the word reflects a panoply of colors rather than one. The Zohar (*Idra
   Rabbah* 141b, and see *Zohar Terumah* 139a) *seems* likewise to take it as a
   hue of red that includes other shades as well.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein does not treat these as flatly opposed on the color.
Even though Raavad, Rashi and the others do not explicitly identify *argaman* as
red, it does, he writes, still seem to be their understanding; they seem to hold that *argaman* takes in
multiple shades of red. Rambam's approach is echoed by the later authorities
named above.

**Which red.**

- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) first writes crimson red, then cites Rasag that
  *tola'as shani* is the crimson red, and concludes that *argaman* must be a
  different shade of red; after that he mentions those who explain *argaman* as
  *lac*.
- Several midrashim assert that *argaman* resembles fire, which points toward
  orange (*Sifrei Zuta*, *Midrash HaGadol*, and *Yalkut Midrashei Teiman* to
  Bamidbar 4:13; *Midrash Agur* ch. 14), and several Yemenite sources (*Midrash
  Chefetz*, *Meor HaAfeilah* to Shemos 25:4) make *argaman* a yellowish red
  against the strong red of *tola'as shani*.

So perhaps, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests, Radak would agree that *argaman*
was orange-colored: explaining it as red does not preclude explaining it as
orange, orange being essentially a shade of red mixed with yellow. What is clear
is that none of these sources sees *argaman* as a mixture of red with blue or
green, and that omission seems to obviate the notion that it is what we call
purple.

**Where the root sits.** The root seems to comprise five letters, א-ר-ג-מ-ן. Ibn
Ezra asserts that such four- and five-letter roots are either compound roots
fused together or loanwords borrowed from another language.
- Scholars including Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein and Dr. Chaim Tawil take the Hebrew
  *argaman* as borrowed from the Akkadian *argamannu*; the American
  archaeologist William Foxwell Albright argued that it cognates with Hittite
  and Ugaritic words meaning "tribute" or "offering," which would evoke
  *argaman* as an expensive dyed cloth often paid as tribute.
- Rashi (to Tehillim 68:28), basing himself on *Machberes Menachem*, *seems* to
  explain *argaman* from the triliteral ר-ג-ם, which usually means gathering, or
  stoning a person to death, and which is in turn related to ר-ק-ם, embroidery,
  possibly through the interchangeability of *kuf* and *gimmel*. Rashi does not
  say so himself, but Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein observes that the common
  denominator across those meanings is gathering things together, stones to kill
  or threads to make needlework, and that this perhaps suggests Rashi follows
  Raavad's panoply of shades.
- Ibn Ezra (to Mishlei 26:8) also *seems* to take *argaman* from ר-ג-ם, but
  explains that root as exalted things, so that *argaman* seemingly names an
  exalted sort of dyed fabric.
- *Ohalei Yehudah* offers a portmanteau of *oreg* (weaving) and *manah* (a
  respectable portion), *argaman* being an important garment in the ancient
  world; alternatively, and this he prefers, *argavan* comprises א-ו-ר (light)
  with ג-ו-ן (color or appearance), for the bright color it denotes. Rabbi
  Reuven Chaim Klein adds a proposal of his own, that *argavan* could be seen as
  a contraction of א-ר-ג with ג-ו-ן, the middle *gimmel* belonging to both
  etymons.
- The Midrash (*Bamidbar Rabbah* 12:4) reads *argaman* homiletically as the sun,
  which prepares, literally weaves (*oreg*), different forms of sustenance
  (*man*), or as Hashem, who weaves the world together so that each thing brings
  out its own species (*min*) and one species does not mix with another.

## Distinctions

### argaman (ארגמן) and argavan (ארגון)
Thirty-eight appearances in Tanach. *Argavan* in Divrei HaYamim II 2:6 and
*argavana* in Daniel 5:7 are alternate forms of the same word, on the
interchangeability of *mem* and *vav*, and *argavana* is also how Targum renders
*argaman*. One point holds across every view: *argaman* names dyed wool, not a
color.

Two passages make it fairly clear that *argaman* is not purple.

- Rashi (to Shir HaShirim 7:6) implies *argaman* is a color sometimes found in
  women's hair; as Professor Athalya Brenner-Idan first pointed out, taking
  *argaman* as purple there is dissatisfactory, since no natural hair is purple,
  and she supposes that in that instance *argaman* may not name a specific color
  at all but stand in for anything expensive or rare.
- And Targum Onkelos with Rashi (to Bereishis 49:11) write that *argaman*
  resembles the color of wine, which again seemingly precludes purple.

Brenner-Idan herself, it should be said, reads *argaman* as a general term taking
in shades of red that run from pink all the way to violet or dark purple; she
supports this from the Temple Scroll among the Dead Sea Scrolls, which uses the
expression *argaman adom*, "red *argaman*," implying that *argaman* on its own
can also cover shades not strictly red.

Rav Hirsch (on Terumah) also identifies *argaman* as a darker shade of red,
which Rabbi Klein understood as burgundy, standing for man, and *tolaas shani*
as a lighter red, standing for animals.

Why the conventional translation says purple at all: the Septuagint consistently
renders *argaman* as the Greek *porphyra*, the antecedent of the Latin *purpura*
and ultimately of the Old English *purpure*, from which Modern English *purple*
derives. That English word did not always mean the red-blue combination it means
now. In several languages "purple" means red, and what we call purple is
"violet"; the same was true in English until relatively recently, the *Oxford
English Dictionary* giving as an alternate definition "Formerly: of any generally
red shade; (now) of a deep, rich shade intermediate between crimson and violet."
So *argaman* translated "purple" may be a generic sort of red rather than our purple. *Bamidbar Rabbah* 12:4 calls *argaman* the most esteemed of the fabrics
of the Mishkan and Mikdash because it represents the garments of royalty, and
midrashic Hebrew uses *purpira* for royal clothing, as of Mordechai in *Pirkei
D'Rabbi Eliezer* ch. 50; various Greco-Roman historians likewise record that
Tyrian purple was a controlled commodity generally reserved to the royal family.
That the Greek word is a cognate of English *purple* does
not mean the clothes in question were the color we call purple.

### segol (סגול)
In 1894 Yechiel Michel Pines introduced *segol* as a new word for purple; it
seems to have been influenced by the English "violet," originally the name of a
purple-colored flower and later the word for the color itself. The Gemara
(*Berachos* 43b, *Shabbos* 50b) mentions a plant called *siglei*, which Rashi
(there) explains as the three-petaled violet. Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein suggests
that *siglei* derives from the Aramaic *sigla*, a cluster of grapes, probably
because the formation and color of grapes on a cluster resemble those of the
violet flower; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein would further argue that *sigla* may itself come
from *eshkol*, shin
interchanging with samech and kaf with gimmel, noting that Targum Yerushalmi
regularly renders *eshkol* as *sigla*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*)
notes that *sigla* also names the vowel *segol*, three dots in a cluster.

### lilach (לילך)
The same move again: the lilac flower's name extended to its color. The Academy
of the Hebrew Language recognizes the modern words for lavender and mauve
alongside it. All of these are Modern Hebrew color vocabulary and have no
standing in Tanach or Chazal.

## Rendering guidance

- In Chumash and in the Mishkan and Mikdash passages, keep *argaman*
  transliterated, glossed once per work as red-dyed wool, beside *techeiles* and
  *tola'as shani*.
- Trap: English "purple" will absorb *argaman* and then argue backwards, so that
  the Mishkan acquires a color no source gives it. If a translation must use
  "purple," attach the note that the older English sense was a deep red.
- Second trap: *argaman* and *tola'as shani* are both reds. Where the pasuk lists
  them together, the English must not give both the same word; on Rasag's reading
  *tola'as shani* holds the crimson, so *argaman* takes the other shade, which
  the Yemenite sources make a yellowish red.
- Never use *segol* or *lilach* to render anything in Tanach; where a modern
  Hebrew text uses them, "purple" and "lilac" are correct.
- Where a passage compares *argaman* to fire, to the gold of the *kapores*, or to
  wine, let the comparison stand in English rather than substituting a color
  name; the comparison is the source's own evidence for the shade.
