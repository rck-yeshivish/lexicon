# Altar: mizbeach / matzeivah / bamah

## Bottom line

*Mizbeach* is the **altar** proper, built of many stones (or of plated wood in
the Mishkan), square, with four horns. *Matzeivah* is the **single-stone altar**
or **pillar**, beloved in the days of the Avos and forbidden afterwards; where no sacrifice is in view it is a **pillar** or
**monument**, and Ramban denies it is an altar at all. *Bamah* is a **high place**, a **private altar**
away from the central place of worship. The Torah itself contrasts the
commanded *mizbeach* with the hated *matzeivah*.

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מזבח | mizbeach | altar | many stones; named for *zevach*/*zoveach*, sacrifice and slaughter (Radak, *Sefer HaShorashim*); square with four horns (Shemos 27:1-2) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Is a matzeivah an altar?**

1. **Yes, an altar of one stone.** Rashi: *matzeivah* means exactly what
   *mizbeach* means, except that a *matzeivah* is erected from a single stone
   while a *mizbeach* is built from several. On this reading Devarim 16:22, "you
   shall not erect for yourselves a *matzeivah*, which Hashem your God hates,"
   forbids offering on single-stone altars.
2. **No, a pillar at the entrance.** Ramban objects: if both words meant altar,
   and the Canaanites served idols on both, why does the Torah command the one
   and hate the other? He therefore takes *matzeivah* as the pillar the idolaters
   set up to mark the entrance of an idolatrous temple, a utilitarian monument
   rather than a place of offering.

On Rashi's reading the *matzeivos* of the Avos pose a difficulty: Hashem was
pleased with the one Yaakov set up at Beis El (Bereishis 28:18-22), and later
identified Himself to him as the One for whom that *matzeivah* was set up
(Bereishis 31:13). The answer is historical, not lexical: the *Sifrei* (to
Devarim 16) says Hashem originally
cherished single-stone altars in the era of the Avos, and that by the time the
Jews left Mitzrayim He abhorred them, because the Canaanites had formally adopted
them as a medium of idol worship.

**Why the Golden Altar is called a mizbeach**, though no animal was slaughtered
on it. Three views:

1. **It subdues evil.** The Zohar (*Vayakhel* 219a): the incense offered on it
   has the power to subdue destructive forces; Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk adds
   that the offerings on it effectively "slaughter" those forces, so the name
   from *zoveach* is exact. Aharon's incense stopping the plague (Bamidbar
   17:11-15) is the case in point.
2. **Borrowed by shape.** Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach and Rabbi Avraham Maimuni: it
   is called a *mizbeach* simply because the Inner Altar was made in the same
   form as the Outer, square and horned (Shemos 30:2), only smaller; on their
   explanation the name is a borrowed term.
3. **Blood does reach it.** Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): in rare cases, Yom
   Kippur among them, sacrificial blood is brought to that altar, so the name is
   literal.

## Distinctions

### mizbeach (מזבח)
Root ז-ב-ח. The noun *zevach* is a sacrifice and the verb *zoveach* is
sacrificial slaughtering; the *mizbeach* is where blood and the other portions of
animals, birds, and flour offerings are brought. The Mishkan's was
copper-plated *shittim* wood, the one in the Beis HaMikdash that Shlomo HaMelech
built was of stone. The Torah insists it be square, length equal to width, with
four "horns" on its corners (Shemos 27:1-2). Two items bear the name: the
*Mizbeach HaChitzon*, the Outer Altar in the courtyard, and the *Mizbeach
HaPnimi* or *Mizbeach HaZahav*, the Inner or Golden Altar for the daily incense
(see the dispute above on why the second carries the name).

### matzeivah (מצבה)
Root נ-צ-ב, as in *netziv*, something standing, a pillar. Beyond a single-stone
altar it names any commemorative monument, and in ordinary speech a tombstone.
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer explains that *nitzav* implies a prolonged or
continuous standing, and that a *matzeivah* is so called because it is erected
to stand in place for a long time.
Devarim 16:22 forbids it outright; Rashi and Ramban part company over what
exactly is forbidden (above).

### bamah (במה)
Literally a high place, hence a private altar, since these were commonly built
on higher ground; it is related to *bimah*, the raised platform from
which the Torah is read. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *bamah* to the
biliteral root ב-ם, the conglomeration and meeting point of multiple parties: a
*bamah* is a site at which many people gathered in communal worship, and it
also denotes a high place (which many commentators take as the word's original
meaning) because such gatherings typically happened at elevated places to
increase their visibility. Sacrifices outside the central place of worship are
forbidden (Devarim 12:5-18), but the Mishnah (*Zevachim* 14:6) holds that the
prohibition did not apply while the central sanctuary was wholly temporary: until
the Beis HaMikdash was built, and outside the 369 years the Mishkan stood at
Shiloh, certain offerings were permitted on a private *bamah*. Much of the First
Temple period saw Jews in both kingdoms continue to offer on *bamos*, which drew
them toward outright idolatry.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *mizbeach* "altar," *matzeivah* "pillar" or "single-stone altar,"
  *bamah* "high place" or "private altar." Plural *bamos*.
- Trap: English "altar" will swallow all three. Where a passage sets the
  commanded *mizbeach* against the hated *matzeivah* (Devarim 16:22 beside
  16:21), or against the illicit *bamos* of the kings, the English must keep
  three distinct words or the contrast is lost.
- Second trap: *matzeivah* in a Chumash context is a place of offering; in modern
  Hebrew it is a gravestone. Do not let the modern sense leak into a rendering of
  Yaakov's *matzeivah* at Beis El.
- With *bamah*, "high place" carries the literal sense and "private altar" the
  halachic one. Where the passage is about the prohibition rather than the
  topography, "private altar" is clearer.
- Where *Mizbeach HaZahav* and *Mizbeach HaChitzon* both appear, render them
  "the Golden Altar" and "the Outer Altar" rather than transliterating; the
  contrast is the point.
