# Family: mishpachah / bayis / machaneh / she'er

## Bottom line

*Mishpachah* is the ordinary word for **family**, the clan or extended kin.
*Bayis* is the **household**, the people under one roof, and sometimes
specifically one's **wife** (Ibn Janach). *Machaneh* is more precisely a
**camp** than a family, even in Vayishlach, where it names Yaakov's people, as
*bayis* does in the same parashah. *She'er* is a **blood relative** or kinsman,
from the "flesh" sense of the word, and it too can mean **wife**. *Pamalia* is
an **entourage** or retinue, human or angelic.

## Distinctions

### mishpachah (משפחה)

- Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, and Radak trace it to ש-פ-ח but split the root's sense
  in two, unconnected: "maidservant" ([*shifchah*](handmaid-amah-shifchah-eved.html#%D7%A9%D7%A4%D7%97%D7%94)) and "family/clan"
  (*mishpachah*).
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*), tracing the word instead
  to the root sin/samech-peh-ches, reads a single core sense of "attachment": a
  family is a set of people attached to one another by common descent, and a
  *shifchah* is a woman attached to her mistress's household. *HaKtav
  V'HaKabbalah* (to Bereishis 24:38) and Rav Hirsch (to Vayikra 25:5) take the
  same line.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus and Rabbi Zev Hoberman sharpen it to an *appendage*
  attached to something else, as in *sapachas*, a form of tzaraas (Vayikra
  13:2); on that reading the children are like appendages ascribed to the
  patriarch, and the *shifchah* like an adjunct to an otherwise complete
  household.

### bayis (בית)
Primarily "house," the building.

- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) explains that in "you and your *bayis* shall
  rejoice" (Devarim 14:26) the word means the members of the household, not the
  structure; so too Rashi to Bereishis 42:19 and 45:2.
- Ibn Janach narrows it further: *bayis* there is one's wife. That matches Rabbi
  Yose, who said he never called his wife *ishti* but *baisi* (Gittin 52a);
  Rashi explains that he was recognizing her as the mainstay of the house.
- Rabbi Klein adds an alternative of his own, offered as a "perhaps": that Rabbi
  Yose called her *baisi* because she modestly kept within the confines of her
  home, and that the word *bayis* may itself evoke that idea, since Rabbi
  Pappenheim traces *bayis* to the monoliteral root ב, "inside," and indeed
  *bayis* itself means "inside" (Bereishis 6:14, Shemos 25:11, 37:2, Melachim I
  7:9), like the prefix ב.

### machaneh (מחנה)
The grammarians are unanimous that the root means "dwelling" or "stopping over":
ח-נ-ה for the triliteralists (Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, Radak), ח-ן for the
biliteralists (Ibn Saruk, and Rabbi Pappenheim, who elsewhere reduces it further
to ח). Rabbi Pappenheim's core sense is the opposite of being in transit. Rav
Hirsch: Yaakov's people are a *machaneh* because they are a group settled in one
spot on the way to somewhere else. Hence the word is more accurately "camp" than "family," and in most
places in Tanach the word carries a military sense. The same root gives the
later *tachanah* (bus stop), *chanayah* (parking lot), and *chanus*
(shop/store).

### she'er (שאר)
Ibn Saruk gives three senses: remnant, food, and flesh. Radak takes the last two
to be related, seemingly finding the commonality in "meat," a staple food
that is also flesh; this is comparable to [*lechem*](bread-lechem-kikar-challah-pat.html#%D7%9C%D7%97%D7%9E), which means
food in general and sometimes meat in particular. "Relative" grows out of the
"flesh" sense (Vayikra 18:6), presumably because kin share a common fleshy
source in one common forebear, as Adam said of Chavah, "bone of my bones and
flesh of my flesh" (Bereishis 2:23); in English too we call our kin our "flesh
and blood." Where the Torah obligates a husband in *she'er* (Shemos 21:10),
Chazal (Kesubos 47b) divide over whether that is food or conjugal intimacy, both
legitimate senses of the word. *She'er* likewise narrows to one's spouse, which
Rabbi Klein suggests may be because of the sacred bond of flesh between man and
wife; that narrower sense underlies the halachos that a man inherits his
deceased wife's property (Bava Basra 109b, on Bamidbar 27:11) and that a Kohen
may become tamei for her (Yevamos 22b, on Vayikra 21:2). Rabbi Pappenheim reduces the word to the two-letter core ש-ר,
a focal point: food is one of the foundations on which life hinges, and relatives converge on
one foundational ancestor.

### pamalia (פמליא)
Retinue, court, entourage. In Chazal it is mostly Hashem's angelic host: the
*pamalia* above and the *pamalia* below in Rav Safra's tefillah (Berachos
16b-17a), where Rashi identifies the upper as the angels appointed over the
nations and the lower as the assembly of Torah scholars; Rabbi Alexandri's
statement on Torah *lishmah* making peace between them (Sanhedrin 99b); the
diminished *pamalia* above since the Churban (Chagigah 13b); Rabbi Yochanan's
teaching that Hashem does nothing without first consulting His *pamalia*
(Sanhedrin 38b); and Rabbi Yochanan again on *keshafim* read as an abbreviation
of *makchishin pamalia shel maalah* (Sanhedrin 67b, Chullin 7b). It is also used
of a human court: on the tradition that Iyov was originally an advisor to
Pharaoh (Sotah 11a), the Yerushalmi (Sotah 5:6) calls him a prominent member of
Pharaoh's *pamilia*, spelling the word with an extra yud between the mem and the
lamed.
On its origin:
- Rabbi Gedaliah Lipschutz (*Regel Yesharah* to Derech Eretz Zuta ch. 4) reads
  it as a Hebrew abbreviation, *po maliyasa*, "here is great esteem"; Rabbi
  Eliezer Herstik objects that he fails to see how this clarifies the word's
  meaning.
- Rambam (*Moreh Nevuchim* 2:6) says it is the Greek for "camp" (rendered
  *machaneh* in the standard Hebrew, [*tzava*](splendor-tzvi-tzivyon-tzava.html#%D7%A6%D7%91%D7%90) in the Kapach edition), and *Sefer
  HaAruch* defines it as a company of soldiers.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) reads it with an initial feh rather
  than peh and a chirik under the mem rather than a patach, and on that reading
  identifies the word with Latin *familia*. Alexander Kohut bolsters the
  connection by noting that the Yerushalmi spells the word with a yud between
  the mem and the lamed.

In Rabbi Klein's view this does not conflict with Rambam and the Aruch, since
"camp" and "body of troops" fall within the semantic range of Latin *familia*.

### the English comparison
The *Oxford English Dictionary* traces English *family* to Latin
*famulus*, "servant," which widened to a body of servants making up a
household, then to any entourage or retinue of troops or people, and by the
time English took it from French, to any group connected by blood or affinity;
English *familiar*, "intimate," comes from the same Latin word. The actual Latin
word for "family" was *domus*, literally "house," the ancestor of *domicile*,
*domestic*, *domain* and *domination*. The parallels run three ways: *mishpachah*
and *family* are both bound up with "servant"; *family* from a word for a body of
troops resembles *machaneh* with its military connotation; and Latin *domus* and
Hebrew *bayis* both mean "family" while their core sense is "house."

## Rendering guidance

- *Mishpachah*: "family." *Bayis* of people: "household." *Machaneh*: "camp."
  *She'er*: "kinsman," "blood relative," or in the marital contexts "wife."
- Trap: English "family" will absorb *mishpachah*, *bayis*, and *machaneh*
  alike. In Vayishlach two of them point at Yaakov's people within a few pesukim
  (*machaneh*, Bereishis 32:9 and 33:8; *bayis*, 35:2), and the words are making
  different claims: a body encamped, a household, a lineage. Keep them apart.
- Trap: *bayis* and *she'er* both narrow to "wife." Do not render either as
  "wife" unless the context or the halachah demands it; where a derashah turns
  on the narrowing, render the plain sense and let a note carry the reading.
- *Pamalia* of angels: "entourage" or "retinue," not "family" and not "household
  staff." "Court" works where the sense is a body being consulted (Sanhedrin
  38b).
- Where a source builds on the *mishpachah*/*shifchah* shared root, "attachment"
  is the word that carries both; do not translate *shifchah* as "family member."
