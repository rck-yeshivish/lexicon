# Trembling: ra'adah / ziyah / retet (ra'ash, rogez, ga'ash)

## Bottom line

*Ra'adah* is **trembling**: primarily felt within the body and not seen (Wertheimer), or
inner fear so full that it breaks out in tremors (Malbim). *Ziyah* is a
**shudder**, a quiver felt rather than seen (Pappenheim), Onkelos's word for
*vayecherad* at Sinai and Targum's standard word for *ra'ash*. *Retet* is the
**lighter** tremble (Wertheimer). *Ra'ash* is **quaking** or **tumult**,
vigorous movement of any kind, and also loud noise. *Rogez* is **agitation**,
physical in Hebrew (Shadal) and anger in Biblical Aramaic (Rashbam), though not
always anger (Bedersi). *Chareid* is **trembling** too, the word behind
*chareidim*. Yoma 4b strings several of these together and Shmuel II 22:8 puts three in one pasuk.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether ra'adah is visible.**

- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: *ra'adah* primarily refers to a shaking felt
  within the body and not discernible to an onlooker (Daniel 10:11, *omadti
  marid*), against *chil*, which he claims refers to tremors that can be seen,
  except in cases such as Tehillim 55:5, where the *chil* is explicitly in the
  heart.
- Malbim: *ra'adah* is quaking in fear specifically where inner fear grows so
  full that it becomes manifest outwardly in tremors.

**Retet with a tav and retet with a tes.** Each spelling appears once: with a
tav in Hoshea 13:1, with a tes in Yirmiyahu 49:24.

- For the tav form, Radak and Ibn Janach (each in his *Sefer HaShorashim*)
  define it as *ra'adah*, a definition that seemingly derives from the Targum,
  where *retet* is the Aramaic for *ra'adah* (Onkelos renders the *ra'ad* of
  Shemos 15:15 as *reteta*); Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) defines
  it as *chil* and *retet* with a tes. All three trace it to ר-ת-ת.
- For the tes form, Ibn Saruk (biliteral ר-ט) defines it as *chil* and *retet*
  with a tav, and Radak (triliteral ר-ט-ט) as just *retet* with a tav.
- Somewhat of an outlier, Ibn Janach defines it as pain in the joints, not
  trembling.
- Rabbi Pappenheim (biliteral ר-ט) synthesizes the two approaches: a disease in
  which nerves and muscles suddenly flex and relax many times in a row, causing
  convulsions (Rabbi Klein wonders whether he means something like Parkinson's),
  or a disease causing stomach contractions like those of a woman giving birth.

It makes sense to treat the two spellings as one word, tav and tes
interchanging, a point already made by rabbinic philologists such as Rabbi
Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*), Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (*Sefer HaGalui*), Rabbi
Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*), and others.

**The order of intensity in Shmuel II 22:8 (Tehillim 18:8).**

- Malbim, as cited in *Sefer HaCarmel*, reads the pasuk as ascending: *ga'ash* a
  subtler movement than *ra'ash*, and *ra'ash* less intense than *rogez*. In his
  *Yair Ohr* he writes that *ga'ash* is a little extra movement and *ra'ash*
  more than that, in line with the *Sefer HaCarmel* citation.
- Rabbi Reuven Landau (*Degel Machaneh Reuven* §61) writes just the opposite on
  the last two: *ra'ash* is more intense than *rogez*.

Rabbi Klein does not decide between them ("either way").

**What ziyah was at Sinai.** Rabbi Klein gives at least two ways of reading the
*ziyah* of Yoma 4b. Onkelos renders *vayecherad* as *ziyah* both when the
mountain trembled (Shemos 19:18) and when the people trembled (19:16), so
perhaps it is the mountain's shaking, or it might be the nation's overwhelming
awe. The Mechilta (*D'Rabbi Yishmael, BaChodesh* §9) explains *vayanu'u*, "and
they stirred," in Shemos 20:15 as *ziyah*, possibly supporting the second.
*Baal HaTurim* (to Shemos 20:15) sees in that stirring an allusion to the way
Jews typically sway when they learn Torah.

## Distinctions

### ra'adah (רעדה) and retet (רתת, רטט)
Root ר-ע-ד, nine appearances: *ra'ad* seizing the mighty of Moav (Shemos 15:15),
the land that quivers at Hashem's glance (Tehillim 104:32), and the *ra'adah* of
Tehillim 2:11, "rejoice in trembling," which Yoma 4b applies to the giving of
the Torah, given "with dread, with fear, with
*retet*, and with *ziyah*." (The second Luchos were given on Yom Kippur: Taanis
30b and Bava Basra 121a, on the Mishnah in Taanis 4:8.) In Modern Hebrew *re'idat adamah*, "quivering
of the land," is an earthquake.

*Retet*, for Rabbi Wertheimer, is a lighter form of shaking than *ra'adah* or
*chil*. In the Gemara *retet* (with a tav) splits: a kohen is disqualified once
he is too old, defined as when his hands and feet shake (*yirtat*), no fear in it
(Chullin 24a to 24b), while the halachic concept of *mirtat* is a person too
afraid to jeopardize his credibility or standing, with no physical shaking in it
(Avodah Zarah 32b, 40b, 61b). In Modern Hebrew a phone's vibrate mode is *retet*
or *retitah*.

### ziyah (זיע) and its family
Rabbi Pappenheim's biliteral ז-ע, movement triggered by anxiety. He compares it
to the tremor of a harp string, held tight by the instrument yet still moving so
slightly that it is felt by touch and not seen. From it: *za*, as Mordechai
neither rose nor stirred before Haman (Esther 5:9), the disturbance coming from
outside and leaving him unshaken. *Zeiyah*, sweat (Bereishis 3:19): many misquote
Yoma 4b as saying the Torah was given with *zeiyah*, an obvious error, though
Pappenheim does connect the two words; for him sweat is the body's reaction to
inner heat from exertion, anger or fear, so *zeiyah* is linked to internal
agitation, in contrast with *za*/*ziyah*, usually the effects of an
external one. *Za'avah* in the tochachah (Devarim 28:25), fulfilled, says
Yirmiyahu, for Menashe's sins (Yirmiyahu 15:4; see also Yirmiyahu 24:9, 29:18,
34:17; Yechezkel 23:46; Divrei HaYamim II 29:8): Onkelos renders it *ziyah*, which is probably what led Rashi there to
explain it as fear and shaking in fear, those who hear being so frightened that
they hope it will not happen to them; and Rashi (Vayikra 20:18, Shmuel I 20:30,
Yirmiyahu 15:4) writes that the vav of *za'avah* is not part of its root ז-ע.
*Zava'ah*, an earthquake (Berachos 54a), the trembling of the earth, now understood
as pressure from the interaction of tectonic plates; Pappenheim, following the
science of his day, explained it as air and gases trapped underground. Pappenheim also derives two words for anger
from ז-ע: *za'am* (with a final mem) and *za'af* (with the biliteral א-ף, the
face, its shaking reflecting anger). Rabbi Mordechai Kohen of Safed (*Siftei
Kohen* to Bamidbar 15:27) writes that one who sins by mistake with *avodah zarah*
brings an *eiz* (ע-ז) as a sin-offering so that it should shake (ז-ע) him into
thinking about the choices that led him there. In Rabbinic Hebrew *mizazea* is
something frightening that causes *ziyah*; Modern Hebrew uses it for something
heartrending or upsetting.

### ra'ash (רעש), ga'ash (געש), rogez (רגז), chareid (חרד)
*Chareid*: root ח-ר-ד, fifty-four times in Tanach by Even-Shoshan's concordance,
plus twice in the place-name Charadah (Bamidbar 33:24 to 25). Yitzchak, on
realizing he had blessed the wrong son, *vayecherad... charadah gedolah ad meod*
(Bereishis 27:33); the people and the mountain at Sinai (Shemos 19:16, 19:18);
Boaz shaken at midnight by the woman at his feet (Rus 3:8). Yeshayahu 66:5
addresses "those who tremble (*chareidim*) at His word," whence *chareidi* for the
community seen as especially devoted to Hashem and Torah; similar names were
given to the Quakers, a seventeenth-century English Christian group said to be
named for their trembling at meetings, and the Shakers, a later offshoot.

*Ra'ash* is absent from Chumash but appears close to fifty times after it. It
refers to shaking in fear but can also refer to any vigorous movement: the
earthquake in Uzziyah's day is simply "the *ra'ash*" (Amos 1:1, Zecharyah 14:5),
and the *b'ra'ash* of *U'Nesaneh Tokef* seemingly means a deadly earthquake.
Another meaning of Biblical *ra'ash* is loud noise, its usual Modern Hebrew sense;
Rabbi Klein links noise and movement through sound as vibrating air. Targumic
Aramaic renders *ra'ash* as a rule by *ziyah*: Targum Yonasan and *Devarim Rabbah*
11:10 explain the *kol ra'ash gadol* of Yechezkel 3:12 as *ziyah*, and that pasuk
with its Targum is in *U'va L'Tzion*. *Rogez* and *ra'ash* stand together in the
long Tachanun of Mondays and Thursdays, "from *ra'ash* and *rogez*," likely based
on Iyov 39:24, which sets them side by side.

*Ga'ash*: root ג-ע-ש, which appears nine times in Tanach (Yirmiyahu
5:22, 25:16, 46:7 to 8; Iyov 34:20; Shmuel II 22:8; Tehillim 18:8). In Modern
Hebrew *harei ga'ash*, literally tumultuous mountains, are volcanoes; *Har Ga'ash*
is a Biblical place-name, south of Yehoshua's burial place (Yehoshua 24:30,
Shoftim 2:9; see also Shmuel II 23:30, Divrei HaYamim I 11:32). Rabbi Klein has
found no source that makes the reish of *ra'ash* and the gimmel of *ga'ash*
interchangeable, though both roots end in ע-ש.

- *Rogez*: Shadal (to Bereishis 45:24 and Shemos 15:14) says it primarily means
  physical shaking, then applied to any emotional agitation, even not from fear,
  as in *lev ragaz* (Devarim 28:65), literally a trembling heart, here
  fear-induced anxiety.
- Rashbam (to Bereishis 45:24) notes that in Biblical Aramaic it denotes
  specifically anger; in rabbinic usage a *ragzan* is one prone to anger
  (Shabbos 156a, Kiddushin 40b), and in that sense we pray *b'rogez racheim
  tizkor*, from Chavakuk 3:2. Yiddish *broiges* comes from *b'rogez* and
  reflects only that one meaning.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*, on words for anger) clarifies that
  though *rogez* sometimes means angered, it is not always tied to anger, and
  sometimes means shaking out of fear.

## Rendering guidance

- Yoma 4b's list: give each term its own English. "With dread, with fear, with
  trembling and with shuddering" keeps each term apart; repeating one word loses
  the drashah. In Shmuel II 22:8, three verbs, three words, ascending per Malbim
  (Rabbi Reuven Landau reverses *ra'ash* and *rogez*).
- Trap: English "shaking" absorbs *ra'adah*, *retet*, *ra'ash* and *rogez* at
  once. Reserve "quake" for *ra'ash*, "shudder" for *ziyah*, "tremble" for
  *ra'adah* and *retet*, and let *rogez* take "agitation" by context.
- רגז in Aramaic: "anger" (Rashbam). In Hebrew, by context: "trembling" or
  "agitation" (Shadal), but "anger" where the passage calls for it, as in
  *b'rogez racheim tizkor* (Chavakuk 3:2); Bedersi warns that it is not always
  anger.
