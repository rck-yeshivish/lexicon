# Outer garment: aderes / glima / itztala / tachrich

## Bottom line

*Aderes* is the biblical word: a **mantle**, an outer garment that, Radak
explains, displays the wearer's importance. *Glima* is its Aramaic counterpart
in the Gemara, an **unfitted robe**, which Rashi explains as a flat sheet of
cloth rather than a tailored garment. *Itztala* is a **ceremonial cloak**, an
outer garment tied to office and rank. *Tachrich* is a **wrap**, and in the
plural, *tachrichin*, the burial shrouds. Rashi glosses the first three alike as
*tallis*, but *glima* and *itztala* can carry slightly different connotations:
*glima* stresses the garment's shapelessness, *itztala* the wearer's standing.

## Distinctions

### aderes (אדרת)
Eleven occurrences in Tanach. It is a furry article of clothing, rendered
variously "robe," "cloak," "cape," "gown," "coat" and "mantle." Eisav is born
"ruddy, entirely like a hairy *aderes*" (Bereishis 25:25). Achan steals a
Babylonian *aderes* from the consecrated spoil of Yericho (Yehoshua 7:21, 7:24).
It is above all Eliyahu HaNavi's garment: he hides his face in it at the
mountain (Melachim I 19:13), he is called a hairy man, which, Rabbi Klein
suggests, is probably a reference to the *aderes* (Melachim II 1:8), he splits
the Yarden with it (Melachim II 2:8), and Elisha takes up the *aderes* that fell
from him as the sign of the succession (Melachim II 2:13-14).

Rashi (Zecharyah 13:4) and Ibn Janach and Radak in their respective *Sifrei
HaShorashim* define *aderes* as a *tallis*, a shawl.

- Radak ties it to the root aleph-dalet-reish, *adir*, greatness and power: the
  *aderes* displays its wearer's importance.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim treats the opening aleph and closing tav as accretions
  and traces the word to the two-letter element dalet-reish, freedom from
  restraint, whose family he gives as *dror* (emancipation), *dardar* (a thorn
  growing wild on ownerless ground), *dirah* (a home, where a man may act as he
  wishes), *dor* (a generation, the span of one's residence in this world),
  *medurah* (a bonfire spreading unchecked), *doher* (a horse's neighing when
  let out to roam), *neder* (an obligation freely self-imposed), and *adir*
  (unrestrained power). He offers two ways to fit the *aderes* into this: either
  it hangs loosely on the body, unlike fitted clothing, or only the *adirim*
  wear it.

Targum Onkelos renders *aderes* at Bereishis 25:25 as *gelem*, cognate to
*glima*; Targum Yonasan at Yehoshua 7:21 and 7:24 renders it *itztala*.

### glima (גלימא)
Frequent in the Gemara for an outer garment, often formal. It stands in the
*kesubah*, where the husband pledges even "the *glima* on my shoulders," and
today it often names the gold coat of the Rishon LeTzion (on which garments
qualify, see Rabbi Yosef Chaim of Baghdad, *Torah Lishmah* 327). The root seems
to be the biblical gimmel-lamed-mem, which occurs three times (Melachim II 2:8;
Tehillim 139:16; Yechezkel 27:24) in the sense of wrapping or of unformed
matter, and which gives *golem*, the Mishnah's opposite of the *chacham* (*Avos*
5:7), a man not yet formed; the famous legend of the Golem is of a soulless man
with the outer trappings of a human being and no intellect from a soul.

Shabbos 77b expounds *glima* as "made like a *golem*."

- Rashi there: a garment uncut to the limbs, essentially a flat sheet covering the whole body, unfitted and therefore shapeless beside
  tailored clothing.
- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) reads it the other way: a man wrapped
  up in a *glima* is somewhat stuck in place and unable to move freely, and so
  resembles a *golem*.

Rashi's own glosses vary. Once he defines *glima* as *aderes* (Shabbos 10a),
once as *sudar* (Bava Metzia 47a), in at least one place as *tachrichin*, where
the sugya concerns a corpse's clothing (Shabbos 150b), but in the great majority
of places as *tallis* (Shabbos 110b, 138b; Eruvin 94a, 96b, 102b; Kesubos 79b;
Bava Kamma 119b; Bava Metzia 28a, 114b; Bava Basra 128a; Sanhedrin 7a; Chullin
127a).

Rabbi David Golumb (*Targumna* to Bereishis 49:26) and Rav Hirsch (Vayikra
13:59; Tehillim 139:16) suggest a connection between *glima* and *klimah*,
humiliation, through the interchange of gimmel and kaf. Rav Hirsch explains: as
a *golem* lacks shape, so the humiliated man feels stripped of his form, part of
his humanity taken from him. Rabbi Golumb notes the parallel to Chazal's reading
of *levush* as *lo bushah*, no shame.

- As for the word's outside origins, Dr. Michael Sokoloff, in his dictionary of
  Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, relates *glima* to an apparent Akkadian cognate
  *gulenu*, "coat".
- Dr. Alexander Kohut takes it as Persian, "as he often does."

### itztala (איצטלא)
An outer garment worn as a mark of status. Targum renders Achan's Babylonian
*aderes* by it, and Rav (Sanhedrin 44a) specifies that what Achan coveted was a
silk *itztala*; Rashi there (and at Bechoros 34b) glosses it *tallis*. It
appears in the Mishnah at *Gittin* 6:5 as something a husband might demand as
the condition of a *get*. In the Gemara it is nearly always a status garment: a
silk *itztala* written into a woman's *kesubah* (Yevamos 66b; Bava Metzia 17a),
and the rule that where one litigant stands in rags and the other in a costly
*itztala*, the court tells the wealthy one either to dress like his opponent or
to clothe his opponent like himself (Shevuos 31a). So too the idiom *itztala
d'rabbanan*, the cloak of the sages, standing for the honor of rabbinic
authority.

*Sefer HeAruch* notes that the word is also spelled with a samech, *istala*; so
Targum to Esther 7:6, where Esther charges Haman with wanting to wear the king's
*istala*, and Targum to Bereishis 45:22, where Yosef's gifts to his brothers are
*istalvan*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) already noted the
inconsistency. It makes more sense once one realizes that the word is a loanword
rather than native. *Itztala* comes from Latin *stola* and Greek στολή
(*stolē*), words for a garment or equipment, which *The American Heritage
Dictionary of Indo-European Roots* traces to the root *stel-*, "standing,
putting," behind English *still*, *apostle*, *epistle*, *install*, *pedestal*,
*gestalt* and more; the closest English cognate is *stole*, the long narrow
scarf-like garment often worn by Christian clergy as a sign of the duties of the
ministry, which Rabbi Klein compares to Eliyahu's *aderes*. On the spelling, a
cursory look at the *Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon Project* suggests to him that
the samech spelling belongs to Jewish Palestinian Aramaic, while Jewish
Babylonian Aramaic writes the word either way. David Curwin of the *Balashon*
blog relates in the name of the late Rabbi Dr. Seth Mandel that *tallis* derives
from the same Greek etymon as *itztala*, both borrowed from Latin *stola* and
Greek *stolē*. Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (*HaMadrich HaMaspik*) also notes the
similarity between the two words, which Rashi equates (to Sanhedrin 44a and
Bechoros 34b, above).

Rabbi Klein takes *itztala* as the word that acquired a figurative sense similar
to that of English "mantle," as in "to assume the mantle," the outer garment
standing for the duties of the role; he lists as further English parallels
*investiture* (with *vest* and *vestment*), "stepping into the shoes," and
*defrocking* (from *frock*).

### tachrich (תכריך)
Mordechai is dressed in a *tachrich* of *butz* and *argaman* (Esther 8:15). The
root kaf-reish-kaf means to wrap or surround, and gives Rabbinic *krach*, a
walled city, and Modern Hebrew *karich*, a wrap. Targum to Esther 8:15 renders
*tachrich* as *glima*, another garment one wraps around oneself. In Rabbinic
Hebrew the plural *tachrichin* is the standing term for burial shrouds.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "cloak" or "robe" will absorb *aderes*, *glima*, and *itztala* at once,
  and Rashi's *tallis* gloss makes the collapse tempting. Where a passage
  contrasts them, use "mantle" for *aderes*, "robe" for *glima*, and "cloak of
  office" or "ceremonial cloak" for *itztala*.
- *Aderes* in the Eliyahu narratives is "mantle." Rabbi Klein attaches the
  English idiom "assume the mantle" to *itztala*, not to *aderes*, though it also
  fits Elisha taking up the fallen *aderes* at Melachim II 2:13-14. "Coat" is on
  Rabbi Klein's own list of accepted renderings, so it is not wrong, only
  flatter.
- *Itztala d'rabbanan* is figurative. Render it "the mantle of the sages" or
  leave it transliterated and gloss it; "the sages' robe" reads as a literal
  garment and loses the point.
- *Tachrich* singular is "wrap" (Esther 8:15); *tachrichin* is "shrouds." Never
  carry the shroud sense back into the Esther pasuk.
- Where *glima* and *golem* appear in the same sugya (Shabbos 77b), keep the
  wordplay: "a *glima* is made like a *golem*," with a gloss on the unformed
  sense, rather than translating both away.
- *Aderes* rendered *tallis* by the *rishonim* is a definition, not a
  translation for a modern reader; writing "prayer shawl" for Eliyahu's *aderes*
  is an anachronism.
