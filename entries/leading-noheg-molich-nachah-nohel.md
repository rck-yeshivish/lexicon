# Leading: noheg / molich / nachah / nohel

## Bottom line

Four verbs, all "lead," and they are not interchangeable. *Noheg* is to **drive**:
leading by force, from strength, keeping the charges on a fixed course, and it
is the one word of the four that can be used of leading something to its ruin.
*Molich* is to **take** or **bring**, a form of *holech*, moving people from one
place or state to another. *Nachah* is to **guide**: leading that shields those
led from harm and brings them to rest. *Nohel* is to **lead with care**, the
leader who not only shields but provides and sustains. The trap is Shiras HaYam,
where *nachisa* and *neihalta* stand in one pasuk (Shemos 15:13); one English
"led" for both erases the very contrast the pasuk is built on.

## Distinctions

### noheg (נוהג)
Root נ-ה-ג by the classical lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon,
Radak), about thirty times in Tanach, three of them in Shemos (3:1, 10:13,
14:25). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, as is his way, takes it back to the biliteral
ה-ג, treating the opening nun as extraneous. He explains that two-letter root
two ways, once in *Yerios Shlomo* and once in *Chesheik Shlomo*; the column
presents only the latter, saying it finds that one easier to follow. There the
core sense is consistent action: *hogeh*/*hagus*/*higayon* is sustained thinking
(Yehoshua 1:8, Mishlei 15:28, Tehillim 19:15, 49:4), and *hegeh* is sustained
sound, crying or cooing or saying the same thing over and over (Yeshayahu 31:4,
38:14, 59:3, 59:11, Tehillim 37:30, 71:24, Mishlei 8:7). *Noheg* fits because
leading means holding those under one's influence to a consistent course, as a
shepherd does his flock (Shemos 3:1, Tehillim 78:52) and a general his army
(Divrei HaYamim I 20:1).

But the consistency is morally neutral, which is why *noheg* alone among the
four can go bad. Lavan uses it of Yaakov "leading" his daughters like captives
(Bereishis 31:26); Iyov of wicked men who drive off an orphan's donkey (Iyov
24:3); and the Torah of the Egyptian chariots that drove their riders straight
into the sea (Shemos 14:25). Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (*HaRechasim
LeVik'ah*), cited also by Rabbi Ezra Reuven Dangoor, the Chief Rabbi of Baghdad,
in his *Adei Zahav*, makes this the distinction: *nachah* and *nohel* always
carry a positive sense, since they imply an element of greatness in the one
leading, a stronger man leading weaker ones or an older leading younger and
using that greatness to help them, while *noheg* can name leading someone or
something to its downfall.

Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: *noheg* implies leading from a position of power
and strength, as when Hashem "led" the east wind before the makkah of arbeh
(Shemos 10:13). Malbim (to Shemos 10:13) reads the same pasuk as deliberate
intervention, Hashem stepping into nature to bring about what would not have
happened on its own, in contrast to the more hands-off *nachah* and *nohel*.
Malbim (to Tehillim 31:4) elsewhere frames it as *nachas*, difficult to render
but close to pleasantness and calm: *nachah* and *nohel* lead with *nachas*,
*noheg* is heavy-handed; Rabbi Yosef Shapotshnick of London (*Revid HaZahav* to
the Siddur, on Az Yashir) explains similarly. Rav Hirsch, who was
Shapira-Frankfurter's great-nephew, puts it a third way (to Shemos 13:17):
*noheg* is blind following, the led not giving it much thought, while *nachah*
implies a shared mind, the led seeing their leader as bringing them to a goal
they want.

Derivatives: *manhig* (leader), and in later Hebrew *nahag* (driver) and *hegeh*
(steering wheel). *Higayon* in the sense of logic or dialectics is a medieval
neologism, later than Biblical Hebrew; *Peirush HaMilos HaZaros* by Rabbi
Shmuel Ibn Tibbon, who translated the *Moreh Nevuchim* into Hebrew, explains it
as coined to put Greco-Arabic philosophy into Hebrew. All of these hang on
leading: a driver leads his car and passengers to their destination, the wheel
leads the car in the right direction, and logic leads a person to his
conclusions. The column adds on its own account that logic fits Pappenheim's
"consistency" too, since an argument holds only by consistent reference to one
framework. *Minhag* in Tanach is the way one rides (Melachim II 9:20); in
rabbinic usage it is a custom, a settled habit that a person, family, or
community holds to consistently, and so a rubric that "leads" a man in how to
act. And just as *noheg* has a dark side, so does *minhag*: many have decried
customs raised above their worth or plainly problematic, and pointed
homiletically to *minhag* spelled backwards yielding *gehinnom* (responsa Mahari
Mintz §66, Rabbi Yaakov Emden's *Migdal Oz*, *Beis Middos, Aliyas Vatranus*;
*Pele Yoetz* s.v. *minhag*; *Sdei Chemed* Klalim Maareches Mem §38).

### molich (מוליך)
Rabbi Wertheimer: *molich* in the sense of leading is simply an inflection of
*holech*, going or walking. It names the leader's part in making something
happen, in getting those in his charge to go from one place or situation to
another (Devarim 8:2, 8:15; Yirmiyahu 2:6; Tehillim 136:16). The root is ה-ל-ך,
whose forms appear over 1550 times in Tanach. Rabbi Pappenheim adds that leading
is not the root's own sense at all but a borrowed or ancillary one, and the same
holds for *yolech* where Hashem "led" the sea with a strong east wind through
the night (Shemos 14:21), taking charge of a force of nature for the sake of the
morning's miracle. Though the mem of *molich* is plainly no part of the root,
Shadal (to Bereishis 36:31) derives *melech*, king, from *molich*, in the sense
that a king leads his people in a given direction. The column notes in passing
that there is much to say about *melech* and its apparent synonyms, Rabbi
Shlomo of Urbino's lexicon of Hebrew synonyms *Ohel Moed* listing fifty-seven of
them in Biblical Hebrew, all naming a position of leadership or authority of one
kind or another, but leaves the subject for another time.

### nachah (נחה)
About forty times in Tanach (Shemos 13:17, 15:13; Tehillim 77:21 among them),
four of them in Shemos: Hashem did not lead them (*nacham*) by the Philistine
road (13:17), the pillar of cloud led the way (*lanchosam*, 13:21), "You led
(*nachisa*) with Your kindness" (15:13), and Moshe is told to lead the nation
(*nechei*, 32:34). Rabbi Pappenheim ties it to *menuchah* (rest),
which he derives from the biliteral נ-ח or from the single letter ח: *nachah*
is the leadership that takes itself to be responsible for keeping its charges
clear of mishap and bringing them to a state of rest. His proofs: the pillar of
cloud that led the Jews through the wilderness is *nachah* (Shemos 13:21,
Nechemiah 9:12), the Clouds of Glory being there to protect and to clear away
whatever might trip them up; and "You led (*nachisa*) with Your kindness this
nation that You redeemed" (Shemos 15:13) speaks of Hashem taking them out of a
dangerous place. Moshe is told to *nechei* the nation, that is, to instruct
them (Shemos 32:34).

Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) frames *nocheh* differently:
leading a person all the way to his final destination, not merely along a leg of
the way.

### nohel (נהל)
Rabbi Wertheimer: a more caring leading, one that supplies those led with what
they need to thrive. So Yaakov tells Esav he will lead his household on slowly
(*esnahalah*, Bereishis 33:14), the father-provider; and Yosef "led them with
bread" through the famine (*vayenahaleim*, Bereishis 47:17), the benevolent
ruler sustaining his people. Rabbi Pappenheim draws the same line against
*nachah*: *nachah* saves from the bad, *nohel* also helps in a positive,
nurturing way, which is why Shiras HaYam uses *nachah* for leading the Jews
*out of* Egypt and *nohel* for leading them *in* to the holy abode. Rabbi
Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Bereishis 47:17) gives an abbreviated
version of Rabbi Pappenheim's whole scheme. Later Hebrew gives *menahel*,
principal or manager, *hanhalah*, administration, and *mankal*, a contraction of
*menahel klali*, general manager.

Rabbi Shimon Goldblit (*Leshon Chachamim*) shifts the pieces: for him *noheg*
is the protective leading (much as Rabbi Pappenheim explains *nachah*), which is
why Tehillim 78:52 uses *vayenahageim* of Hashem bringing the Jews safely
through a desolate place, while *nohel* is the leading that also actively
advances them. On that basis he explains the yeshivos' calling their
administrator a *menahel*: he clears the obstacles and also sees to each
student's growth. Rabbi Tedeschi Ashkenazi reads *noheg* the other way about:
he insists it is leading passively, the leader standing before or behind while
his charges travel forward of their own accord, and *nohel* is the leader who
actively has a hand in taking them farther than they could have gone alone.
Malbim adds that *nohel* is used specifically of leading those already tired or
spent, who need extra encouragement and inspiration to reach what they can.

On the root: Rabbi Yosef Grayever of Ostrow (*Gelilei Chesef*) has a long
exposition on the biliteral string ה-ל, which he takes as the ultimate root of
many Hebrew words and whose core sense he gives as separating. *Nohel* on his
account is actively supervising those in one's charge, thinking how to aid them
and doing all one can to save them and separate them from whatever harm may
come, which the column notes somewhat resembles Rabbi Pappenheim and Rabbi
Goldblit in making *nohel* an active leading. Under the same string he gathers
*ohel*, a tent, which separates a private space out of a public one; *hilula*,
a wedding in Talmudic Aramaic, marriage separating a woman from every man but
her husband, though in later kabbalistic usage the word means the celebration of
a yahrzeit; *hallel* and *tehillah*, praise being a way of saying that what is
praised stands out and apart; *holelus*, foolishness, an unworthy pursuit one
ought to separate from; *hilulim* (Vayikra 19:24), the fourth-year fruits set
apart to be brought to the Beis HaMikdash; *ahal*, aloe, a fragrance that
carries farther than others and so is classed separately; *halah*, henceforth,
marking off a stretch of time or space; *halom*, here, a place set apart by its
importance; *yahal* and *halo*, illumination, since spreading light lets a
person tell one object from another; and *halmah*, breaking, smashing a thing
into separate parts. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (*Ohr Chadash* to Bereishis
33:14) takes the same two letters as "light": the leader's task is to enlighten
those in his charge as to the right path and help them follow it. Rabbi Shmuel
Yehudah Steiger (*Avnei Shayish*) and Mr. Yehoshua Yaron of Haifa (*Mevo
L'Midrash HaLashon*) see *nohel* and *noheg* both from נ-ה, with an added lamed
or gimmel, in the sense of drawing or pulling, the leader taking the initiative
and pulling his people along a path; so *vayinahu* (Shmuel I 7:2) of the Jews
letting themselves be drawn after Hashem's dictates. They take *nachah* from the
same root with a ches set between the nun and the hei, and connect to it a rare
verb form of *nahar*, to lead, which occurs four times in Tanach (Yeshayahu 2:2;
Yirmiyahu 31:12, 51:44; Michah 4:1), a river's current pulling water along a
course as a leader draws those under him along his. Later Hebrew gives *menahel*
(principal), *hanhalah* (administration), and *mancheh* (master of ceremonies)
from *nachah*.

## Rendering guidance

- Fix one English verb to each and hold it through a passage: *noheg* "drive"
  (or "lead" where "drive" is too harsh), *molich* "take" or "bring", *nachah*
  "guide", *nohel* "lead gently" or "sustain".
- The worked example is Shemos 15:13. "You guided (*nachisa*) with Your kindness
  the nation You redeemed; You led them with care (*neihalta*) in Your strength
  to Your holy abode." One "led" for both flattens the pasuk, since the first
  half is rescue from harm and the second is being brought home and provided for.
- Trap: English "lead" absorbs all four. Where a source is comparing them (a
  Malbim, a Pappenheim, a shmuess on Hashem's leadership through the midbar),
  never render two of them with the same word in the same paragraph.
- *Noheg* of a negative outcome should be "drove," not "led": the chariots that
  drove their riders into the sea (Shemos 14:25), Lavan's charge that Yaakov
  drove his daughters off like captives (Bereishis 31:26).
- *Minhag* is "custom"; keep it transliterated in a sefer. Where a source plays
  on *minhag* and *noheg* together, the English needs the link spelled out,
  since "custom" and "drive" share nothing.
