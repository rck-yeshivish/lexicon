# Camel: gamal / nakah / becher / kar

## Bottom line

These are not synonyms but stages and kinds. *Becher* (masculine) and *bichrah*
(feminine) are the **young camel**, newly born and not yet able to carry loads;
grown, it becomes *gamal* for a male and *nakah* for a female (Rabbi Nosson of
Rome, *Sefer HeAruch*). *Gamal* is the default word and can cover females too when
no distinction is being drawn; Tanach marks a she-camel by the phrase *gemalim
meinikos*, nursing camels (Bereishis 32:16). *Kar* and *kirkaros* are contested,
and not every view takes either of them as a camel.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What bichrei means in Yeshayahu 60:6.**

- Targum, Rashi, Mahari Kara, and Radak there, and Ibn Janach and Radak in their
  *Sifrei HaShorashim*, read it as ב-כ-ר: young camels, *becher* in the
  absolute.
- Rabbi Saadia Gaon (in his Tafsir) and Ibn Ezra read the beis as the
  preposition "with" or "through," leaving *kar*.
- Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*) explicitly refuses any
  connection between *bichrah* and the *kar hagamal* of Bereishis 31:34.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein leaves the dispute standing. Rabbi Ernest Klein holds
*becher* cognate with the Arabic *bakuru* and the Akkadian *bakru*, both of them
a young camel.

**What kar means.**
- Ibn Ezra himself appears to give two answers in two places. To Yeshayahu 16:1
  it is a kind of camel that runs very fast; Rabbi Yehudah Ibn Balaam links this
  to the Gemara's *gamla parcha*, the "flying camel" (Yevamos 116a, Sanhedrin
  107b). To Bereishis 31:34, where Rachel hides Lavan's terafim in the *kar
  hagamal*, it is the camel's saddle-basket. Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi
  (*HaMadrich HaMaspik*) reconciles them: the core sense is a pillow, a camel's
  saddle is called *kar* because it looks like two pillows side by side, and
  from the saddle the name passed to the animal.
- Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) makes *kar* a young camel outright.
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) cites his father Rabbi Yosef Kimchi that *kar* is
  a *keves*, a young sheep, not a camel at all.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim leaves the camel aside and ties *kar* to כ-ר, digging:
  a *kar* is a fertile field, marked out by ditches dug round it, and *karim*
  are the fattened animals that graze there.
- The Gemara (*Niddah* 31b), as Rashi reads it, uses *kar* to mean a present.

**What kirkaros are** (Yeshayahu 66:20).

- Ibn Ezra: an especially prominent kind of camel, which he links to his own
  *kar*.
- Radak (there and in *Sefer HaShorashim*): a general word for a camel or any
  swift-running animal.
- Rabbi Yosef Bechor Shor (to Bamidbar 7:3): young female calves that skip and
  jump as though dancing.

All three are live.

**Why the camel is named from ג-מ-ל.**
- Menachem Ibn Saruk simply lists three senses of the root, growing, repaying,
  and camel, without joining them.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 12:16, 21:8, 24:17) unifies them under "sufficiently
  nourished and no longer needing outside help": a weaned child (Bereishis 21:8,
  Shmuel I 1:23, Yeshayahu 28:9, Hoshea 1:8), a ripened fruit the tree has
  finished feeding (Bamidbar 17:23, Yeshayahu 18:5), a debt repaid and so
  brought to its conclusion (Tehillim 7:5, Shmuel I 24:18, Mishlei 31:12,
  Tehillim 13:6, 116:12); and he suggests that perhaps the camel too, having
  filled its hump with water and needing none for a long while, seems "weaned"
  from drinking. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds, as his own, that Rav Hirsch does
  not say so but that these explanations may support tying ג-מ-ל to ג-מ-ר,
  *gamar*, "finishing," on the lamed/reish interchange: a toddler or a fruit is
  weaned only on completing its term of growth, a debt is typically repaid only
  when the loan reaches maturity (see Bava Basra 5b), and perhaps the camel too,
  which functions in the desert once it has finished drinking.
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehudah*) offers several
  etymologies; the best, to Rabbi Klein, is a compound: *gav* (body, inside)
  plus *maleh* (full), the beast that fills itself before a long crossing. Rabbi
  Klein finds the other three less compelling: from *gamad* (a dwarf, the
  camel's opposite), from *gav* plus *mal* (he notes it is unclear whether
  *Aholei Yehudah* means "cut" or "speaking"), and from *giben* (humpbacked) on
  the interchange of mem with beis and lamed with nun. *Aholei Yehudah* also
  connects *gamal* to *gomel*, repaying, on the strength of Chazal's teaching
  that camels are modest in mating and will not couple before others (Rashi to
  Bereishis 32:16, *Bereishis Rabbah* 76:7): the camel behaves as though it
  knows Hashem repays those who act properly.
- Kli Yakar (to Bereishis 24:14): *gamal* is from the root of *gomel chessed*.

## Distinctions

### gamal (גמל)
Fifty-four occurrences as the animal, on the root ג-מ-ל, whose verbs mean
weaning, ripening, and requiting and run to another thirty-seven appearances in
Tanach by Even-Shoshan's concordance. Rabbi Yitzchak Avineri notes that the word
is always masculine in form in Biblical Hebrew and yet takes in females as well:
where the pasuk has Yaakov sending Esav she-camels it says *gemalim meinikos*
(Bereishis 32:16), nursing camels, the masculine noun qualified rather than
exchanged for a feminine one. Three men in Tanach carry the root in their
names, Gamliel (Bamidbar 1:10, 2:20, 7:54, 7:59, 10:12), Gemali (Bamidbar
13:12), and Gamul (Divrei
HaYamim I 24:17), and Gamla appears in the Mishnah (Yoma 3:9, Yevamos 6:4);
whether these are camels or one of the other senses seems an open question. Rabbi Aharon Marcus
(*Keses HaSofer* to Bereishis 10:2) connects the name Gomer to the root as well,
on the lamed/reish interchange.

*Gamal* has cognates across the Semitic languages, Aramaic *gamla*, Arabic
*jamal*, Akkadian *gammalu*, and was borrowed into non-Semitic languages too,
Greek *kamelos* and Latin *camelus*; the gimmel, whose own name seems related to
*gamal* and whose character looks much like a camel, became there a hard c or k,
which is the English "camel." The Mishnah, for its part, calls
the third Greek letter *gamla* rather than gamma (Shekalim 3:2).

### nakah (נאקה)
Twice in the Mishnah (Shabbos 5:1, Keilim 23:2), spelled נאקה or נקה depending
on the text; not in Tanach, though its apparent cognate *meinikos* is, and the
Mishnaic word is cognate with the Arabic *nakah*, likewise a female camel.
Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) notes two roots for
nursing used almost interchangeably, י-נ-ק and נ-י-ק. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
strips the yud and then the nun, arriving at the monoliteral ק, "expulsion of
what is superfluous," parent of *hakaah* (regurgitation), *kei* (vomit), and
*kaas*, a bird that throws up what it eats (Ibn Ezra to Vayikra 11:18). From ק
comes נ-ק, cleaning: *menakiyos*, the tubes of the shulchan that kept the lechem
hapanim from spoiling; *nakik*, a hollow rock as though cleaned of its minerals;
*naakah*, prayer, emptying the heart of its sorrows; *anakah*, a lizard whose
sound resembles unbroken prayer; and *yenikah* and *meineches*, emptying the
mother's milk. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that the Mishnaic *tinok* and Aramaic *yenuka*, a baby, belong here
too. On that basis he says it seems cogent to assume that *nakah* for
the female camel likewise relates to nursing, hers being the camel from which
other camels nurse.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own explanation is that both
Hebrew terms for the camel carry a tie to nursing: the female word straight from
nursing, the male word from weaning, the move away from it, which is the thing
the male camel does not do. He suggests this may arise from the animal's
physiology, camels drinking in quantity and storing the water in humps he calls
breast-like, so that the camel's physical makeup appears symbolically linked to nourishment
drawn in abundance and stored.

### becher and bichrah (בכר, בכרה)
Yirmiyahu 2:23 likens Israel to a *bichrah kalah*, a swift young she-camel
twisting her ways. Rashi and Radak there: a young she-camel, which is said to run faster and
wilder than a male or an older animal. Rashi (to Sanhedrin 52a) explains why:
she is not accustomed to heavy loads, so she feels free to act more impulsively and less
responsibly. Targum renders *becher* and *bichrah* as *hugnei* (see also
Rashi to Yeshayahu 60:6, Yirmiyahu 2:23, Sanhedrin 52a); the word occurs once in
Bavli (Sanhedrin 52a) and once in Yerushalmi (Chagigah 2:1). Dr. Michael
Sokoloff, in his dictionary of Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, defines *hugna* as a
dromedary, as against the two-humped Bactrian camel, which is not native to the
Near East. *Hugnei* is not a native Aramaic word at all but a loanword from the
Greek εὐγενής (*eugenes*), "noble," "of good stock," from *eu*, "well," and
*genos*, "race" or "stock"; the name Eugene and the term eugenics come from the
same root. The related
Talmudic *mehugan* or *hagun* carries the sense of worthy, of good stock
(Kiddushin 70a, Eruvin 100b, Nedarim 20a, Yevamos 108a, Kesubos 22a and 54a,
Bava Kamma 80a).

*Becher*/*bichrah* also surfaces where one would least expect it.

- Rabbi Ernest Klein and other etymologists derive the English "albacore," a
  kind of tuna, from the Spanish *albacora*, and that from the Arabic *al
  bakrah*, "the young camel"; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says how camels relate to
  tuna is beyond him and he would like to know.
- The Oxford English Dictionary cites this etymology and rejects it.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "camel" alone will absorb *gamal*, *nakah*, *becher*, and *bichrah*.
  Where the age or sex is doing work, write "she-camel," "young camel," "young
  she-camel."
- Bereishis 32:16 lists *gemalim meinikos* and their young against thirty male
  camels. Render the first "nursing she-camels," not "camels," or Yaakov's gift
  loses its structure.
- Yirmiyahu 2:23: *bichrah kalah* is "a swift young she-camel." "Young camel"
  drops the feminine, and with it Rashi's point about a beast never yoked.
- *Kar* in Bereishis 31:34 must be the saddle-basket, since Rachel sits on it.
  *Kar* in Yeshayahu 16:1 is disputed; do not silently harmonize the two.
- Do not press *gamal* into "dromedary" or "Bactrian." Tanach does not mark the
  distinction. Sokoloff's "dromedary" is a gloss on Targum's Aramaic *hugnei*,
  not on *gamal*, and even there *hugnei* is a Greek loanword for "of good
  stock"; keep the two apart.
