# Song: shirah / zimrah / mizmor

## Bottom line

*Shir* / *shirah* is **song**: the sung, worded song, and the general term for
song sacred or secular (Malbim). *Zemer* / *zimrah* is **music**, or
**melody**: instrumental music (Ibn Ezra, Radak, Sforno, the Vilna Gaon, Rav
Hirsch, Wertheimer), or the tune as against the words (Mecklenburg), and in
Malbim's second view the specifically religious song of Hashem's praise; when
the two stand together *shirah* comes first, *zimrah* second, because *zimrah*
is the higher (Malbim). *Mizmor* is **psalm** as a title, and in the
superscriptions *mizmor shir* / *shir mizmor* the *mizmor* is the tune and the
*shir* the words (Pappenheim and Pincus, whom the article calls two credible
authorities who beg to differ with Ibn Ezra, who holds there is no difference).
The thing not to get wrong: שירו וזמרו is "sing and play," two acts, not "sing
and sing."

The article opens by quoting the American historian Cyrus Gordon (1908-2001) to
the effect that the ancients were no less talented than we are but expressed
their intelligence differently, handling language so deftly that modern scholars
take a long time even to notice the riddles in it, let alone their subtleties;
the *shir*/*zemer* pair is offered as one such nuance.

## Distinctions

### shir / shirah (שיר, שירה)
Root ש-י-ר. The simplest way of telling the two apart, the article says, is that
*shirah* is verbal song and *zimrah* instrumental music, a reading proffered by
a bevy of authorities including Ibn Ezra (Tehillim 105:2), Radak (Divrei HaYamim
I 16:9), Sforno (Tehillim 105:2), the Vilna Gaon (cited in his son's *Be'er
Avraham* to Tehillim 27:6), Rav Hirsch (Tehillim 33:2) and Rabbi Shlomo Aharon
Wertheimer. Malbim: the more general term, taking in song both in a religious
and in a secular sense. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel (1760-1828): simply an expression
of one's happiness, without necessarily tying it back to its Source. Rabbi Yaakov
Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865): the lyrics of poetic verse without the tune. Rabbi
Yitzchak Hutner (1906-1980): praise of Hashem from a position of rapture and
attachment to Him, that is, His immanence. The Maharal of Prague (1520-1609):
*shirah* is a thing whose beginning is attached to its end, as a *sher* is a
bracelet (Yeshayahu 3:19), an animal's collar (Shabbos 5:1), or coins found
arranged in a ring (Bava Metzia 25a); song comes at the completion of a phase or
task, since happiness comes through completion, and then one gives thanks.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935-2017): only with the hindsight of completion, where
the beginning can be attached to the end, is song fitting, not beforehand; and
the whole Torah is called a *shirah* (Devarim 31:19) because Haazinu is a
microcosm of the whole Torah and so of the whole history of the world, a history
shaped as a circle, since at the end man will return to his former place of
glory as though Adam's eating from the Eitz HaDaas had never happened. The
article notes a tension here: *yashar* seems to mean the *opposite* of *shirah*,
a line that goes continuously straight against a circle whose end leads back to
its beginning. Even so, Rabbi Shapiro observes, rabbinic literature plainly
connects them, since the cows that carried the Aron back walked straight
(*vayisharnah*, Shmuel I 6:12) and Avodah Zarah 24b expounds that they *sang*.
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740-1814) connects *shir* to *yashar*, straight, in the
other direction: a song follows a straight path in holding one theme without
deviating, so its beginning and end are linked as variations on the one theme.
Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak of Siedlce (1848-1907): from *shur*, a wall, since a
song is built of many lines carefully arranged as a wall is of bricks, and pull
one out and the edifice may fall; which is also why a *sher* is a circular piece
of jewelry, a round thing having to be fully intact to be whole.

Rabbi Immanuel Frances (1618-1703): *shir* denotes the singer's power to
mesmerize his listeners and hold their attention as though he ruled over them,
which links it to words of strength, *sharir* (strongly established), *shur*
(wall) and *sherarah* (authority); and the song of Tanach often speaks of the
singer in the third person where one expects the first (Bereishis 49:2; Bamidbar
24:3; Shoftim 5:12) because true song is like an out-of-body experience, the
singer seeing himself as a separate entity. Rabbi Yaakov Haber relates that he
heard from a certain Chassidishe Rebbe, in the name of the Chasam Sofer, that
*shirah* is related to *shirayim*, leftovers, song being the leftovers of the
soul, one of the only ways it can express itself in a world dominated by
materialism.

### zemer / zimrah (זמר, זמרה)
Root ז-מ-ר, which in the verb *zomer* also means cutting down or pruning
(Vayikra 25:3; one of the 39 melachos). The bevy of authorities above:
instrumental music, hence *klei zemer* and Yiddish *klezmer*, a portmanteau of
*klei* (instruments) and *zemer* (music). Malbim: somehow a higher, more intense
form of song than *shirah*, which is why whenever the two appear in tandem
*shirah* comes first and *zemer* second; and, in what the article says is
possibly a separate explanation, the specifically religious song that speaks of
Hashem's praises. Edel, similarly: *zimrah* is always a means of acknowledging
Hashem's part in bringing the happiness and thanking Him for it. Mecklenburg: the
tune or melody, whether sung or played on an instrument. Rabbi Hutner: praise of
Hashem as an appreciation of His transcendence and how far He is from man; and
since *zimrah* is also a verb for cutting, a man who recognizes that
unapproachable transcendence is in effect cutting off his own existence, his
being paling beside Hashem's infinite greatness. Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk in
*Noam Elimelech* (to Bereishis 47:28, Shemos 19:1): *zimrah* is cutting away the
outer distractions that impede a man's avodah; and both he and the Chasam Sofer
explain Pesukei DeZimrah as cutting down the *klipos* in preparation for
complete rapture with Hashem. Pappenheim: *zomer*, cutting or pruning, is
discarding the riffraff and leaving the best, so *zimrah* is the choicest segment
of a song, as though the rest had been cut out. Analak: *zimrah* does not imply
the whole edifice but one small part of it, one segment standing independent or
cut off from the rest.

Rabbi Frances, on the other side of the pair: when Yaakov sent his sons down to
Mitzrayim for food in the famine he sent with them the *zimras* of the land of
Canaan (Bereishis 43:11), taken to mean the best of it, and Rashi connects
*zimras* to *zemer* by explaining it as the choicest produce over which people
would sing. Alternatively, Frances explains, pruning a vineyard of unnecessary
shoots is *zomer* (Vayikra 25:3), which is the art of making music, the artist
expunging every unneeded element so the song comes out wholly good. Rabbeinu
Efrayim ben Shimshon (to Bereishis 43:11) reads that *zimras ha'aretz* as fine
wine, called *zimras* because wine gladdens a man (Tehillim 104:15) and glad
people sing (*zemer*). Rabbi Nachman of Breslov (1772-1810) reads it as a special
*niggun* of Eretz Yisrael that Yaakov sent to the Egyptian ruler.

### mizmor (מזמור)
Root ז-מ-ר. Ibn Ezra (to Tehillim 48:1): there is no difference between *mizmor
shir* and *shir mizmor*. Against him the article says it found two credible
authorities who beg to differ. Pappenheim: used side by side the terms take
specific senses, *shir* the words and lyrics, *mizmor* the tune; within a given
song the words sometimes begin before the tune and sometimes the tune is played
first, so *shir mizmor* is the song whose *shir* element precedes its *mizmor*,
and *mizmor shir* the converse. Rabbi Shimshon Pincus (1944-2001, *Shabbos
Malkesa*), similarly: *shir* is the story a song tells, the words that voice a
man's happiness and thanks, and *mizmor* the tune within which the story is
told, the wordless melody for elation too intense to put logically into words;
so a perek that begins in words and rises past them is headed *shir mizmor*, and
one that begins at that intensity and only afterwards, as it subsides, lets the
words start is headed *mizmor shir*.

## Rendering guidance

- שירו לה' זמרו: "sing to Hashem, play [music]" or "sing and make music."
  Two verbs in English, in this order.
- שיר / שירה alone: "song"; שירת הים "the Song of the Sea"; the Torah as
  שירה, "a song."
- זמרה alone: "music," "melody," or "song of praise" by context (Malbim's
  second view); זמירות "hymns," "songs" (Shabbos *zemiros* stay transliterated
  in most house styles).
- מזמור as a superscription: "a psalm"; מזמור שיר "a psalm, a song"; keep the
  Hebrew order and let the reader see two nouns.
- פסוקי דזמרה: "the Verses of Song" (or keep the Hebrew); the article's gloss
  is "Verses of Hymns."
- Trap: English "song" absorbs all three, and "sing" absorbs both verbs. Where
  the passage pairs them, or a derashah turns on the order (Malbim's rule),
  give שיר "song/sing" and זמר "music/play," and never invert.
- Trap: "psalm" is fine for מזמור as a title but not for מזמור in Pincus's
  sense, the wordless melody; there write "melody."
- Where the drush is on ז-מ-ר as pruning (Hutner, Noam Elimelech, Pappenheim),
  the English cannot carry the pun; gloss it once ("*zimrah*, which also means
  to prune") rather than translating the root into the running line.
