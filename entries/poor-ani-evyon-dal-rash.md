# Poverty: ani / evyon / misken / rash / dal / dach / mach / heilech

## Bottom line

*Ani* is **poor**, the general word, a man still holding onto his dignity.
*Evyon* is **destitute**, worse off and past the shame of asking. *Dal* is
**reduced**, one who has lost what he had, not necessarily a charity case.
*Rash* is **dispossessed**, poor from the cradle or stripped of property.
*Misken* is **wretched, unfortunate**, poor and despised with it. *Dach* is
**emaciated** (physically crushed), *mach* **downtrodden** (crushed by
circumstance). *Heilech* is the **itinerant**, a door-to-door beggar or
wanderer. The Gemara seems to grade *ani* and *evyon*, and *evyon* is the
lower of the two.

## Distinctions

The Midrashim (*Midrash Tanchuma*, *Behar* §3; *Midrash Mishlei* ch. 22;
*Vayikra Rabbah* §34:6) list up to nine Hebrew words for the poor: *ani*,
*evyon*, *misken*, *rash*, *dal*, *dach*, *mach*, *ish techachim* and *heilech*.

### ani (עני)
- Bava Metzia 111b: given the choice, hire the *ani* rather than the *evyon*,
  for the *evyon* is worse off but accustomed to it and unashamed to ask for
  handouts, while the *ani* is too embarrassed to ask and so his need goes
  unmet. The Gemara thus seems to imply that *ani* and *evyon* mark degrees of
  poverty: an *ani* is poor but still has his pride and keeps up a normal front,
  while an *evyon* has given up all pretense of self-sufficiency.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (1230-1300) derives *ani* (with an ayin) from
  *inui*, affliction: strictly it is anyone afflicted by anything, though in
  nearly every context the affliction is financial.
- Rabbi Yehuda Kalatz (*Sefer HaMussar*), a late fifteenth-century scholar
  exiled from Spain to North Africa: the *ani* works hard his whole life and
  manages only a meager living.

### evyon (אביון)
Related to *ta'av*, desire: full of wants he cannot fill. Bedersi draws the
striking consequence that a rich man who wants more than he has and cannot get
it is in that respect an *evyon*, and notes that most wealthy people do want
more than they have, so that in certain ways they may aptly be called
*evyonim*, which, Rabbi Klein notes, sharpens the Mishnah "Who is rich? He who is happy with his
lot" (*Avos* 4:1). Rabbi Yehuda Kalatz agrees on the definition and adds a moral
edge: the *evyon* is lazy and brought it on himself by refusing even to try,
having no trade, no skill, no business, and not even the knack of appealing for
charity.

The gradation carries halachic weight in *matanos la'evyonim* on Purim.
- Some authorities, such as Rabbi Yair Chaim Bachrach (1639-1702) and Rabbi
  Moshe Sternbuch, hold that one should give preferentially to an *evyon* rather
  than an ordinary *ani*.
- Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein (1829-1907) and Rav Chaim Kanievsky read it the
  other way: the Megillah says the gifts may be given *even* to an *evyon*, and
  certainly to an *ani*, whose need is more pressing and otherwise less likely
  to be met precisely because he will not ask.
- Along these lines Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866-1935) reports an idea
  he saw in an unpublished commentary of Rabbi Avraham ben Yitzchak HaLevi
  Tamach (d. 1393) to *Eishes Chayil*: the doubled clause "she stretched her
  palm to an *ani* and sent forth her hands to an *evyon*" (Mishlei 31:20) gives
  one hand to the *ani* and two to the *evyon*, whose need is greater.
  Wertheimer saw that commentary only in manuscript; it was published later, by
  Dr. Leon Aryeh Feldman of Rutgers University, in a 1971 *Sefer Zikaron*
  honoring Dr. Shmuel Mirsky.

### misken (מסכן)
Related to *sakanah*, danger: the forced austerity endangers his life. Rabbi
Yehuda Kalatz: one who looks to others for support and gets mockery instead.
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: so poor and beaten down that people take him for
a madman and pay him no attention.

### rash (רש)
Tied to *yerushah*, inheritance, in two directions. One: property dispossessed
by others, so that he has lost everything. Two, as Wertheimer suggests: a poor
man son of a poor man, who never knew prosperity and inherited his poverty from
the cradle.

### dal (דל)
Literally minus or subtracted. Malbim: *dal* is anyone who lost money, even
one not poor enough to take charity, which is why *dal* never appears in a
charity context. Rabbi Yehuda Kalatz: specifically a man once rich who lost his
wealth. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814): the dalet-lamed root
primarily means drawing water from a well (hence *deli*, a pail), borrowed for
poverty because a man bereft of his fortune is like a well emptied of its water.

### dach (דך)
Crushed, sharing a root with *medochah*, a pestle. He looks smashed because want
of food has left him gaunt. Bedersi: even a rich man may be called *dach* if
illness has made him thin.

### mach (מך)
Also crushed, but abstractly rather than physically: at the mercy of others,
squashed by having to subordinate himself to them. Bedersi offers an
alternative: the *mach* need not have a low income at all, but is pressed by
obligations beyond his means. Pappenheim traces the root to the kaf element of
striking, one struck by bad *mazal*.

### ish techachim (איש תככים)
Mishlei 29:13, listed as a word for the poor in some of the midrashic lists and
not in others. Wertheimer locates the dispute in the root. If the root is the
kaf element, it means stricken, like *mach*. If the root is tav-vav-kaf, it is
from *toch*, inside or middle, and names a man of means (he has something
inside) or one of middling circumstances, not a poor man at all.

### heilech (הלך)
From "going," and it occurs once in Tanach in this sense, in Nasan's parable to
David (Shmuel II 12:4). Either the poor man who
goes door to door raising money, or the man whose assets have all gone. It can
equally mean any panhandler or itinerant peddler whatever his means, which is
perhaps why, as Rabbi Klein suggests, it appears in only some of the midrashic
lists.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "poor" will absorb *ani*, *evyon*, *dal*, *rash*, and *mach* at
  once. Reserve "poor" for *ani*, and give each of the others its own word.
- The pair עני ואביון is graded, not a doublet: "the poor and the destitute,"
  in that order. Where a source darshens the order (the Purim *matanos*
  discussion, Mishlei 31:20), a flattened "the poor and needy" erases the
  argument.
- *Matanos la'evyonim*: leave it transliterated. If it must be
  rendered, "gifts to the destitute," never "gifts to the poor," which
  contradicts the halachic discussion above.
- *Dal* against *ani*: since Malbim ties *dal* to loss rather than to
  eligibility for charity, "the reduced" or "one brought low" beats "the poor
  man" in a passage about a fall from wealth.
- *Dach* and *mach* are near-homophones with opposite emphases, physical against
  circumstantial. Where both appear, "emaciated" and "downtrodden" keep them
  apart.
- *Misken* is the only one of these with contempt built in. "Wretched" carries
  it; "poor" does not.
