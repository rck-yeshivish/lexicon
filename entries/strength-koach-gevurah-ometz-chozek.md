# Strength: koach / gevurah / chozek / ometz (otzem, oz, eitan, adir, kabir, takif)

## Bottom line

*Koach* is **strength**, the general word: the plain ability to do or withstand
something (Rabbi Pappenheim). In Tanach and Torah sources it is generally
physical strength, which man shares with animals (Rabbi Soloveitchik).
*Gevurah* is **might** or **heroism**. For Rabbi Pappenheim it is the act that
turns potential *koach* into actual strength; for Rabbi Mecklenburg, *koach*
used to defeat opposition rather than merely withstand it; for Rabbi
Soloveitchik, the heroism, given to man alone, to transcend the instinct for
survival. The Mishnah's *gibbor* is the one who conquers his yetzer (Avos 4:1).
Bedersi and Wertheimer make *chozek* the standard word for **strong** and
*ometz* strength beyond the normal measure. For Rabbi Pappenheim, *chozek* is
*koach* intensified, the strength by which one stands out above the rest. The
Vilna Gaon makes *chozek* outer, physical strength and *ometz* strength of the
heart, and *chazak v'ematz* is read four more ways besides. *Eitan* is strength
that holds back (Rabbi Edel) or that endures (Rav Hirsch); *adir*, the most
powerful of a whole group (Rabbi Pappenheim); *oz*, strength of spirit that
others cannot easily deter (Wertheimer), or for Rabbi Wessely a primarily Divine,
supernatural power; *takif*, strength that surrounds and **overpowers** (Rabbi
Pappenheim).

## Distinctions

### koach (כח)
- Rabbi Pappenheim: the most general of the words, the plain ability to do or
  withstand something.
- Rabbi J. B. Soloveitchik (1903 to 1993), in *Catharsis*: *koach* in Tanach and
  Torah sources generally means physical strength, and is not unique to man,
  since man and beast alike possess it; so the morning berachos thank Hashem
  twice, for girding Yisrael with *gevurah* and as He "who gives *koach* to the
  tired," recalling two different aspects of man's powers.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865), in a similar way to Rabbi
  Pappenheim: *koach* is the power of maintaining a given state, withstanding
  the forces that try to break it. Alternatively, *koach* is mental fortitude
  and concentration: so the Targum (to Devarim 8:18) renders *koach* as
  *eitzah*, the mental strength to think a problem through and decide on a
  course, and answering *Amen yehei shmei rabbah* "with all his *koach*"
  (Shabbos 119b) means with all one's mental focus, not screaming the words as
  loud as possible (see Rashi there, pace Tosafos).

### gevurah (גבורה)
- The Mishnah (Avos 4:1) asks who is a *gibbor* and answers, he who conquers his
  yetzer; Rabbi Klein reads this as tying *gevurah* to self-control, just like
  *eitan*.
- Rabbi Soloveitchik echoes the idea: *gevurah* is given exclusively to man, his
  ability to transcend the brute instinct of survival he shares with the
  animals, and is best translated "heroism," the courage to enter a situation
  that may not be ideal for his physical survival. Accordingly a *gibbor* is always one who enters the battle and fights with all his might, victor or not, and one who dares to keep fighting for what he believes in generally
  emerges the victor even if he loses the immediate battle.
- Rabbi Pappenheim offers a similar explication, but acknowledges that Tanach
  also borrows the word for elements of nature without free will, like water
  (e.g. Bereishis 7:18-19); for him *gevurah* is the act by which one actualizes
  one's potential *koach*, an outward show of strength.
- Rabbi Mecklenburg: where *koach* withstands what opposes it, *gevurah* is a
  more proactive use of *koach* that seeks to defeat the opposition entirely.
- Rabbi Klein adds: if *gevurah* entails holding oneself back, Hashem is the
  immaculate *Gibbor*, holding back His anger and giving the wicked time before
  punishing them (see Yoma 69b), and for this reason He is often called *Gibbor*
  (see Yirmiyahu 32:18 and the daily liturgy).
- Rabbi Shapira-Frankfurter (below) makes *gevurah* physical strength.
- Shadal (to Shemos 15:1): [*gever*](man-adam-ish-enosh-gever.html#%D7%92%D7%91%D7%A8) comes from *gavah*, since one higher and more
  exalted than another usually overpowers him.

### chozek (חזק) and ometz (אמץ)
Moshe, passing the leadership to Yehoshua, tells him *chazak v'ematz* (Devarim
31:7); Hashem says it to him at Devarim 31:23 and three times more (Yehoshua 1:6,
1:7, 1:9), and the
people say it to Yehoshua (1:18); David says it to Shlomo (Divrei HaYamim I
22:13, 28:20). The pair seems redundant, and it is read in these ways:

1. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (a 13th century Spanish sage) and Rabbi
   Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935): *chozek* is the standard word for
   strength, *ometz* an above-normal amount. A man who grew weak and was nursed
   back to a normal man's strength has *chozek*; *ometz* cannot be said of him.
2. The Vilna Gaon (to Yehoshua 1:7, Mishlei 24:5): *chozek* is outer, physical
   strength, *ometz* strength in the heart. Malbim echoes this in *Yair Ohr*,
   his work on synonyms.
3. Malbim elsewhere (to Yeshayahu 28:2) seems to take a different approach:
   *chozek* is temporary strength that atrophies with time, *ometz* a resilient
   strength that constantly recharges itself and never falters.
4. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743 to 1826), in *HaRechasim
   LeVikah* (Bereishis 25:23), departs from this model with three kinds of
   strength: *gevurah* physical strength, *ometz* strength of the heart
   (spiritual resolve), *chozek* strength of will (courage). *Chozek* is needed
   to enter a battle or any danger without fear, *ometz* to stay in the battle
   and not run away when things heat up.
5. Berachos 32b: *chazak* is strength in Torah study, *ematz* in good deeds.
   Rabbi Wertheimer fits this to his view: the greater a scholar becomes, the
   more effort he must exert to keep performing good deeds and not lose himself
   in the theoretical world of study, and that super-human fortifying of the
   will is properly *ometz*.
6. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814): *ometz* is from מ-ץ,
   sucking or squeezing (*mitz*, juice; *metzitzah*, sucking), a person
   squeezing out every last ounce of energy. Red horses are [*amutzim*](red-adom-shani-chamutz.html#%D7%90%D7%9E%D7%95%D7%A6)
   (Zecharyah 6:3) because their exertion brings their blood to the surface of
   the skin, as if squeezed out, coloring even their hair red.

Rabbi Pappenheim also sets *chozek* against *tokef*: *chozek* is the strength to
withstand being overpowered by another, *tokef* the strength actually to
overpower others. He also makes *chozek* extra-strength *koach*, a non-standard
ability by which one person stands out above the rest, and calling someone
*chazak* means he is more powerful than someone else (see *adir*, below).

### otzem (עצם) and oz (עז)
- Rabbi Dr. Asher Weiser (editor of the Mossad HaRav Kook edition of Ibn Ezra on
  the Torah): *ometz* is related to *otzem*, the aleph interchanging with the
  ayin and the other two consonants switching places by metathesis.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel (1760 to 1828): *otzem*/*etzem* is growth in added
  quantity (see Shemos 1:20; Devarim 7:1; Yoel 2:2; Tehillim 35:18), strength
  through numbers, and from there the word expanded to inherent strength, quality added to quantity. Alternatively, it is borrowed from *etzem*,
  bone, the strongest part of the body.

- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935 to 2017) finds a common theme in all words with ע-ץ:
  *eitz*, a tree, contains within itself everything that will come from it;
  *etzem*, self, the sum of one's potential; *atzuv*, sad, one who retreats into
  himself and does not expand outward; *atzel*, lazy, who keeps his potential
  bottled up; *otzer*, stop, holding something back from expanding; *eitzah*,
  advice, which an honest advisor can give only as far as he grasps the *etzem*
  of the matter. So *otzem* is potential strength pent up within a person and
  not yet outwardly expressed. (Rabbi Pappenheim offers a similar exposition of
  ע-ץ.)
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916): a tree is *eitz* for its hard, strong
  trunk, and *atzuv* is related to *eitz* as wood, a dry state bereft of life
  and happiness; in a fascinating twist he connects *otzem* and *eitz* to *oz*,
  all meaning strong or hard, by the interchange of tzadi and zayin.

Rabbi Wertheimer compares *oz*/*izuz* to *az*, strong or bold: ע-ז is strength of
spirit, a resolve others cannot easily dissuade or deter. A person's *oz* is so
palpable that it shows in his face (see Koheles 8:1), and so it is poetically
called a garment he wears (see Mishlei 31:25). Moshe
told the spies to see whether the Canaanites were *chazak* or weak (Bamidbar
13:18), and they reported them *az* (13:28): charged with gauging physical
strength, they examined the Canaanites' psychological resolve instead, and found
a determination that could be seen on their faces.

Rabbi Pappenheim traces ע-ז to something stable, unchanging, unwavering. Its
derivatives include *azus*, brazenness, being unwilling to compromise, and *oz*,
the strength that lets something withstand all opposition; so too goats are
*izzim* because their unbendable legs give them stable footing to jump and climb
with ease.

Rabbi Naftali Hertz Wessely (Weisel, 1725 to 1805): the primary meaning of *oz*
always refers to Divine, supernatural power, in contrast to *gevurah*, which can
also refer to power within the normal course of nature. Rabbi Edel cites him and
adds that this is the sense of "Hashem gives *oz* to His nation" (Tehillim
29:11): He lifts Yisrael above the rules of nature. On this
view *oz* means anything strong only in a borrowed sense. "Give *oz* to God"
(Tehillim 68:35) cannot literally mean that a mortal strengthens Hashem; when
Yisrael follow His will He showers them with abundance, as if they had given
Him more strength (see Eichah Rabbah 1:33). Rabbi Yerucham Levovitz (1873 to
1936) draws a lesson from this: when you do someone a favor, let him do
something for you in return so that he does not feel forever indebted; so
Hashem, who does us the ultimate favor of giving and sustaining our life, lets
us feel we can do something for Him in return by keeping the Torah and giving
Him *oz*.

### eitan (איתן)
Melachim I 8:2 calls Tishrei the month of the *Eitanim*. The Gemara (Rosh Hashanah 11a) explains that the Avos,
called *eitanim*, "powerful ones," were born in it, which leads the Gemara
elsewhere to identify Eitan HaEzrachi (Tehillim 89:1) with Avraham, the
"powerful" one who stemmed the tide of polytheism (see Maharsha to Bava Basra
15a). Rabbi Klein holds that five words for power, *eitan*, *koach*, *gevurah*,
*adir* and *kabir*, are not true synonyms.

- *Nachal eitan* (Devarim 21:4): for Rashi [*nachal*](river-nahar-yeor-nachal.html#%D7%A0%D7%97%D7%9C) is a valley or wadi and
  *eitan* strong or hard, a rocky place; for Rambam (Hilchos Rotzeach 9:2)
  *nachal* is a river and *eitan* strong-flowing.
- Rabbi Edel, without referring to either, reconciles their views: *eitan* is
  literally the river bank, which seems to hold the waters back from passing its
  threshold, and that restraint takes great strength, so *eitan* came to mean
  strength and power. *Eitan* describes Yosef's unshakeable righteousness
  (Bereishis 49:24) and the rocky habitat of Yisro's descendants (Bamidbar
  24:21), who, Rabbi Klein notes, like Avraham showed strong discipline and
  self-control.
- Rav Samson Raphael Hirsch (to Shemos 14:27) adds that *eitan* is something
  strong and durable that has lasted a long time, like bedrock.

On Tishrei, the Gemara there adds that even if the Avos were not born in it, the
month is "powerful" for being blessed with more timely mitzvos than other
months; Metzudas David (to Melachim I 8:2): it is a powerful month for its many
holidays that serve self-perfection. Rabbi Klein's lesson: Tishrei can make a
person a firm, strongly anchored stalwart, as hard to budge as rock.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Vayikra 23:24): Tishrei is *yerach
ha'eisanim*, as in *vayashav hayam lifnos boker le'eisano* (Shemos 14:27),
because in it we begin to return through [*teshuvah*](return-shuv-chazarah.html#%D7%AA%D7%A9%D7%95%D7%91%D7%94) to our pristine, unblemished
selves; and the Avos are called *eisanim* because they reached that state.

Rabbi Pappenheim admits he is unsure how to break the word down.

- In *Yerios Shlomo* he takes the root as ת-ן, "give": *eitan* is a
  concentration of strength that can only be a Divine gift, which no one could
  mistake for something natural.
- Shadal (to Shemos 14:27): *eisan* is related to *tan*, giving, something so
  strong it gives without tiring; of a river, as the sea at Shemos 14:27 or
  *nachal eisan*, it is a flow that never fails, and from there it was borrowed
  for anything especially strong.
- In *Cheshek Shlomo* he connects it to א-ת, reduced further to ת, "connection"
  (*es* connects a verb to its object and sometimes means "with"; *os*, a sign,
  connects the sign and the signified): *eitan* is power concentrated by the
  extreme compression of many connected components, as in rock. He also makes
  *eitan* inherent, qualitative strength, strong in itself even if compressed
  from smaller parts, as against *kabir*.

### kabir (כביר), adir (אדיר), takif (תקיף)
*Kabir* (Rabbi Pappenheim): quantitative strength, strong for being made of many
units banded together (see Rashi to Berachos 8a, who associates *kabir* with the
power of communal prayer). He then divides quantitative strength: *otzem* where
the sub-units do not necessarily contribute an equal force, *kabir* where the
components are equally powerful. The *Pri Toar* (Yoreh De'ah 19:10) relates
*achbar*, the mouse, to *kabir*.

- *Adir*: Rabbi Pappenheim, calling someone *chazak* means he is more powerful
  than someone else, *adir* that he is the most powerful of a whole group. It is
  thus quite apt that Hashem is described as *adir* (see Shemos 15:6; Tehillim
  8:2, 93:4). Rabbi Pappenheim traces *adir* to ד-ר, freedom of motion without
  limitation or constriction. Hashem is certainly free of subjection to any
  other force, having created everything.
- Rabbi Edel: *adir* is related to *adar*/[*hadar*](beauty-hod-hadar.html#%D7%94%D7%93%D7%A8), beauty and glory, since
  Hashem's acts of strength bring Him honor and glory by showing man His
  omnipotence.
- Shadal (to Shemos 15:6): *adir* is the Aramaic form of [*ezor*](belt-avnet-chagor-eizor.html#%D7%90%D7%96%D7%95%D7%A8), Hebrew zayin
  often becoming dalet in Aramaic, because *adir* is power that encompasses
  everything, as a belt encircles the body; so too [*aderes*](garment-aderet-glima-itztala.html#%D7%90%D7%93%D7%A8%D7%AA), a cloak that goes
  around it.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (Shekalim 5754): *adir* is the level at which a thing's
  strength is one with its very being. A power one has gathered and acquired is
  something other than oneself; *adir* is strength identical with the one who
  has it. So *mikolos mayim rabim adirim mishberei yam* (Tehillim 93:4): the
  might is in the waters themselves. The malachim argued that this level cannot
  rest on earth, hence *asher tenah hodcha al hashamayim* (Tehillim 8:2), and
  when they conceded to Moshe Rabbeinu they closed with *mah adir shimcha bechol
  ha'aretz* (8:10). The Avos are called *adirim* (Menachos 53a) because they
  carry all of this within themselves. So too the Gemara (Beitzah 15b): one who
  wants his property to endure should plant an *adar* in it, for *adir bamarom
  Hashem*. The Aruch (in *Mesores HaShas*) explains that he should give tzedakah
  from it, which makes the property his in essence; Rashi, that the *adar* is a
  notable tree by which the field is known as his. Either way, says Rabbi Moshe
  Shapiro, the *adar* raises a thing from something acquired to something one's
  own in essence.

*Takif*: the standard Aramaic of the Targumim for *chozek* and *oz*, and also a
Hebrew word in Tanach (see Esther 9:29, 10:2; Koheles 4:12; Daniel 4:27).

- Rabbi Pappenheim traces it to ק-ף, a complete circle (*hakafah*/[*heikef*](around-saviv-heikef-afef-chug.html#%D7%94%D7%99%D7%A7%D7%A4),
  circumference, encircle; *kafah*/*kafui*, frozen, since the outer perimeter of
  a liquid freezes first; he also relates *kof*, monkey, to this root but admits
  he does not know how to explain the connection). His etymology recalls his
  tracing of *chayil*, strength, to ח-ל, circular motion: *tokef*/*takif* is
  strength that can totally surround and overpower another.
- Rabbi Edel: describing Hashem as *takif* invokes His all-encompassing power
  and sovereignty.

The Talmud's Aramaic *matkif* is one sage "attacking" or overpowering another's
position with a persuasive argument.

## Rendering guidance

- *koach* "strength"; *gevurah* "might", or "heroism" where the passage works
  with Rabbi Soloveitchik's sense; so the morning berachos can read "who girds
  Yisrael with might" and "who gives strength to the tired" (Rabbi
  Soloveitchik would give *gevurah* as "heroism"); *chozek* "strength",
  *chazak* "strong"; *ometz* "fortitude", "resolve"; *oz* "might", *az* "bold";
  *eitan* "steadfast"; *adir* "mighty"; *kabir* "mighty in number"; *takif*
  "overpowering"; *otzem* "vastness" (number) or "innate strength".
- חזק ואמץ: "be strong and resolute" fits Bedersi and
  Wertheimer's scale (the greater word second) and the Vilna Gaon's outer
  strength and strength of heart alike; "strong and courageous" only if
  "courageous" is held for *ometz* throughout. Malbim's temporary against lasting
  strength and Rabbi Shapira-Frankfurter's courage to enter against courage to
  stay are further readings; where a passage works
  with one of them, the English should follow it.
- Trap: "strength" absorbs every word here, "power" absorbs *koach*, *oz* and
  *takif*. When several sit together, assign each its English before drafting
  and hold it throughout. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (a late 15th century Italian
  scholar) counts 36 Biblical words for strength or power in *Ohel Moed*, his
  lexicon of synonyms; Yonah Wilheimer (1830 to 1913), the Viennese publisher of
  its 1881 edition, notes that this is somewhat of an exaggeration.
