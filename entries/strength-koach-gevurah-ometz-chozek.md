# Strength: koach / gevurah / chozek / ometz (otzem, oz, eitan, adir, kabir, takif)

## Bottom line

*Koach* is **strength**, the general word: the plain ability to do or withstand
something (Rabbi Pappenheim); in Tanach and Torah sources generally physical
strength, which man shares with beast (Rabbi Soloveitchik). *Gevurah* is
**might** or **heroism**: for Rabbi Pappenheim the act that actualizes potential
*koach*, for Rabbi Mecklenburg *koach* used to defeat opposition rather than
merely withstand it, for Rabbi Soloveitchik the heroism given to man alone to
transcend the instinct of survival; the Mishnah's *gibbor* conquers his yetzer
(Avos 4:1). On *chozek* the two articles carry different views: for Bedersi and
Wertheimer it is the standard word for **strong** and *ometz* is strength above
the normal measure, while for Rabbi Pappenheim *chozek* is already
extra-strength *koach*, the strength by which one stands out above the rest.
The Vilna Gaon makes *chozek* outer, physical strength and *ometz* strength of
the heart, and the article gives four more readings of *chazak v'ematz*.
*Eitan* is strength that holds back (Rabbi Edel) or endures (Rav Hirsch); *adir* the most powerful of a
whole group (Rabbi Pappenheim); *oz* strength of spirit that others cannot
deter (Wertheimer), or, for Rabbi Wessely, primarily Divine, supernatural power;
*takif* strength that surrounds and **overpowers** (Rabbi Pappenheim). Trap: one
English word for all; never let *koach* and *gevurah* both be "strength".

## Distinctions

### koach (כח)
Rabbi Pappenheim: the most general of the words, the plain ability to do or
withstand something. Rabbi J. B. Soloveitchik (1903 to 1993), in *Catharsis*:
*koach* in Tanach and Torah sources generally means physical strength, and is
not unique to man, since man and beast alike possess it; so the morning
berachos thank Hashem twice, for girding Yisrael with *gevurah* and as He "who
gives *koach* to the tired," recalling two different aspects of man's powers.
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865), in a similar way to Rabbi
Pappenheim: *koach* is the power of maintaining a given state, withstanding the
forces that try to break it. Alternatively, *koach* is mental fortitude and
concentration: so the Targum (to Devarim 8:18) renders *koach* as *eitzah*, the
mental strength to think a problem through and decide on a course, and
answering *Amen yehei shmei rabbah* "with all his *koach*" (Shabbos 119b) means
with all one's mental focus, not screaming the words as loud as possible (see
Rashi there, pace Tosafos).

### gevurah (גבורה)
The Mishnah (Avos 4:1) asks who is a *gibbor* and answers, he who conquers his
yetzer; the article reads this as tying *gevurah* to self-control, as it ties
*eitan* to restraint. Rabbi Soloveitchik echoes the idea: *gevurah* is given
exclusively to man, his ability to transcend the brute instinct of survival he
shares with the animals, and is best translated "heroism," the courage to enter
a situation that may not be ideal for his physical survival. Accordingly a
*gibbor* is not always the victor but always one who enters the battle and
fights with all his might, and one who dares to keep fighting for what he
believes in generally emerges the victor even if he loses the immediate battle.
Rabbi Pappenheim offers a similar explication, but acknowledges that Tanach
also borrows the word for elements of nature without free will, like water
(e.g. Bereishis 7:18-19); for him *gevurah* is the act by which one actualizes
one's potential *koach*, an outward show of strength. Rabbi Mecklenburg: where
*koach* withstands what opposes it, *gevurah* is a more proactive use of *koach*
that seeks to defeat the opposition entirely. The article adds, on its own
account and conditionally: if *gevurah* entails holding oneself back, Hashem is
the immaculate *Gibbor*, holding back His anger and giving the wicked time
before punishing them (see Yoma 69b), and for this reason He is often called
*Gibbor* (see Yirmiyahu 32:18 and the daily liturgy). Rabbi Shapira-Frankfurter
(below) makes *gevurah* physical strength.

### chozek (חזק) and ometz (אמץ)
Moshe, passing the leadership to Yehoshua, tells him *chazak v'ematz* (Devarim
31:7, 31:23); the article has Hashem repeat it four more times (Yehoshua
1:6-18; see Sources); David says it to Shlomo (Divrei HaYamim I 22:13, 28:20).
The article asks what the seemingly redundant pair means, and gives these
readings:

1. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (a 13th century Spanish sage) and Rabbi
   Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935): *chozek* is the standard word for
   strength, *ometz* an above-normal amount. A man who grew weak and was nursed
   back to a normal man's strength has *chozek*; *ometz* cannot be said of him.
2. The Vilna Gaon (to Yehoshua 1:7, Mishlei 24:5): *chozek* is outer, physical
   strength, *ometz* strength in the heart. Malbim echoes this in *Yair Ohr*,
   his work on synonyms.
3. Malbim elsewhere (to Yeshayahu 28:2) seems to take a different approach:
   *chozek* is temporary strength that atrophies with time, *ometz* a resilient
   strength that constantly recharges itself and never falters.
4. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743 to 1826), in *HaRechasim
   LeVikah* (Bereishis 25:23), departs from this model with three kinds of
   strength: *gevurah* physical strength, *ometz* strength of the heart
   (spiritual resolve), *chozek* strength of will (courage). *Chozek* is needed
   to enter a battle or any danger without fear, *ometz* to stay in the battle
   and not run away when things heat up.
5. Berachos 32b: *chazak* is strength in Torah study, *ematz* in good deeds.
   Rabbi Wertheimer fits this to his view: the greater a scholar becomes, the
   more effort he must exert to keep performing good deeds and not lose himself
   in the theoretical world of study, and that super-human fortifying of the
   will is properly *ometz*.
6. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814): *ometz* is from מ-צ,
   sucking or squeezing (*mitz*, juice; *metzitzah*, sucking), a person
   squeezing out every last ounce of energy. Red horses are *amutzim*
   (Zechariah 6:3) because their exertion brings their blood to the surface of
   the skin, as if squeezed out, coloring even their hair red.

Rabbi Pappenheim also sets *chozek* against *tokef*: *chozek* is the strength to
withstand being overpowered by another, *tokef* the strength actually to
overpower others. In "The Power to Hold Back" he makes *chozek* extra-strength
*koach*, a non-standard ability by which one person stands out above the rest,
and calling someone *chazak* means he is more powerful than someone else (see
*adir*, below). The articles do not set this beside Bedersi and Wertheimer, for
whom *chozek* is the standard word.

### otzem (עצם) and oz (עז)
Rabbi Dr. Asher Weiser (editor of the Mossad HaRav Kook edition of Ibn Ezra on
the Torah): *ometz* is related to *otzem*, the aleph interchanging with the ayin
and the other two consonants switching places by metathesis. Rabbi Yehuda Leib
Edel (1760 to 1828): *otzem*/*etzem* is growth in added quantity (see Shemos 1:8
as printed, 1:20; Devarim 7:1; Yoel 2:2; Tehillim 35:18), strength through
numbers, and from there the word expanded to inherent strength, added quality
and not only quantity. Alternatively, it is borrowed from *etzem*, bone, the
strongest part of the body.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935 to 2017) finds a common theme in all words with ע-צ:
*eitz*, a tree, contains within itself everything that will come from it;
*etzem*, self, the sum of one's potential; *atzuv*, sad, one who retreats into
himself and does not expand outward; *atzel*, lazy, who keeps his potential
bottled up; *otzer*, stop, holding something back from expanding; *eitzah*,
advice, which an honest advisor can give only as far as he grasps the *etzem* of
the matter. So *otzem* is potential strength pent up within a person and not yet
outwardly expressed. (Rabbi Pappenheim offers a similar exposition of ע-צ.)
Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916): a tree is *eitz* for its hard, strong trunk,
and *atzuv* is related to *eitz* as wood, a dry state bereft of life and
happiness; in a fascinating twist he connects *otzem* and *eitz* to *oz*, all
meaning strong or hard, by the interchange of tzadi and zayin.

Rabbi Wertheimer compares *oz*/*izuz* to *az*, strong or bold: ע-ז is strength of
spirit, a resolve others cannot easily dissuade or deter. A person's *oz* is so
palpable that it shows in his face (see Koheles 8:1), and so it is poetically
called a garment he wears (see Mishlei 31:24 as printed; see Sources). Moshe
told the spies to see whether the Canaanites were *chazak* or weak (Bamidbar
13:18), and they reported them *az* (13:28): charged with gauging physical
strength, they examined the Canaanites' psychological resolve instead, and found
a determination that could be seen on their faces.

Rabbi Naftali Hertz Wessely (Weisel, 1725 to 1805): the primary meaning of *oz*
always refers to Divine, supernatural power, in contrast to *gevurah*, which can
also refer to power within the normal course of nature. Rabbi Edel cites him and
adds that this is the sense of "Hashem gives *oz* to His nation" (Tehillim 29:11;
the article prints 28:11): He lifts Yisrael above the rules of nature. On this
view *oz* means anything strong only in a borrowed sense. "Give *oz* to God"
(Tehillim 68:35) cannot literally mean that a mortal strengthens Hashem; when
Yisrael follow His will He showers them with abundance, as if they had given
Him more strength (see Eichah Rabbah 1:33). Rabbi Yerucham Levovitz (1873 to
1936) draws a lesson from this: when you do someone a favor, let him do
something for you in return so that he does not feel forever indebted; so
Hashem, who does us the ultimate favor of giving and sustaining our life, lets
us feel we can do something for Him in return by keeping the Torah and giving
Him *oz*.

### eitan (איתן)
"The Power to Hold Back" opens with Tishrei, which Melachim I 8:2 calls the
month of the *Eitanim*. The Gemara (Rosh Hashanah 11a) explains that the Avos,
called *eitanim*, "powerful ones," were born in it, which leads the Gemara
elsewhere to identify Eitan HaEzrachi (Tehillim 89:1) with Avraham, the
"powerful" one who stemmed the tide of polytheism (see Maharsha to Bava Basra
15a). The article sets out to show that five words for power, *eitan*, *koach*,
*gevurah*, *adir* and *kabir*, are not true synonyms.

*Nachal eitan* (Devarim 21:4): for Rashi *nachal* is a valley or wadi and
*eitan* strong or hard, a rocky place; for Rambam (Hilchos Rotzeach 9:2)
*nachal* is a river and *eitan* strong-flowing. Rabbi Edel, without referring to
either, reconciles their views: *eitan* is literally the river bank, which seems
to hold the waters back from passing its threshold, and that restraint takes
great strength, so *eitan* came to mean strength and power. The article then
observes that *eitan* describes Yosef's unshakeable righteousness (Bereishis
49:24) and the rocky habitat of Yisro's descendants (Bamidbar 24:21), who like
Avraham showed strong discipline and self-control. Rav Samson Raphael Hirsch
(to Shemos 14:27) adds that *eitan* is something strong and durable that has
lasted a long time, like bedrock. On Tishrei, the Gemara there adds that even if
the Avos were not born in it, the month is "powerful" for being blessed with
more timely mitzvos than other months; Metzudas David (to Melachim I 8:2): it
is a powerful month for its many holidays that serve self-perfection. The
article's lesson: Tishrei can make a person a firm, strongly anchored stalwart,
as hard to budge as rock.

Rabbi Pappenheim admits he is unsure how to break the word down. In *Yerios
Shlomo* he takes the root as ת-ן, "give": *eitan* is a concentration of
strength that can only be a Divine gift, which no one could mistake for
something natural. In *Cheshek Shlomo* he connects it to א-ת, reduced further to
ת, "connection" (*es* connects a verb to its object and sometimes means "with";
*os*, a sign, connects the sign and the signified): *eitan* is power
concentrated by the extreme compression of many connected components, as in
rock. He also makes *eitan* inherent, qualitative strength, strong in itself
even if compressed from smaller parts, as against *kabir*.

### kabir (כביר), adir (אדיר), takif (תקיף)
*Kabir* (Rabbi Pappenheim): quantitative strength, strong for being made of many
units banded together (see Rashi to Berachos 8a, who associates *kabir* with the
power of communal prayer). He then divides quantitative strength: *otzem* where
the sub-units do not necessarily contribute an equal force, *kabir* where the
components are equally powerful.

*Adir*: Rabbi Pappenheim, calling someone *chazak* means he is more powerful
than someone else, *adir* that he is the most powerful of a whole group. The
article finds it apt that Hashem is described as *adir* (see Shemos 15:6;
Tehillim 8:2, 93:4). Rabbi Pappenheim traces *adir* to ד-ר, freedom of motion
without limitation or constriction, and Hashem is free of subjection to any
other force. Rabbi Edel: *adir* is related to *adar*/*hadar*, beauty and glory,
since Hashem's acts of strength bring Him honor and glory by showing man His
omnipotence.

*Takif*: the standard Aramaic of the Targumim for *chozek* and *oz*, and also a
Hebrew word in Tanach (see Esther 9:29, 10:2; Koheles 4:12; Daniel 4:27). Rabbi
Pappenheim traces it to ק-פ, a complete circle (*hakafah*/*heikef*,
circumference, encircle; *kafah*/*kafui*, frozen, since the outer perimeter of a
liquid freezes first; he also relates *kof*, monkey, to this root but admits he
does not know how to explain the connection). His etymology recalls his tracing
of *chayil*, strength, to ח-ל, circular motion: *tokef*/*takif* is strength that
can totally surround and overpower another. Rabbi Edel: describing Hashem as
*takif* invokes His all-encompassing power and sovereignty. The Talmud's
Aramaic *matkif* is one sage "attacking" or overpowering another's position
with a persuasive argument.

## Rendering guidance

- *koach* "strength"; *gevurah* "might", or "heroism" where the passage works
  with Rabbi Soloveitchik's sense; so the morning berachos can read "who girds
  Yisrael with might" and "who gives strength to the tired" (our rendering;
  Rabbi Soloveitchik would give *gevurah* as "heroism"); *chozek* "strength",
  *chazak* "strong"; *ometz* "fortitude", "resolve"; *oz* "might", *az* "bold";
  *eitan* "steadfast"; *adir* "mighty"; *kabir* "mighty in number"; *takif*
  "overpowering"; *otzem* "vastness" (number) or "innate strength".
- חזק ואמץ: our rendering is "be strong and resolute", which fits Bedersi and
  Wertheimer's scale (the greater word second) and the Vilna Gaon's outer
  strength and strength of heart alike; "strong and courageous" only if
  "courageous" is held for *ometz* throughout. Malbim's temporary against lasting
  strength and Rabbi Shapira-Frankfurter's courage to enter against courage to
  stay are further readings the article leaves standing; where a passage works
  with one of them, the English should follow it.
- Trap: "strength" absorbs every word here, "power" absorbs *koach*, *oz* and
  *takif*. When several sit together, assign each its English before drafting
  and hold it throughout. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (a late 15th century Italian
  scholar) counts 36 Biblical words for strength or power in *Ohel Moed*, his
  lexicon of synonyms; Yonah Wilheimer (1830 to 1913), the Viennese publisher of
  its 1881 edition, notes that this is somewhat of an exaggeration.
