# Whistle: shreikah / tziftzuf

## Bottom line

*Shreikah* is a **whistle**, used in two opposite settings: the whistle or hiss
of astonishment at a ruined place that had been thriving, and the signal by
which Hashem calls the masses to gather, at the end of days to gather His people
home. Rabbi Yaakov Berger notes that biblical *shreikah* seemingly always means
whistling with the mouth alone, while the Aramaic *mashrokita* of Daniel is an
instrument. *Tziftzuf*, which the column calls its apparent synonym, is
**whistling, chirping, tweeting**, the bird's sound, and by extension a low,
rapid whisper or half-intelligible **prattle**; in the Rema's halachic usage
*mitzaftzef* is simply "to whistle." This entry reserves English "whistle" for
ש-ר-ק and gives *tziftzuf* "chirp" or "prattle" where the two stand together,
so the roots stay apart in English; that is a rendering choice, not a
distinction the sources draw.

## Distinctions

### shreikah (שריקה)
Root ש-ר-ק. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*, and to Zechariah 10:8) quite
prosaically defines it broadly as moving the lips to produce a sound. Rabbi
Shlomo Ibn Prachon (*Machberes HeAruch*) expands on it: specifically puckering
the lips and exhaling air through them; he adds that the vowel *shuruk* is
related to the root because the mouth puckers to make its *oo* sound as though
about to whistle. The column wondered whether Hebrew ש-ר-ק, whistling, is
connected to Aramaic ש-ר-ק, smooth or slippery, since whistling makes a smooth
sound, but its language guru Rabbi Shaul Goldman rejected the supposition. Two
uses stand opposite each other in Tanach.

*Shreikah* of desolation: passersby whistle at the ruin of a place that had
been thriving. So Eichah 2:15 to 16 of Yerushalayim after its fall, and so the
warning already given to Shlomo HaMelech at the Beis HaMikdash's construction
(Melachim I 9:8). Yirmiyahu warns of it constantly (19:8, 25:9, 29:18, 50:13).
Rashi (to Eichah) defines it as a whistle or hiss through the teeth, and adds
that in ancient times people whistled on encountering an important site that
had fallen still.

*Shreikah* of summons: Hashem whistles to the fly at the edge of Egypt's rivers,
impelling a multitude of Egyptian soldiers to gather and invade Ashur
(Yeshayahu 7:18), and whistles to the nations to gather against Yisrael
(Yeshayahu 5:26). In the most uplifting sense, He whistles to gather His people
in from exile (Zechariah 10:8); Rashi there explains it from the way whistlers
whistle to call those who have gotten lost, so Hashem will invite all who have
strayed to return to Him. The *Tur* (*Orach Chaim* §286) famously explains the
reversed-alphabet acrostic that opens *Tikanta Shabbos* as alluding to
*tishrok*, "He will whistle," the last four letters of the alef-beis, the Final
Whistle that brings the Redemption.

English *screak* and *shriek* are typically traced to the Proto-Indo-European
root *ker* or *skr*; the column wonders whether the Hebrew *shreikah* had some
influence on them, or whether both are onomatopoeic imitations of sounds heard
in nature.

### the sharakrak / racham (שרקרק, רחם)
*Chullin* 63a identifies the non-kosher *racham* (Vayikra 11:18; Devarim 14:17)
as the *sharakrak*, a name clearly derived from ש-ר-ק, and teaches that its
sitting on the ground and chirping or whistling is a sign of Mashiach's
approach, citing Zechariah 10:8. *Perek Shirah* gives that same pasuk as the
*racham*'s song. Maharsha (to *Chullin* 63a), Rabbi Avraham Palagi (*Avraham
Anochi* to Yeshayahu §52) and Rabbi Shlomo Tapiero (*Mikveh HaMayim* to
Yeshayahu 52:7) resolve the "feet of the herald" of Yeshayahu 52:7, where one
would expect a herald's mouth, by this: the herald is the *racham*, whose feet
on the ground as it chirps are the sign that portends the redemption. Rabbi
Yissachar Shlomo Teichtal (*Eim HaBanim Semeichah* 2:12), asked how the
beginning of the redemption could come through Zionist pioneers who violate
Shabbos, deflected the question by noting that in the Gemara too the harbinger
is an impure bird, the *racham*, not a kosher one. On which bird is meant, the
column refers to Rabbi Amitai Ben-David's *Sichas Chullin* to *Chullin* 63a.

### mashrokita (משרוקיתא)
An instrument in Nevuchadnetzar's music (Daniel 3:5, 3:7, 3:10, 3:15), in the
Aramaic part of Daniel; there is no real way of knowing which instrument is
meant. Ibn Ezra (to Daniel 3:5) surmises that it is cogent to take the root as
ש-ר-ק, so some wind instrument with a whistle-like sound (perhaps a band of
pipers or flutists); Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) lists it under
that root, and Rashi to Daniel 3:5 makes a similar point briefly. Rabbi Yaakov
Berger of Kiryat Sefer (*Milon Leshon Mikra*, unpublished) points out that
biblical Hebrew *shreikah* seemingly always means whistling with the mouth
alone, while the Aramaic *mashrokita* whistles through an instrument.

### tziftzuf (צפצוף)
Four times in Tanach as a verb for whistling, chirping, tweeting, all in
Yeshayahu (8:19, 10:14, 29:4, 38:14). A noun of the same four letters,
*tzaftzafah*, appears once, a type of willow (Yechezkel 17:5); Rabbi Moshe
Kimchi (to Yechezkel) bridges the two meanings of the quadriliteral by noting
that birds would gather at that willow and chirp there. Ibn Janach and Radak
(*Sefer HaShorashim*) trace *tziftzuf* to the four-letter צ-פ-צ-ף. Menachem Ibn
Saruk instead reads it as the doubled biliteral צ-פ, and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
(*Cheshek Shlomo*), implicitly following him, writes at length on that root:
its core is something that spreads on top of something else, giving *tzofeh*
and *mitzpeh* (a gaze spread over a wide field), then *tzipiyah* (waiting, the
man in trouble looking in every direction for his rescuer), and then
*tziftzuf*, the bird's sound as the voice of its anticipation of its mate or its
next meal.

*Tziftzuf* often refers to a low whisper, which sounds almost like whistling
since the mouth is narrowed as in whistling: speaking quickly, constantly, low.
It can also mean verbal pattering, including blabbering about rumors. Rashi and
Ramban (to Vayikra 14:4) use it for the mindless prattling, typified by
*lashon hara*, that brings *tzaraas*. Asked how that squares with "whistling,"
Rabbi Shaul Goldman explained that the original sense is bird-noise, also used
for babbling or any speech meant to be audible but almost always
unintelligible; that is the descriptive sense. The column adds its own
prescriptive sense: speech whose meaning renders it unfit, such as rumors and
*lashon hara*.

In Modern Hebrew *tziftzuf* is the sound of an alarm, and one who is
*mitzaftzef* ignores the pleas of others and marches to his own beat. The column
says this is probably borrowed from Yiddish *fife* (a cognate of English
*pipe*), "whistle," which came to mean ignoring others, as one who keeps
whistling while told to stop. It adds that it is not insignificant that the
Germanic *piper*/*fifer* repeat a *p*/*f* sound as *tziftzuf* repeats its *f*,
and that it is convinced English *cheep* is connected to the *tzif* of
*tziftzuf* (the column prints *tifz*).

### Whistling as a practice
The late Lubavitcher Rebbe encouraged his followers to whistle to express
elated happiness, and perhaps to symbolize, or even bring about, the impending
*geulah*. The column traces this to the Rashaz, who taught (*Ohr Torah* to
Bereishis 49:11) that the *shuruk*, the vowel tied to *shreikah*, has three dots
one below the other because a person should lower himself to the
self-effacement of recognizing that he is nothing before Hashem, a level reached
through loving Hashem in the happiness of mitzvos, and which he calls the entire
purpose of the Messianic era. In other chassidic and mussar circles teachers
commonly chide students not to whistle as a *goyish* practice. Rabbi Efrayim
Greenblatt (*Rivevos Efrayim* 8:604) knows of no source forbidding it, though
his cheder teacher told him it was forbidden. The *Chazon Ish* (cited in
*Orchos Shabbos* ch. 21 fn. 60) said that nowadays whistling is a breach of
modesty, though in *Aliba D'Hilchasa* (vol. 69 p. 83) Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky is
quoted as never having heard this from him. The column's teacher Rabbi Yosef
Yitzchok Lerner (*Shemiras HaGuf VeHaNefesh Tinyana* p. 454) cites a bevy of
Kabbalistic sources that whistling can arouse harsh judgments, and that the
arch-heretic Jacob Frank used whistling as evil magic.

## Rendering guidance

- *Shreikah* at a ruin: "whistle" works, but "hiss" is often truer to Rashi's
  through-the-teeth description and avoids the cheerful English connotation.
  For a place made an object of it, "an object of hissing" reads better than
  "a place of whistling."
- *Shreikah* as summons: "whistle for," never "whistle at." Yeshayahu 5:26 and
  7:18 are calls to muster; Zechariah 10:8 is a call to come home.
- *Tziftzuf*: "chirp" or "twitter" for birds, "prattle" or "babble" for people.
  Reserve "whistle" for ש-ר-ק so the two roots stay apart in English.
- Trap: English "whistle" will swallow *shreikah*, *tziftzuf* and *mashrokita*
  at once. Where a passage puts them together, or where a derashah turns on the
  root (the *Tur* on *tishrok*, the *sharakrak* of *Chullin* 63a), keep ש-ר-ק
  rendered by "whistle" and push *tziftzuf* to "chirp."
- Halachic register: the Rema (*Orach Chaim* §338:1) rules that one may be
  *mitzaftzef* on Shabbos, that is, whistle. Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein
  (*Aruch HaShulchan* there) finds that obvious for the mouth, and whistling
  with an instrument obviously forbidden, so he proposes that the Rema's
  novelty is that even whistling with the fingers is allowed. There,
  *mitzaftzef* is plainly "to whistle," not "to chirp."
- Modern Hebrew *tziftzuf* (an alarm's beep; and *mitzaftzef* for a man who
  ignores everyone and goes his own way) is a later development and must not be
  read back into a pasuk.
