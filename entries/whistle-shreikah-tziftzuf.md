# Whistle: shreikah / tziftzuf

## Bottom line

*Shreikah* is a **whistle** made with the mouth alone, in two opposite settings:
the hiss of astonishment at a ruined city, and the signal-whistle by which a man
calls the scattered back to him. *Tziftzuf* is the bird's **chirp**, and by
extension a low, rapid, half-intelligible **prattle**. Do not let English
"whistle" cover both: a *tziftzuf* is a sound made about something, a *shreikah*
is a sound made at something. The Aramaic *mashrokita* (Daniel) is the one place
the root leaves the mouth and becomes an instrument.

## Distinctions

### shreikah (שריקה)
Root ש-ר-ק. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*, and to Zechariah 10:8) defines it
plainly as moving the lips to produce a sound. Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Prachon
(*Machberes HeAruch*) narrows it: puckering the lips and forcing air through
them; he adds that the vowel *shuruk* carries the name because the mouth
puckers to make the *oo* sound. Two uses stand opposite each other in Tanach.

*Shreikah* of desolation: passersby whistle at the ruin of a place that had
been thriving. So Eichah 2:15 to 16 of Yerushalayim after the churban, and so
the warning already given to Shlomo HaMelech at the Beis HaMikdash's dedication
(Melachim I 9:8). Yirmiyahu returns to it repeatedly (19:8, 25:9, 29:18,
50:13). Rashi (to Eichah) explains it as a hiss through the teeth, and records
that whistling at a great site fallen silent was the practice of the time.

*Shreikah* of summons: Hashem whistles to the fly at the edge of Egypt's rivers
to bring its forces up (Yeshayahu 7:18), and whistles to the nations to gather
against Yisrael (Yeshayahu 5:26). At the far end, He whistles to gather His
people in from exile (Zechariah 10:8); Rashi there explains it from the way a
whistler whistles to call back those who have wandered off. The *Tur* (*Orach
Chaim* §286) reads the reversed-alphabet acrostic that opens *Tikanta Shabbos*
as spelling *tishrok*, the final whistle of the Redemption, out of the last four
letters of the alef-beis.

### the sharakrak / racham (שרקרק, רחם)
*Chullin* 63a identifies the non-kosher *racham* (Vayikra 11:18; Devarim 14:17)
as the *sharakrak*, a name built straight off ש-ר-ק, and teaches that its
sitting on the ground and whistling is a sign of Mashiach's approach, citing
Zechariah 10:8. *Perek Shirah* gives that same pasuk as the *racham*'s song.
Maharsha (to *Chullin* 63a), Rabbi Avraham Palagi (*Avraham Anochi* to
Yeshayahu 52) and Rabbi Shlomo Tapiero (*Mikveh HaMayim* to Yeshayahu 52:7)
resolve the "feet of the herald" of Yeshayahu 52:7 by this: the herald is the
*racham*, whose feet on the ground are what makes its whistle the sign.

### mashrokita (משרוקיתא)
The Aramaic instrument in Nevuchadnetzar's orchestra (Daniel 3:5, 3:7, 3:10,
3:15). Ibn Ezra (to Daniel 3:5) and, briefly, Rashi take the root as ש-ר-ק, so
a wind instrument with a whistling tone; Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*) lists it under that root. Rabbi Yaakov Berger (*Milon Leshon Mikra*,
unpublished) marks the distinction that matters for rendering: biblical Hebrew
*shreikah* is always mouth-whistling, while the Aramaic *mashrokita* whistles
through an instrument.

### tziftzuf (צפצוף)
Four times in Tanach as a verb, all in Yeshayahu (8:19, 10:14, 29:4, 38:14).
Ibn Janach and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) take it from the quadriliteral
צ-פ-צ-ף, which also yields *tzaftzafah*, a willow (Yechezkel 17:5); Rabbi Moshe
Kimchi (to Yechezkel) joins the two senses by noting that birds gather at that
willow and chirp there. Menachem Ibn Saruk instead reads *tziftzuf* as the
doubled biliteral צ-פ, and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) builds on
that: the core of צ-פ is spreading out over something, giving *tzofeh* and
*mitzpeh* (a gaze spread over a wide field), then *tzipiyah* (waiting, the man
in trouble looking in every direction for his rescuer), and then *tziftzuf*, the
bird's sound as the voice of its anticipation of its mate or its food.

The second sense of *tziftzuf* is human: rapid, low, barely articulate speech.
Rashi and Ramban (to Vayikra 14:4) use it for the mindless prattling, typified
by *lashon hara*, that brings *tzaraas*. Rabbi Shaul Goldman: the original sense
is bird-noise, extended to any speech meant to be heard but effectively
unintelligible.

## Rendering guidance

- *Shreikah* at a ruin: "whistle" works, but "hiss" is often truer to Rashi's
  through-the-teeth description and avoids the cheerful English connotation.
  For a place made an object of it, "an object of hissing" reads better than
  "a place of whistling."
- *Shreikah* as summons: "whistle for," never "whistle at." Yeshayahu 5:26 and
  7:18 are calls to muster; Zechariah 10:8 is a call to come home.
- *Tziftzuf*: "chirp" or "twitter" for birds, "prattle" or "babble" for people.
  Reserve "whistle" for ש-ר-ק so the two roots stay apart in English.
- Trap: English "whistle" will swallow *shreikah*, *tziftzuf* and *mashrokita*
  at once. Where a passage puts them together, or where a derashah turns on the
  root (the *Tur* on *tishrok*, the *sharakrak* of *Chullin* 63a), keep ש-ר-ק
  rendered by "whistle" and push *tziftzuf* to "chirp."
- Halachic register: the Rema (*Orach Chaim* §338:1) permits being *mitzaftzef*
  on Shabbos, which the *Aruch HaShulchan* explains as extending even to
  whistling with the fingers. There, *mitzaftzef* is plainly "to whistle," not
  "to chirp"; the halachic idiom has absorbed the ש-ר-ק sense.
- Modern Hebrew *tziftzuf* (an alarm's beep; and *mitzaftzef* for a man who
  ignores everyone and goes his own way) is a later development and must not be
  read back into a pasuk.
