# Chewing: koses / lo'es

## Bottom line

Both are rabbinic; neither is biblical, and Biblical Hebrew has no plain verb
for chewing at all (*maaleh geirah* means brings up the cud, not chews it).
*Koses* is to **chew**, and Rashi defines it both as eating something raw and as
eating in an unusual way. *Lo'es* is also to **chew**, and Rashi flatly equates
the two (*lo'es* means *koses*). Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own suggestion is
that perhaps no semantic difference is called for at all, since the two seem to
come from different languages. Members of the Mazuz family, however, seem to
presume a difference. Rabbi Meir Mazuz holds that *le'isah* typically implies
chewing toward swallowing, while *kesisah* does not necessarily carry that
implication; Rabbi Yitzchak Barda takes *kesisah* as the more enjoying chew and
*le'isah* as the more workaday one. Rabbi Klein grants that the difference the
Mazuz family presumes is hard to substantiate, and hard even to state exactly.

## Distinctions

### koses / kesisah (כסס, כסיסה)
The one with the wider spread of citations. Berachos 37a: one who is *koses* raw wheat or rice
says *borei pri ha'adamah*, not *borei minei mezonos*; the Tosefta (*Berachos*
4:6-7) uses the same verb for the rejected opinion of *borei minei zera'im*.
Mishnah *Nedarim* 6:10 and Tosefta *Nedarim* 3:7 ask whether one who forswore
wheat may still be *lachus* raw wheat. Berachos 36b and Yoma 81a use *kas* for
chewing dry pepper or ginger on Yom Kippur, which Rava holds is not biblically
"eating." Megillah 7b has Abaye so hungry he could be *koses* the plate.

Rashi gives two definitions.

- To Megillah 7b, Kesubos 77a, Berachos 36b, Yoma 81a and Chullin 15a: *kesisah*
  is eating in any abnormal or non-standard way. (Maharsha to Megillah 7b
  suggests the plate there may simply have been edible.)
- To Beitzah 26b: whatever can be eaten raw, eating it raw is *koses*.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein calls this second one a slightly different definition.

Etymology: several philologists, among them Dr. Asher Weiser and Rabbi Yaakov
Yehudah Zilberberg (*Di Kasif*), tie rabbinic כ-ס-ס to biblical ק-ס-ס, which
occurs once, *yikoses*, of cutting down a metaphoric tree's fruit (Yechezkel
17:9).

- Rashi there links ק-ס-ס to ק-צ-ץ (cutting) by the samech/tzadi interchange.
- Mahari Kara links it to כ-ת-ת (smashing) by kuf/kaf and samech/tav.
- Ibn Janach and Radak take the root as triliteral.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk takes it as the biliteral ק-ס.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) implicitly follows the biliteral
  and defines ק-ס as "a cut tube": *yikoses* takes the cutting side; *keses* (a
  scribe's stylus, Yechezkel 9:2, 9:11), *kesem* (a magic wand, or magical
  writings written with a *keses*), and *kisam* (toothpick) take the tube side,
  tubes cut lengthwise, while the *kesavos* of the Shulchan (Shemos 25:29,
  Bamidbar 4:7) are tubes cut across their width. He includes *kaskeses*, the
  scale of a kosher fish, in the same family.

Two extensions. *Kaskasa* is to crunch or crush between the teeth; the piyut
*Elokei HaRuchos* of Rabbi Yosef Tuv-Elem of Bonfils, recited in some
communities on Shabbos HaGadol, requires *kiskusei* of the maror, which the
*Ohr Zarua* (*Hilchos Pesachim* 256) reads as munching, so that one does not
swallow the maror without tasting its bitterness, while Rabbi Shamshon Raphael
Ortzel of Bayit Ve'Gan suggests it may instead mean vigorous shaking (as in
*Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim* 651:9 for the lulav), meaning shake the charoses
off first. The verb *l'kaskes* in Mishnah *Niddah* 9:7 is vigorous rubbing in
laundering, which perhaps resembles chewing in its consistent forceful
movement.

Rabbi Ernest Klein
(no relation to Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein) traces *kesisah/koses* back to the Akkadian
*kasasu* (cutting into pieces) and *kissatu* (fodder), and holds them related to
an Arabic cognate meaning pounding, grinding or pulverizing.

The French *couscous* (from the Arabic *kuskus*), a food of small bits of
pounded grain, is also cognate with *koses*, since it describes chopped grains
that look as though they were pre-chewed; but the English *kasha* (buckwheat)
has no connection to it, having come by way of Yiddish from the Slavic
languages, where it meant groats, cereal or porridge and not buckwheat in
particular. The philologist Samuel Krauss links כ-ס-ס to ע-ס-ס (*assisi*,
juicy), possibly because chewing a food releases its inner juices and lets one
taste its flavors. And one Dr. A.
Weiss (in YU's rabbinic journal *Chorev*, 1943, vol. 7, p. 55) claims that the *kos* of
the rabbinic *kos shel akarin*, a contraceptive draught, is not *kos* in the
sense of cup at all but a cognate of *koses*, the ingredients having been cut or
chopped into small bits and mixed together.

Also from this root, *kisnin*: Targum renders the biblical *nikkudim*, food
provisions (Yehoshua 9:5, 9:12; Melachim I 14:3), as *kasnin*, and the halachic
*pas haba'ah b'kisnin* (Berachos 42a) uses the same word.

- Radak (to Melachim I 14:3) relates *kisnin* to *koses*: they are the sorts of
  treats one munches at a party or a wedding. Comparable definitions are cited
  in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel (*Ohr Zarua, Hilchos Berachos* 149) and Rav
  Hai Gaon (*Sefer HaNer* to Berachos 42a).
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Hoil Moshe* to Melachim I 14:3) suggests a
  different tie to *koses*, since *kisnin* are a food that crumbles into small
  pieces.

### lo'es / le'isah (לעס, לעיסה)
Three times in the Mishnah. *Shabbos* 19:2: if cumin was not ground before
Shabbos one may chew (*lo'es*) it whole for the milah dressing. *Niddah* 9:7:
chewing grits for a cleaning agent against blood stains. *Pesachim* 2:7: one
may not chew (*lo'es*) wheat on Pesach for a wound dressing, since it will
become chametz. Berachos 44b: spleen is good for the teeth and bad for the
bowels, so chew it and spit it out, again with *lo'es*.

Rashi twice defines it by the other word, to Pesachim 39b and to Berachos 44b:
*lo'es* is *koses*. The root ל-ע-ס is Aramaic and Syriac for chewing and appears
in Targum (to Koheles 12:3); beyond that its origin is obscure.

- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) explains *mitalot*, the
  molars that grind food right before one swallows, as a fusion of the
  biliterals ת-ל, hanging, and ל-ע, swallowing; he notes an affinity between
  that root and ל-ע-ט, to guzzle or gulp (*haliteini*, Bereishis 25:30, as his
  teacher Shadal notes there; *malitin*, force-feed, Mishnah *Shabbos* 24:3),
  and relates these roots as well to ל-ע-ס.
- Rabbi Avi Kobernick (*Hipuch Otiyos*) offers a speculative metathesis of
  ס-ל-ע, rock: a [*sela*](rock-tzur-chalamish.html#%D7%A1%D7%9C%D7%A2) is what will not break down, and *lo'es* is precisely
  breaking down.

### Are they synonyms?
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own suggestion is that perhaps the two need no semantic
difference at all, since they appear to be of different origins: *koses* seems
to be a native Hebrew word, *lo'es* seems to be a loanword from Aramaic, so
their overlap would not be superfluity.

Members of the Mazuz family of Tunisia seem to presume a real difference, though
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein grants that it is hard to substantiate and hard even to
say exactly what it means.
- Rabbi Matzliach Mazuz (*Ish Matzliach* vol. 2, *Orach Chaim* 47), discussing
  at length whether *pilpel* is the common pepper, black pepper, or both, raises
  how the use of *kas* rather than *lo'es* for chewing *pilpel* on a fast day
  might bear on that question.
- Rabbi Meir Mazuz (*Arim Nissi* to Pesachim 39b): *le'isah* typically implies
  chewing as part of eating, in preparation for swallowing, while *kesisah* does
  not necessarily bear that implication; that is why Rashi glosses *lo'es* as
  *koses* in the case of chewing wheat for a bandage, to say that this man was
  not chewing in order to swallow but only to soften the grain.
- Rabbi Yitzchak Barda (a son-in-law of Rabbi Matzliach Mazuz) notes that the
  Rema (*Orach Chaim* 567:3, following the Tur) permits *lo'es* of cinnamon
  sticks and other unpulverized spices on a fast day other than Yom Kippur, to
  wet the throat, provided one spits it out, while his source, the Ravyah
  (*Hilchos Taanis* 861), writes *lachus*; he explains *kesisah* as the chewing
  with more enjoyment of the spices' sweet juices than *le'isah* implies. Rabbi
  Klein glosses that as *kesisah* possibly connoting forceful or even indulgent
  mastication and *le'isah* leaning toward a more utilitarian chewing, but that
  gloss is his own paraphrase of Rabbi Barda.

## Rendering guidance

- Default both to "chew." Rashi's own gloss makes any forced English
  distinction misleading in a plain translation.
- Trap: "chew" absorbs both, and "munch" will pull *koses* toward the snack
  sense of *kisnin*. Where a sugya turns on the word choice (Rema against the
  Ravyah at *Orach Chaim* 567:3; *Arim Nissi* on Pesachim 39b), do not
  translate at all; keep both transliterated and set the dispute out.
- *Koses* in the berachos sugya is "chews [it raw]"; supply the bracket, since
  the whole halachah rests on the food being eaten in its unprocessed state.
- *Pas haba'ah b'kisnin* stays transliterated. Radak's "treats one munches at a
  celebration" is a gloss for a note, not a rendering.
- Bamidbar 11:33, *bein shineihem*, is "between their teeth," not "while they
  were still chewing"; the Torah has no verb for chewing and the phrase should
  not acquire one.
- *Maaleh geirah* (Vayikra 11:3-7, Devarim 14:6-8) is "brings up its cud," not
  "chews its cud," wherever the literal wording is load-bearing.
