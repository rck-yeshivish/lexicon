# Artisan: charash / choshev / rokem

## Bottom line

The Torah's three words for Betzalel's and Oholiav's skills (Shemos 35:35,
38:23) are not synonyms. *Charash* is the **craftsman** who reshapes raw
material, wood, stone or metal. *Choshev* is the **designer**, who plans and
combines finished materials into a figured work, hence *maaseh choshev* brocade.
*Rokem* is the **embroiderer**, working in colors. The trap: both are
"embroidery" in English, and the Gemara (Yoma 72b) distinguishes them by how many
faces the work shows, which no single English word carries. Keep both
transliterated in any Mishkan passage.

## Distinctions

### choshev (חושב) and rokem (רוקם)
Yoma 72b: both are embroidery, but *choshev* produces two faces and *rokem* one.
What that means is disputed.

- Rashi (Yoma 72b, and to Shemos 26:1, 26:31): *choshev* is weaving one image (a
  lion) into one fabric and another (an eagle) into a second, then plying the two
  so each side shows a different figure. *Rokem* is the harder skill, one
  symmetrical image in a single fabric reading the same from either side.
- Rambam (Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 8:15) and Abarbanel (to Shemos 26:1) reverse it:
  *rokem* embroiders on one face only (the other left blank, per Rabbi Chaim
  Kanievsky in *Derech Chochmah*), and *choshev* is what Rashi called *rokem*.

Neither the Gemara nor these commentators say what *charash* means here.

Shadal reads the two words developmentally: *rokem* first meant a primitive
patchwork done without a needle, drawing shapes on one cloth and affixing it to
another, and he ties ר-ק-ם by metathesis to ק-ר-ם, *krum*, a membrane overlaying
what is beneath. Later technique let one stitch the picture into the cloth
itself, but the craftsman kept the old name; later still came the ability to
form shapes within the weaving, work demanding real planning, and that craftsman
is a *choshev*, cognate with *machshavah*. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 26:1) runs
parallel, linking ר-ק-ם to ר-ג-ם (piling stones on a man), the embroidered
foreground appearing piled on its background.

Radak (to Shoftim 5:30, Yechezkel 17:3, and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) defines
*rokem* simply as one who weaves or sews a multicolored textile. Ibn Janach
named the first half of his *Sefer HaDikduk* *Sefer HaRikmah* for that reason,
alluding to *rikmos ha'adamah*, gardens of many species planted together, and so
to the *rokem* who makes fabrics of many colors.

### charash (חרש)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, cited in part by Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
(*HaKesav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 35:35): the root ח-ר-ש means plowing, the
preparatory labor of agriculture, never the farmer's object in itself. Because
plowing mattered so much, the root was extended to any craft of preparing
materials: the metalworker (Bereishis 4:22, Yeshayahu 44:12, Ezra 3:7), the
woodworker (Shemos 31:5, 35:33, Yeshayahu 44:13, Melachim II 12:12, Divrei
HaYamim I 22:15), the stoneworker (Shemos 28:11, Shmuel II 5:11). And because
such work requires planning, the same root came to mean thinking and scheming
(Shoftim 16:2, Shmuel I 23:9, Mishlei 3:29, Yeshayahu 3:3, Iyov 4:8).

Pappenheim's own line between the two: the *charash* changes materials out of
their natural state, chiseling a stone, cutting a beam, melting a metal; the
*choshev* takes materials as they are and arranges them, joining dye to cloth,
laying fabrics together to make a picture. Because he changes nothing in the
material and works only by arrangement, his name comes from thought, the more
abstract creativity. Radak and Ibn Janach in their *Sifrei HaShorashim* file
*choshev* under ח-ש-ב with *machshavah*, *cheshbon* and *chashuv*; Pappenheim
adds that the *choshev* must know his dimensions and quantities, hence
*cheshbon*.

Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehudah*) links *charash* to
*cheiresh*, mute, since this artisan works in silence, his craft demanding
thought.

### uman (אומן)
Targum Onkelos gives all three separate Aramaic words: *charash* becomes *uman*,
*choshev* becomes *nagar*, *rokem* becomes *tzayar*. *Uman* is not in Tanach but
is common in Mishnah (Bikkurim 3:3, Pesachim 4:6, Kiddushin 4:14, Sanhedrin 3:4,
Erachin 6:3), and its relative *aman* appears once (Shir HaShirim 7:2).
*Machberes Menachem* takes *uman* as any craftsman; Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
implies that only an expert earns the title.

### chacham charashim (חכם חרשים)
Yeshayahu 3:3, among the pillars Yerushalayim would lose; *hecharash v'hamasger*
went into galus with Yechonyah (Melachim II 24:14, 24:16, Yirmiyahu 29:2). Four
readings:

- Rashi (following Gittin 88a and Sanhedrin 38a): a talmid chacham so masterful
  that when he opens his mouth in Torah the rest are like a *cheiresh*, with
  nothing to answer.
- Malbim: from plowing. The chacham furrows through a body of knowledge to reach
  its depths, so "a wise man among plowers."
- Rabbi Yosef Kara, Ibn Ezra and the Vilna Gaon: a wise artisan, one who can
  support himself by his own handiwork.
- Radak and *Metzudas David*: from thought and planning, a master craftsman
  under whom apprentices learn.

A separate Aramaic sense: *charash* is Targum's regular word for one who
practices *kishuf* (Onkelos to Shemos 22:17), and the Gemara uses it that way too
(Rashi to Berachos 62a, Shabbos 75a, Bava Metzia 29b, Chullin 84b). Rabbi Shalom
Shabazi ties this to the mute sense, since spells were cast in a whisper.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 35:35 and 38:23: "craftsman, designer and embroiderer" is the safe set,
  but in a Mishkan passage keep *maaseh choshev* and *maaseh rokem*
  transliterated with a gloss. Rashi and the Rambam disagree about which is
  which, so an English word commits the translation to one side silently.
- Trap: "embroiderer" absorbs *choshev* and *rokem* together, and "craftsman"
  absorbs *charash* and *uman*. Where the Torah lists all three, three distinct
  English words are required.
- *Charash* in a navi's list of exiles (Melachim II 24:14): "craftsmen," not
  "smiths," since the word covers wood, stone and metal alike.
- *Chacham charashim* (Yeshayahu 3:3): render it and footnote the machlokes.
  "Skilled artisan" follows Ibn Ezra and the Gra; "master craftsman" follows
  Radak; "one wise in Torah" follows Rashi and the Gemara. Do not blend them.
- Aramaic *charash* as sorcerer must never be rendered "craftsman"; when both
  senses are in play in one sugya, transliterate.
