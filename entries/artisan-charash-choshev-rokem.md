# Artisan: charash / choshev / rokem

## Bottom line

The Torah's three words for Betzalel's and Oholiav's skills (Shemos 35:35,
38:23) are not synonyms. *Charash* is the **craftsman** who reshapes raw
material, wood, stone or metal. *Choshev* is the **designer**, who plans and
combines finished materials into a figured work, hence *maaseh choshev* brocade.
*Rokem* is the **embroiderer**, working in colors. Both are embroidery, and the
Gemara (Yoma 72b) distinguishes them by how many faces the work shows.

## Distinctions

### choshev (חושב) and rokem (רוקם)
Yoma 72b, in a passage Rabbi Klein calls somewhat cryptic: both are embroidery,
but *choshev* is embroidery by weaving, which produces two faces, and *rokem* is
embroidery by stitching, which produces one. What that means is disputed.

- Rashi (Yoma 72b, and to Shemos 26:1, 26:31): *choshev* is weaving one image (a
  lion) into one fabric and another (an eagle) into a second, then plying the two
  so each side shows a different figure. *Rokem* is the harder skill, one
  symmetrical image in a single fabric reading the same from either side.
- Rambam (Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 8:15) and Abarbanel (to Shemos 26:1) order it
  differently: *rokem* embroiders on one face only (the other left blank, per
  Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky in *Derech Chochmah*), and *choshev* is what Rashi
  called *rokem*.

Neither the Gemara nor these commentators say what *charash* means here.

Shadal reads the two words developmentally: *rokem* first meant a primitive
patchwork done without a needle, drawing shapes on one cloth and somehow
affixing that cloth to
another, and he ties ר-ק-ם by metathesis to ק-ר-ם, *krum*, a membrane overlaying
what is beneath. Later technique let one stitch the picture into the cloth
itself, but the craftsman kept the old name; later still came the ability to
form shapes within the weaving, work demanding real planning, and that craftsman
is a *choshev*, cognate with *machshavah*. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 26:1) runs
parallel, linking ר-ק-ם to ר-ג-ם by the interchange of kuf and gimmel: just as
*regem* is stoning a man by piling stones on him, so the *rokem*'s embroidered
foreground appears piled on its background.

Radak (to Shoftim 5:30, Yechezkel 17:3, and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) defines
*rokem* simply as one who weaves or sews a multicolored textile. Ibn Janach
divided his *Sefer HaDikduk*, one of the first studies of the Hebrew language,
into two parts, *Sefer HaRikmah* and *Sefer HaShorashim*; in his introduction he
explains that he named the first part for the wide range of subjects in it,
alluding to *rikmos ha'adamah*, botanical gardens in which many species were
planted together, and so back to the biblical *rokem*, one who produces fabrics
of many colors. (Rabbi Klein notes that he has been unable to find the phrase
*rikmos ha'adamah* anywhere but that introduction, and that Ibn Janach's work
was written in Judeo-Arabic and only later translated into Hebrew.)

### charash (חרש)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, cited in part by Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
(*HaKesav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 35:35): the root ח-ר-ש means plowing, the
preparatory labor of almost any agricultural endeavor, never the farmer's object
in itself. Because plowing mattered so much, the root was extended to other
crafts of preparing materials: the metalworker (Bereishis 4:22, Yeshayahu 44:12, Ezra 3:7, Divrei
HaYamim II 24:12), the
woodworker (Shemos 31:5, 35:33, Yeshayahu 44:13, Shmuel II 5:11, Melachim II
12:12, Divrei
HaYamim I 22:15), the stoneworker (Shemos 28:11, 31:5, 35:33, Shmuel II 5:11).
And because
such work requires planning, the same root came to mean thinking and scheming
(Shoftim 16:2, Shmuel I 23:9, Mishlei 3:29, Yeshayahu 3:3, Iyov 4:8).

Pappenheim's own line between the two: the *charash* changes materials out of
their natural state, chiseling a stone, cutting a beam, melting a metal; the
*choshev* takes materials as they are and arranges them, joining dye to cloth,
laying fabrics together to make a picture. On that basis Pappenheim suggests
that perhaps, since the *choshev* effects no change in the material itself and
works only by arrangement, his name comes from thought, the more
abstract creativity. Radak and Ibn Janach in their *Sifrei HaShorashim* file
*choshev* under ח-ש-ב with *machshavah*, *cheshbon* and *chashuv* (Modern
Hebrew carried this on in *machshevon*, a calculator,
*machshev*, a computer, and *cheshbonis*, an invoice); Pappenheim
adds that the *choshev* must know his dimensions and quantities, hence
*cheshbon*.

Menachem Ibn Saruk identifies five distinct meanings of the root ח-ר-ש:
muteness, deafness, thinking, artisanship and plowing.

Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehudah*) links *charash* to
*cheiresh*, mute, since this artisan generally works in silence, his craft
typically demanding much thought.

### uman (אומן)
Targum Onkelos gives all three separate Aramaic words: *charash* becomes *uman*,
*choshev* becomes *nagar* (a carpenter in Modern Hebrew), *rokem* becomes
*tzayar* (a painter in Modern Hebrew). None of the three is in Tanach, but
*uman* is common in Mishnah (Bikkurim 3:3, Pesachim 4:6, Kiddushin 4:14,
Sanhedrin 3:4, Avodah Zarah 5:7,
Erachin 6:3, Taharos 7:3), and its close relative *aman* appears once (Shir
HaShirim 7:2).
*Machberes Menachem* takes *uman* as any craftsman; Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
implies that only an expert earns the title. In the Gemara an *uman* can be a
bloodletter: Rashi (to Bava Basra 21a) explains the *uman* whom a Torah scholar's
city must have as a bloodletter or barber, and the word means a bloodletter in
Taanis 21b as well; elsewhere *umana* clearly refers to a ritual circumciser
(Shabbos 133b).

### chacham charashim (חכם חרשים)
Yeshayahu 3:3, among the pillars Yerushalayim would lose; *hecharash v'hamasger*
went into galus with Yechonyah (Melachim II 24:14, 24:16, Yirmiyahu 29:2). Four
readings:

- Rashi (following Gittin 88a and Sanhedrin 38a): a talmid chacham so masterful
  that when he opens his mouth in Torah the rest are like a *cheiresh*, with
  nothing to answer.
- Malbim: from plowing. The chacham furrows through a body of knowledge to reach
  its depths, so "a wise man among plowers."
- Rabbi Yosef Kara, Ibn Ezra and the Vilna Gaon: a wise artisan, one who can
  support himself by his own handiwork.
- Radak and *Metzudas David*: from thought and planning, so literally "a wise
  man among thinkers," a master craftsman under whom beginner apprentices might
  study their craft.

A separate Aramaic sense: *charash* is Targum's regular word for one who
practices *kishuf* (Onkelos to Shemos 22:17 on *michasheifah*), and the Gemara
uses it that way too (Rashi to Berachos 62a, Shabbos 75a, Pesachim 110a, Bava
Metzia 29b, Chullin 84b).
- The Yemenite poet Rabbi Shalom Shabazi ties this to the mute sense, since
  spells were often cast in a whisper.
- Dr. Alexander Kohut, in *He'Aruch HaShaleim*, cites the Maskilic scholar
  Peretz Smolenskin as tying it instead to the thinking and deliberating sense
  of the root, since the dark arts were typically performed in a state of
  deliberate intent.
- Rabbi Klein adds an alternative of his own: it may relate to the artisan
  sense, since for whatever reason the default artisan in Aramaic is one engaged
  in the occult, which he suggests may connect to the idea that Aramaic is a
  language associated with the *Sitra Achra*.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 35:35 and 38:23: "craftsman, designer and embroiderer" is the safe set,
  but in a Mishkan passage keep *maaseh choshev* and *maaseh rokem*
  transliterated with a gloss. Rashi and the Rambam disagree about which is
  which, so an English word commits the translation to one side silently.
- Trap: "embroiderer" absorbs *choshev* and *rokem* together, and "craftsman"
  absorbs *charash* and *uman*. Where the Torah lists all three, three distinct
  English words are required.
- *Charash* in a navi's list of exiles (Melachim II 24:14): "craftsmen," not
  "smiths," since the word covers wood, stone and metal alike.
- *Chacham charashim* (Yeshayahu 3:3): render it and footnote the machlokes.
  "Skilled artisan" follows Ibn Ezra and the Gra; "master craftsman" follows
  Radak; "one wise in Torah" follows Rashi and the Gemara. Do not blend them.
- Aramaic *charash* as sorcerer must never be rendered "craftsman"; when both
  senses are in play in one sugya, transliterate.
