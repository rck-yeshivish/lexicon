# Husband: ish / baal

## Bottom line

Both mean **husband**, and Hoshea sets them against each other: "you will call
Me *ishi* and you will not call Me *baali* any longer" (Hoshea 2:18). Whether
the two differ in meaning at all is the first dispute. Radak, Abarbanel and
Maharsha say they do not, and that the pasuk is only about avoiding a word that
is also the name of the Canaanite idol. The rest of the authorities say they do.
Their readings differ in detail, but many of them point the same way: *baal* is
the **master** or **owner**, the one who acts and lords, while *ish* is the
husband of a mutual bond, the two as one flesh.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether the two words differ at all.**

1. **They are synonyms, and the pasuk is about the idol.** Radak, Abarbanel and
   Maharsha: after the exile the Jews will repent so completely that they will
   not use *baali* of Hashem at all, because they will want distance from the
   homonymous Baal, chief of the Canaanite pantheon, whom the prophets laboured
   to break them of. So they will use its synonym *ishi* instead, and the shift
   is lexical. The Yerushalmi (*Avodah Zarah* 3:6) that the Baal idol was shaped
   like the male member, and Rav Hirsch (to Bamidbar 23:13) that *baal* is kin
   to the Greek *phallus* through the interchange of beis and feh, give the
   avoidance a thematic edge: the Jews will not use the word even in its plain
   sense of husband.
2. **They differ, and the pasuk is about the relationship.** Everyone below.

**How they differ, among those who say they do.** The readings converge but are
not the same.

- **Ownership against partnership.** Rabbi Yochanan (*Kesubos* 71b) has the
  Jews relating to Hashem "like a bride in her father-in-law's home, not like a
  bride in her father's home." Rashi supplies each half in a different place:
  to the Gemara there, that *ishi* implies conjugality and marriage; to Hoshea,
  that *baali* implies lording and awe. Maharsha puts the two together, and
  Rashi's first edition, quoted in the *Shittah Mekubetzes* to *Kesubos* 71b,
  shows that this was Rashi's intent. So *baali* is bare ownership, of the sort
  by which the owner of an ox is the *baal hashor* (Shemos 21:28), and answers
  to the betrothed bride still in her father's house, acquired but with no
  reciprocity; *ishi* is intimate familiarity and a two-way bond, the bride
  already living in her husband's family's home, neither partner lording over
  the other. Malbim (to Hoshea 2:18) reads it much the same way.
- **Covenant against informality.** Rabbi Yosef Dov Soloveitchik (1820 to
  1892): *ishi* is a formal union of man and wife that expects fidelity, while
  *baali* is a pair in an informal relationship with no covenantal expectation
  of monogamy, which is how the idolatrous Jews of Hoshea's day understood
  their tie to Hashem and how they left themselves free to serve other gods
  beside Him.
- **Acquisition against one flesh.** Rabbi Yitzchak Isaac Chaver (1789 to
  1853) holds that even *baali* carries an expectation of loyalty. The
  difference is that *baali* says only that the groom has acquired the bride so
  that she can no longer be wed to another, while *ishi* says the two have
  joined to become one flesh, which is Adam's point in naming her *ishah*, "for
  this was taken from *ish*" (Bereishis 2:23).
- **Union against the act.** Maharal (*Netzach Yisrael* ch. 47): *ish* implies
  a united connection between the spouses, and the letters bear it, since the
  yud of *ish* and the hei of *ishah* are the first two letters of the Sheim.
  *Baal* he derives from *bo'el*, the man as the active party in the act, the
  woman passive, and that imbalance is what Hoshea's vision anticipates
  correcting.
- **Provider against beloved.** The Vilna Gaon (to Devarim 21:13): Hashem
  bestows His abundance for two reasons, as benevolent Master of the Universe
  and because He loves Yisrael as a man loves his wife. Under *baal* they served
  Him for the influx of good He bestows, which is close to the idolatrous idea
  of a god as a mechanical source of benefits owed no relationship; under *ish*
  they will relate to Him as the One who loves them. Elsewhere (to Hoshea 2:18)
  he derives *baal* from *be'ilah* and so reads it as a way of approaching
  marriage fixed on its sensual pleasures. See also his comments to Bereishis
  2:23, Shir HaShirim 1:17, and *Tikkunei Zohar*, *Tikkun* 30.
- **Initiating against sharing.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 20:3, Tehillim 28:4),
  on the interchange of beis and peh, makes *baal* kin to *paal*, action: an
  action is how a man controls a situation to reach his object, and *baal* is
  accordingly the one who initiates, lording over a subdued vassal, against the
  partner who only receives. Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu
  Foundation adds that the same is hinted in *bala*, swallowing, whose root
  beis-lamed-ayin is a metathesis of *baal*, beis-ayin-lamed, which catches the
  aggressive side of a nature that would acquire possessions and people alike by
  swallowing them up. Rav Hirsch reads Hoshea from the other side: under *baali*
  the Jews saw themselves humbled and downtrodden before Him, and afterwards
  they regain their confidence and walk in His path as partners, unsubjugated,
  which is *ishi*.
- **Already happened, not still coming.** Rabbi Meir Emden (1717 to 1798), son
  of Rabbi Yaakov Emden (1698 to 1776), does not read the vision as future at
  all. At Sinai Hashem held the mountain over them like a barrel (*Shabbos*
  88a), so that their acceptance was not free of duress, and that is *baali*, a
  coercive owner who forced the Torah on them. In the days of Achashverosh they
  repented and took the Torah on willingly, "the Jews fulfilled and accepted
  upon themselves" (Esther 9:27), and from then on He was their loving *ish*.

## Distinctions

### ish (איש)

The husband of a mutual bond. Rashi's word for it at *Kesubos* 71b is
conjugality and marriage; Rabbi Yitzchak Isaac Chaver's is one flesh; Maharal's
is a united connection, with the yud and the hei that separate *ish* from
*ishah* spelling the first two letters of the Sheim. What the readings share is
that neither party stands over the other.

### baal (בעל)

The husband as master. The plain evidence is that the word is not reserved to
marriage: the owner of an ox is the *baal hashor* (Shemos 21:28), so *baali* can
say no more than that she has been acquired. Three separate derivations push the
same way. Maharal and the Vilna Gaon take it from *bo'el* and *be'ilah*, the act
in which the man is the active party. Rav Hirsch takes it from *paal*, action,
on the interchange of beis and peh, so that the *baal* is the initiator over one
subdued. And Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg finds the note again in *bala*,
swallowing, a metathesis of the same letters. Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim
LaMikra*) adds a fourth: *baal* is short for *ba al*, "comes upon."

Set against that, the word is also the name of the Canaanite idol, which on the
first view above is the whole of what Hoshea is saying.

## Rendering guidance

Both words say "husband," and a passage that turns on the contrast, Hoshea 2:18
above all, cannot be carried by "husband" twice. Where the two stand together,
*ish* takes "husband" and *baal* wants a word that carries mastery: "master" or
"owner" where the sense is ownership, which is what Maharsha's *baal hashor*
supports. Where the essay's first view is being followed, though, *baali* and
*ishi* are synonyms and the point is only the idol's name, so a rendering that
loads *baal* with domination takes a side in a live dispute.

Outside marriage *baal* is regularly just the owner of a thing, and the trap
runs the other way: "husband" is wrong wherever the *baal* is the owner of an
ox, a field or a pit.
