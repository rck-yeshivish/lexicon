# Hard: kashah / kashiach / perech

## Bottom line

*Kashah* is the general word, and covers both English senses of hard: **hard**
as in rigid and unyielding, and **hard** as in difficult. *Kashiach* is not
hardness of substance at all but cruelty (Ibn Ezra, Radak), for Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Melech specifically toward those one should love: **cruel, callous**. *Perech* literally breaks, so *avodas perech* is
**back-breaking** labor, or, on a second line of reading, **pointless** labor,
work with no end and no product. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (*Ohel Moed*) lists the
three as the Hebrew words for hard.

## Distinctions

### kashah (קשה)
Both senses at once, physical rigidity and difficulty; it is the word for the
"hard" work of Mitzrayim at Shemos 1:14 and 6:9.

- Shemos 7:3, that Hashem will harden (*akasheh*) Pharaoh's heart, is read by
  most commentators in the first sense, Pharaoh empowered to go on refusing and
  left unbudged by the makkos.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg prefers the second: Hashem conveyed to Moshe
  that He would put Pharaoh through a particularly traumatic ordeal, hard for
  his heart to endure.
- Rav Hirsch, on the interchange of kuf with yud and with gimmel, links *kashah*
  to *yesh*, existence, since to us the more solid a thing is the more real it
  seems, and to *gishah/gashash*, approaching and impacting, since only what is
  solid is felt to make contact. Rabbi Klein applies this to the Menorah of
  *mikshah* (Shemos 25:31, 37:22, Bamidbar 8:4), beaten out of a single block,
  which Rashi (to Shemos 25:31) says was hammered into shape: harder than cast
  metal, and made by the kind of impact Rav Hirsch describes.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives from the two-letter core ק-ש a family of hard
  things: *kash*, the stiff lower part of the stalk not even fit for fodder;
  *kishu*, the gourd with its hard rind; *keshes*, the unbending part of the
  archer's weapon; and *mokesh*, a trap made of something hard rather than a
  pliant net, and hard to escape.

In the language of Chazal, *hekesh* is the derashah that compares two areas of
halachah juxtaposed in one pasuk, that is, the impact or collision of two realms
in a single verse. The Arabic term *qiyas* names the
process in Islamic law of deducing new rulings by analogy to existing ones, and
in medieval Hebrew, under the influence of the Ibn Tibbon family who
translated Judeo-Arabic works into Hebrew, *hekesh* took on that meaning too and
came to mean any deductive analogy or syllogism. A *kushya* is a logical
difficulty, the clash between a statement and what reason would say, and by a
tradition from the Geonim leaving a matter as *kashya* does not mean the position
is refuted, only that the difficulty stands and more study is needed. A *kushya*
is not a *she'eilah*: the first tries to undo an assertion, the second only asks
for information.

### kashiach (קשיח)
Twice in Tanach: the ostrich and stork, which lay eggs and may then callously
abandon them to be trampled, the mother bird who "hardens herself against her
young as though they were not hers" (Iyov 39:16); and Yeshayahu 63:17, why do
You harden our hearts from fearing You.

- Ibn Ezra (to Iyov 39:16) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) define it as cruelty.
- Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Melech (*Michlal Yofi* to Yeshayahu 63:17) adds that it
  specifically denotes cruelty in the form of estrangement from one whom a
  person would otherwise have been expected to love. That approach supports the
  notion that the word does not share a grammatical root with *kashah*.
- *Metzudas Tzion* (to Yeshayahu 63:17 and Iyov 39:16) does connect them,
  reading *kashiach* as a portmanteau of *kashah* and *sach*, remove: one is
  harsh with another and then coldheartedly removes the harshness from his own
  thoughts, as in *hesech hada'as*, as though nothing out of the ordinary had
  happened.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's list leaves *kashiach* out, since a third radical
  ches does not fit his system, which admits only hei, aleph, mem, nun, tav,
  yud, or vav as the added letter.

(In Modern Hebrew *disk kashiach* is a calque on the English "hard drive,"
rendering "hard" by *kashiach*, which of course has nothing to do with cruelty
or estrangement.)

### perech (פרך)
Literally to break. A lung so dry that it is brittle is *nifreches* and the
animal is treifah (*Chullin* 46b); a lulav dry enough to be brittle is likewise
*nifreches* and pasul (Tosafos to *Sukkah* 29b); a *pircha* in the give and take
of the Gemara is the question that breaks a chain of reasoning.

- Rashi (to Shemos 1:13): *avodas parech* is back-breaking labor.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 1:13 and 26:31) takes the breaking as separating: the
  Egyptians drafted Yisrael as laborers in order to break the homogeneity of
  Egyptian society and create a class difference between themselves and the
  Jews, and he ties *perech* to *paroches*, the curtain separating the Kodesh
  from the Kodesh HaKodashim.
- A second line reads *perech* as uselessness, what crumbles to waste and serves
  no purpose: *Toras Kohanim* (to Vayikra 25:43) defines the forbidden *perech*
  labor as work with no limit or work that is needless, and Rabbi Saadia Gaon
  explains *avodas parech* as the pointless labor Egypt imposed.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim joins the two: the core sense is breaking a routine by
  interrupting it to begin again. The *paroches* announces that up to here is
  holy and from here on holier. *Perech* labor is the unfair practice of pulling
  a worker off one job to start another, so that he never has the satisfaction
  of finishing and feels all his work crumbling to nothing. That is what the
  Torah forbids with a Jewish bondsman and what Mitzrayim did to Yisrael.

The Haggadah defines *avodah kashah* as *perech*, and Onkelos (to Shemos 1:13-14,
Vayikra 25:53) renders *perech* with the Aramaic cognate of *kashah*, *kashyu*.
Rabbi Klein takes this to suggest that the two terms are more or less
equivalent.

## Rendering guidance

- *Kashah* of an object: "hard," "rigid." Of a task or a decree: "harsh" or
  "difficult." Of Pharaoh's heart: "harden" is standard, but if Rabbi
  Mecklenburg's reading is being cited, render "make it hard for his heart to
  bear" and say so.
- *Kashiach*: "callous" or "cruel," never "hard." Iyov 39:16 reads "she deals
  callously with her young"; "hardens herself" keeps the Hebrew image but hides
  the estrangement Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Melech puts in the word.
- *Avodas perech*: "back-breaking labor" (Rashi), or "crushing, pointless labor"
  (Rasag and *Toras Kohanim*). Where a passage cites both, give both, because the
  halachah in Vayikra 25:43 turns on which one it is.
- *Mikshah*: "hammered work," or "beaten from a single block," not "hard work."
- Trap: English "hard" will take *kashah* and *perech* together, and English
  "hard-hearted" will take *kashah* and *kashiach* together. Rabbi Klein reads
  the Haggadah's equation of *avodah kashah* with *perech*, and Onkelos's
  *kashyu*, as suggesting the two are roughly equivalent, and then works through
  their differences all the same; in translation keep them apart wherever the
  passage turns on the difference.
