# White: lavan / chivar / tzochar (chelbon)

## Bottom line

*Lavan* is the general Hebrew word: **white**, in any of its forms. *Chivar* is
the Aramaic equivalent, used by the Targumim wherever the Hebrew has *lavan*;
render it **white**, or **pale** where it describes a face. *Tzochar* is
contested, and a translator must not assume it: Rashi reads **white**, Ibn Janach
and Bedersi read **combed** or **pristine**, with whiteness only a side effect.
*Chelbon* (Yechezkel 27:18) is probably **white wine**, from *chalav*, but two
other readings compete. The trap: English "white" absorbs all four, and in
Shoftim 5:10 or Yechezkel 27:18 the whole question is whether the word means
white at all.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| לבן | lavan | white | the umbrella Hebrew term for every kind of white (Pappenheim); root of *leveinah*, *levanah*, *levonah* |
| חיור | chivar | white, pale | Aramaic; the Targumic rendering of *lavan* (Rashi treats them as identical); whiteness caused from outside (Pappenheim) |
| צחר | tzochar | white / combed | Rashi: white. Ibn Janach and Bedersi: combed, and only incidentally white |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether lavan and chivar differ at all.** Rashi consistently translates the
Talmudic Aramaic *chivar* back into Hebrew as *lavan* (Berachos 28a, Gittin 69b
and 73a, Bava Basra 73a, Avodah Zarah 28a, Chullin 55b), which reads as identity
of meaning across two languages rather than a synonym pair. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim, without saying he is distinguishing them, defines *chivar* narrowly:
whiteness that is not an object's own color but a property forced on it from
outside, above all by fire. On his account *lavan* is the umbrella and *chivar*
a species under it. The Bottom line keeps Rashi's practical equivalence for
translation and flags Pappenheim's narrowing where fire is in the picture.

**Whether tzochar means white.** Rashi (to Shoftim 5:10 and Yechezkel 27:18)
says yes, and reads Devorah's praise as praise of judges who kept riding
conspicuously white she-donkeys in wartime rather than hide. Rabbi Avraham
Bedersi in *Chotam Tochnit*, the first work on Hebrew synonyms, denies it,
preferring Ibn Janach: *tzochar* is "combed," and combed wool simply happens to
come out white; the donkeys were groomed against fleas, not necessarily white.
Several etymologies support Bedersi's direction. Radal ties *tzochar* to *tzach*
(clean, clear); Rabbi Benzion Chaim Lubetzky notes the metathesis of צ-ח-ר into
ר-ח-צ (*rechitzah*, washing), both suggestions also made independently by Rabbi
Dovberish Rosenberg of Zurich in *Davar Tov*. Rabbi Tedeschi and Rabbi Yehudah
Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehudah*) read it as a portmanteau of *tzach* and
*chivar*; Rabbi Yitzchak of Zeldin (*Shoresh Yesha*) as *tzach* plus *ohr*
(light). Berachos 31b, where Chanah asks that her son be neither too *tzachor*
nor too ruddy, supports "white" in the best texts of Rashi, and Sotah 12a
explains Miriam's name Tzochar from *tzahar*, noon, because her face shone.

**What chelbon wine is.** Rashi: white wine, from *chalav*, matching Rabbinic
*chelbon* for egg white. Menachem (and the Peshitta) read it as rich or oily,
from *cheilev*, a way of saying the wine was fine. Radak: a place-name in Aram,
which Rabbi Tedeschi identifies with Chalav (Aleppo). The Bottom line follows
Rashi, since the parallel with egg white is decisive in Rabbinic usage.

## Distinctions

### lavan (לבן)
Root ל-ב-נ for the classical lexicographers, which makes it kin to *leveinah*
(brick), *levanah* (the moon), *livneh* (white poplar or styrax), and *levonah*
(frankincense). Verb forms mean both "whiten" (Yeshayahu 1:18, Yoel 1:7,
Tehillim 51:9) and "clarify" (Daniel 11:35, 12:10), and most of its color
occurrences cluster in the laws of tzaraas (Vayikra 13). The name Lavan,
Yaakov's father-in-law, belongs here, as do Livni (Shemos 6:17, Bamidbar
3:18-21), Livnah in the Wilderness (Bamidbar 33:20-21, Devarim 1:1) and the
Canaanite Livnah near Lachish (Yehoshua 10:29-39), and Levanon, named for its
snow-capped peaks.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim breaks with the consensus. On his system the final nun
is not part of the core, so *lavan* goes back to ל-ב, the root of *lev* (heart,
and so "middle"), *lavi* (lioness, courageous of heart), *levivos* (wafers that
sustain the heart), *lahav* (flame, which pumps heat from a center as the heart
pumps blood), *leveinah* (a brick, fired in a kiln), and *malbein* (kiln). White
on this reading is the effect of fire, an object heated white-hot, which is why
Rabbinic Hebrew calls heating a metal *libbun*. Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim* and
Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 5:7) explain *lavan* along similar lines.

### chivar (חיור)
Root ח-ו-ר with the vav consonantal. In Tanach the Hebrew verb appears once, of
a face turning pale (Yeshayahu 29:22); the Aramaic adjective appears in *tlag
chivar*, white snow (Daniel 7:9). It is the Targumic standard for *lavan*, and
survives in *chamar chivaryan*, white wine, in *Pitum HaKetores*, and in the
Talmudic *michavarta* for the clearest, most "whitened" explanation. Rabbi
Pappenheim traces it to ח-ר, the root of *chor* (hole), because the fire that
whitens an object penetrates all its nooks.

Where the vav functions as a vowel the same root yields two more families.
*Salei chori*, the baker's baskets (Bereishis 40:16): Rabbi Saadia Gaon, endorsed
by Ibn Ezra and Ramban, reads white bread as against whole wheat; Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) reads white baskets, woven of peeled white bark; Rav Hirsch reads
baskets belonging to important people. And *chorim*, noblemen (Koheles 10:17,
Melachim I 21:8, Nechemiah 4:8), with the Mishnaic *ben chorin*, a freedman:
Rabbi Shmuel de Ucceda (*Midrash Shmuel* to Avos 6:2) cites an anonymous earlier
commentator connecting it to *chivar*, and Rabbi Pappenheim explains that only
the gentry were permitted sun-bleached white clothing, so the word for white came
to mean the important. *Chur* in Achashverosh's hangings (Esther 1:6, 8:15) is
read as luxurious white fabric by Ibn Ezra, Rid, Ibn Janach, Radak, Ralbag,
Rabbi Moshe Chalavah and Rabbi Yosef Nechemias (see Rashi to Megillah 12a).

Rabbi Moshe Yitzchak Tedeschi Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) links *chivar* to
*ohr*, light, on the interchange of ches and aleph; compare Rav Hirsch to
Bereishis 1:3.

### tzochar (צחר)
Root צ-ח-ר, twice in Tanach: Devorah's *asonos tzchoros* (Shoftim 5:10) and
Yechezkel's *tzachar* wool from Damascus (Yechezkel 27:18). Proper names carry
it further: Tzochar father of Efron (Bereishis 23:8), Tzochar son of Shimon
(Bereishis 46:10), and Tzochar as a byname for Miriam (Divrei HaYamim I 4:7),
which Sotah 12a explains from *tzaharayim*. Rashi (to Bamidbar 26:13, with
*Rashba* vol. 1 §12) identifies Tzochar son of Shimon with Zerach son of Shimon,
reading *tzochar* as *tzohar* (bright) on the interchange of ches and hei, which
matches *zerach*, shining.

### chelbon (חלבון) and chalav (חלב)
Yaakov's blessing to Yehudah, whiteness of teeth from *chalav* (Bereishis
49:12), is the article's starting point. Rabbi Matisyahu Strashun and Rabbi
Binyamin Rabinowitz-Teomim read the pair *yayin* and *chalav* there not as wine
and milk but as red wine and white wine, with support from Targum to Shir
HaShirim 5:1, which renders *chalav* there as white wine.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: one English "white" will absorb *lavan*, *chivar*, and *tzochar*. Where a
  passage or a commentary turns on which word is used, keep them apart: *lavan*
  plain "white," *chivar* "pale" or "blanched" when a face or an outside cause is
  in view, *tzochar* left with a note.
- In Shoftim 5:10 do not simply write "white donkeys" without knowing you have
  chosen Rashi over Bedersi. "Groomed she-donkeys" is the other reading.
- *Chivar* is Aramaic. In a bilingual sefer, do not present *lavan* and *chivar*
  as a Hebrew synonym pair; they are the same word in two languages (Rashi),
  with at most a narrowing in Pappenheim.
- *Libbun* in a halachic context is not "whitening" in the color sense but firing
  a vessel to glowing heat; the color word is doing metallurgical work.
