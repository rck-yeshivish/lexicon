# White: lavan / chivar / tzochar (chelbon)

## Bottom line

*Lavan* is the general Hebrew word: **white**, and for Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
it seems to be the umbrella for every manifestation of white. *Chivar* is the
Aramaic equivalent, the Targumim's standard rendering of *lavan*: **white**, or **pale** where it
describes a face. Rashi's glossing of *chivar*
as *lavan* implies, perhaps, that the two mean the same and differ only in
language, while Pappenheim narrows *chivar* to whiteness caused from outside.
*Tzochar* is contested: Rashi reads
**white**, while Bedersi, preferring Ibn Janach, reads **combed**, with
whiteness only a side effect. *Chelbon* wine (Yechezkel 27:18) is **white
wine** for Rashi, rich wine for Menachem and the Peshitta, a wine named for a
place in Aram for Radak, and "sweet wine" in the Targum as Rabbi Aharon Marcus
reads it. In Shoftim 5:10 and Yechezkel 27:18 the whole question is whether the
word means white at all.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether lavan and chivar differ at all.**

- In many of the places where the Talmud's Aramaic *chivar* appears, Rashi
  translates it back into Hebrew as *lavan* (Berachos 28a, Gittin 69b and 73a,
  Bava Basra 73a, Avodah Zarah 28a, Chullin 55b). Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein takes
  this to imply that perhaps Rashi understood the two to mean exactly the same
  thing, though they may not technically be synonyms because one is Hebrew and
  the other Aramaic.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, without saying he is distinguishing them, defines
  *chivar* as a whiteness that is not an object's natural color but a property
  caused by an outside force, as when a thing is whitened in fire. He grounds
  this in what he considered a scientific fact, that most objects are naturally
  white and other colors come from impurities, which a hot furnace melts away to
  leave the natural white; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein calls this quasi-scientific.
  On his account, it seems, *lavan* is the umbrella and *chivar* the whiteness
  of something whitened from outside.

**Whether tzochar means white.**

- Rashi (to Shoftim 5:10) says yes: Devorah praises those who rode white
  she-donkeys, carrying on during the war unafraid of being sighted by enemies
  on such conspicuous animals; and to Yechezkel 27:18 he again reads white wool,
  citing the Shoftim pasuk as proof.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi in *Chosam Tochnis*, the first work on Hebrew synonyms,
  disputes it and prefers Ibn Janach's view that *tzochar* primarily means
  "combed": combed wool simply happens to come out white and pristine. The
  donkeys, he explains, were combed against fleas, not necessarily white.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein finds this fits those etymologies that relate
  *tzochar* to "pristine" or "clean," which in his view shows the core meaning
  is not quite white. Those are Radal's (1798 to 1855) tie to *tzach*, clean,
  clear, pristine, and Rabbi Benzion Chaim Lubetzky's (d. 1945) rearrangement of
  צ-ח-ר into ר-ח-ץ, *rechitzah*, washing, both suggested independently by Rabbi
  Dovberish Rosenberg of Zurich in *Davar Tov*.
- Continuing from *tzach*, Rabbi Tedeschi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) and Rabbi Yehudah
  Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehuda*) read it as a portmanteau of *tzach* and
  *chivar*, white, and Rabbi Yitzchak of Zeldin (*Shoresh Yesha*) as *tzach*
  plus *ohr*, light.
- Elsewhere, the best versions of Rashi to Berachos 31b, where Chanah asks that
  her son be neither too *tzachor* nor too ruddy, explain it as white, and Sotah
  12a explains Miriam's name Tzochar from *tzahar*, noon, because her face
  shone.

**What chelbon wine is.**
- Rashi: white wine, by its color, which lines up with Rabbinic *chelbon* for
  egg white; these readings seemingly take *chelbon* from *chalav*, both being
  white (and *chelbon* is seemingly the source of the name Chelbo borne by
  several sages).
- The Peshitta, and Rashi in the name of Menachem (*Machberes Menachem*), read
  it as fatty or oily, a way of saying the wine was of especially good quality,
  which seems to relate it to *cheilev*.
- Radak: a place-name, an area in Aram that produced quality wine, which Rabbi
  Tedeschi identifies as Chalab, also known as Aleppo or Aram Tzova. A
  tradition, cited by a twelfth-century rabbi, holds that Aleppo was called
  Chalab because Avraham grazed his livestock there and gave out milk to the
  poor.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus notes that Targum Yonasan renders *chelbon* wine *chamar
  cheilat*, sweet wine (see below).

## Distinctions

### lavan (לבן)
Root ל-ב-ן for all the classical lexicographers, which makes it kin to
*leveinah* (brick), *levanah* (the moon), *livneh* (white poplar or styrax), and
*levonah* (frankincense). *Leveinah* appears twelve times in Tanach, mostly in
Shemos of the bricks the enslaved Jews had to make, with a verb for preparing
bricks (Bereishis 11:3; Shemos 5:7, 5:14). Verb forms of *lavan* mean both
"whiten" (Yeshayahu 1:18, Yoel 1:7, Tehillim 51:9) and "elucidate" (Daniel
11:35, 12:10), and a majority of its color occurrences cluster in the laws of
tzaraas (Vayikra 13). The name Lavan, Yaakov's uncle and father-in-law, belongs
here, as does Livni son of Gershon (Shemos 6:17; Bamidbar 3:18-21, 26:58;
Divrei HaYamim I 6:2-14), possibly named after his great-great-grandfather
Lavan, and Levanah of the Nesinim (Ezra 2:45, Nechemiah 7:48). So do the
places Livnah in the Wilderness, also called Lavan (Bamidbar 33:20-21, Devarim
1:1), the Canaanite Livnah near Lachish (Yehoshua 10:29-39, 12:15), and
Levanon, especially the often snow-topped Mount Lebanon. Since Hebrew and Arabic
are both Semitic languages, Arabic *laban*, milk and
specifically sour milk, relates to *lavan* by its white color, and is the basis
of Yiddish and Modern Hebrew *leben*.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) takes a wildly different approach. In
his system two-letter cores take on hei, aleph, mem, nun, tav, yud or vav to make
three-letter roots, so the final nun of *lavan* is not part of the core, and it
goes back to ל-ב, the root of *lev*: heart, and so "middle"; *lavi*, the
lioness, courageous of heart; *levivos*, wafers that sustain the heart or are
shaped like one; *lahav*, flame, which pumps heat from a center as the heart
pumps blood; *leveinah*, a brick, fired in a kiln; and *malbein*, kiln. Many
objects placed in a hot oven become white-hot, so whiteness reflects the effects
of fire, which is why Rabbinic Hebrew calls heating a metal *libbun*. Radak in
*Sefer HaShorashim* and Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 5:7) explain *lavan* along
similar lines.

English *albino* is not related: it comes from Latin *albus*, white, by way of
Spanish or Portuguese, so its *n* is not part of the root; linguists trace
*albus* to the Proto-Indo-European *albho-* (also spelled *hele-bho*), whence
also *albumen*, *album* and perhaps Albania.

### chivar (חיור)
Root ח-ו-ר with the vav consonantal. In Tanach the Hebrew verb appears once, of
a face turning white (Yeshayahu 29:22), and in Modern Hebrew it means pale; more
broadly it just means white, as in the Aramaic *tlag chivar*, white snow (Daniel
7:9). It is the Targumic standard for *lavan*, and survives in *chamar
chivaryan*, white wine, in *Pitum HaKetores*, and in the Talmudic *michavarta*
for the "whitest," clearest, most logically sound explanation. Rabbi Pappenheim
traces it to ח-ר, the root of *chor* (hole), because the fire that whitens an
object penetrates all its nooks and crannies.

Where the vav functions as a vowel, several biblical words are still said to be
related. *Salei chori*, the baker's baskets (Bereishis 40:16): Rabbi Saadia
Gaon, in an explanation seemingly endorsed by Ibn Ezra, Ramban and Shadal
(there), reads white bread as against whole wheat; Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
similarly reads white baskets, woven of peeled white bark. And *chorim*,
dignitaries or noblemen (Koheles 10:17; Melachim I 21:8; Nechemiah 4:8, 4:13,
5:7, 7:5), with the Mishnaic *ben chorin*, a freedman (among many places, *Peah*
3:8, *Gittin* 4:4-6, *Avos* 2:16, 6:2): Rabbi Shmuel de Ucceda (1545 to 1604;
*Midrash Shmuel* to Avos 6:2) cites an anonymous earlier commentator connecting
it to *chivar*; Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 40:16) explains *salei chori* as
baskets of important people; and Rabbi Pappenheim posits that in ancient times
only the gentry were allowed sun-bleached white clothing, so the word for white
came to mean the important. *Chur* in Achashverosh's hangings (Esther 1:6,
8:15) is read by many medieval commentators as luxurious white fabric related to
*chivar*: Ibn Ezra, the Rid, Ibn Janach, Radak, Ralbag, Rabbi Moshe Chalavah and
Rabbi Yosef Nechemias (see also Rashi to Megillah 12a). People named Chur
include Moshe's nephew, grandfather of Betzalel (Shemos 17:10-12, 24:14, 31:2,
35:30, 38:22; Divrei HaYamim II 1:5), and a Midianite chieftain (Bamidbar 31:8,
Yehoshua 13:21).

The Italian scholar Rabbi Moshe Yitzchak Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (1821 to 1898;
*Otzar Nirdafim*) links *chivar* to *ohr*, light, on the interchange of ches
and aleph; compare Rav Hirsch to Bereishis 1:3. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that since white
is what an object shows when it absorbs none of the colors of visible light, the
link between white and light becomes more understandable.

### tzochar (צחר)
Root צ-ח-ר, twice in Tanach: Devorah's *asonos tzchoros* (Shoftim 5:10) and
Yechezkel's *tzachar* wool from Damascus (Yechezkel 27:18). Proper names seem to
carry it further: Tzochar father of Efron (Bereishis 23:8-9), Tzochar son of
Shimon (Bereishis 46:10, Shemos 6:15), and Tzochar as a byname for Miriam
(Divrei HaYamim I 4:7), which Sotah 12a explains from *tzaharayim*. In other
lists of Shimon's sons Zerach appears in place of Tzochar (Bamidbar 26:13,
Divrei HaYamim I 4:24), and Rashi (to Bamidbar 26:13; see also responsa
*Rashba* vol. 1 §12) identifies the two, the names being close in meaning:
he defines *tzochar* as *tzohar*, bright, presumably on the interchange of ches
and hei, which matches *zerach*, shining. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds, as his own
suggestion with no source he has seen, that this Tzochar may have been named
for his great-grandfather Lavan, both names meaning white.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 32:25) connects *shachar*
with *tzachar*, shin and tzadi interchanging.

Another approach relates *tzachar* to red. David J. A. Clines' *A Dictionary of
Classical Hebrew* defines it as "reddish-grey colour"; Rabbi Ernest Klein writes
that the root צ-ח-ר is related to its Arabic cognate *asharu*, "yellowish-reddish
land," the etymon of the name Sahara; a version of Rashi to Berachos 31b, as
printed in the Vilna Shas, says *tzachor* means "red"; and Marcus Jastrow's
dictionary defines *tzachor* as "glistening" and "light reddish."

### chelbon (חלבון) and chalav (חלב)
Yaakov's blessing to Yehudah speaks of whiteness of teeth from *chalav* (Bereishis
49:12). Rabbi Matisyahu Strashun (1817 to 1885)
and Rabbi Binyamin Rabinowitz-Teomim argue that the pair *yayin* and *chalav*
there means not wine and milk but red wine and white wine; see also Targum to
Shir HaShirim 5:1, which likewise renders *chalav* there as white wine.

Cognates of *chalav* appear across the Semitic languages: Aramaic *chalba*,
Arabic *halab*, Ethiopic *halib*, Ugaritic *hlb*, Akkadian *halabu*. Rabbi
Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) suggests that the original word for "white" in all
languages is *chalav*, claiming even a Celtic cognate, and connects Latin
*albus* to *chalav*, the ches softening toward an aleph; so, he explains, the
Alps are named for being white like milk, as is the river Elbe (Latin
*Albis*), though this is nowadays considered something of a
folk etymology. He is unsure whether *chalav* meant milk first and then white,
or white first and then milk. He also considers deriving *chalav* from Aramaic
*chali*, sweet, with a beis added, noting that Targum Yonasan to Yechezkel 27:18
renders *chelbon* wine *chamar cheilat*, sweet wine. On his account *albino*
does after all relate to Hebrew, but to *chalav*, not *lavan*.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: one English "white" will absorb *lavan*, *chivar*, and *tzochar*. Where a
  passage or a commentary turns on which word is used, keep them apart: *lavan*
  plain "white," *chivar* "pale" or "blanched" when a face or an outside cause is
  in view, *tzochar* left with a note.
- In Shoftim 5:10 write "white donkeys" only knowing you have chosen Rashi over Bedersi. "Groomed she-donkeys" is the other reading.
- *Chivar* is Aramaic. Do not present *lavan* and *chivar*
  as a Hebrew synonym pair; on Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's reading of Rashi they are perhaps
  the same word in two languages, with at most a narrowing in Pappenheim.
- *Libbun* in a halachic context is not "whitening" in the color sense but firing
  a vessel to glowing heat; the color word is doing metallurgical work.
