# Mountain: har / givah (pisgah)

## Bottom line

*Har* is the **mountain** and *givah* the **hill**, and what separates them is
disputed. The Vilna Gaon takes it as shape: a *har* rises to a point, a *givah*
is flat on top, a plateau. Malbim takes it as height and position: a *har* is
the tall one, *givaos* the ring of lower hills around it. Radak stands
substantially with the Vilna Gaon. *Pisgah* is the uncommon third word, a
**peak** higher than an ordinary mountain and so the place to look out from.
When Shir HaShirim sets the two side by side, *Chazal* read the pair as the two
sets of forebears, and the mystical reading that follows turns on the shape, not
on the height.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What divides a har from a givah.** Two readings, and they are not the same
question.

1. **Shape.** The Vilna Gaon (*Aderes Eliyahu* to Yeshayahu 2:2, Chavakuk 2:6,
   Shir HaShirim 2:8): a *har* is the classical mountain, its upward slope
   reaching a peak and climaxing at its tip, while a *givah* is raised ground
   whose top does not come to a point but stays flat, a mesa. Radak (to
   Tehillim 114:4) adopts much the same approach.
2. **Height and position.** Malbim (to Yeshayahu 2:2, and *Yair Ohr*): a *har*
   is an overly tall mountain, and *givaos* are a series of shorter hills that
   surround it.

The Bottom line keeps both. The mystical reading below is built on the first,
because it needs the point and the plateau; it does not settle the second.

## Distinctions

### har (הר)

The mountain that comes to a sharp point. In the mystical reading the *har*
carries the male, influential force, which gathers to a peak so as to
concentrate its energy on influencing what lies outside it, and the point of the
mountain is that concentration.

The word reaches into pregnancy: *vatahar*, "and she conceived," is
super-literally "and she became a *har*." On the plain level the connection is
obvious, but on the deeper level it needs an answer, since womankind is
otherwise tied to the *givah*. The answer given is a shift of role. Before
conception she is the recipient of what the male force bestows; after it, she
becomes the influencer with respect to the child she carries, and in taking on
the work of sustaining that child she is more a *har* than she has ever been.

Ibn Ezra (to Tehillim 148:9): mountains are called *har* because, as a pregnant
woman, *harah*, carries a child within, mountains hold resources such as metals
within them (see Devarim 8:9; Iyov 28:2).

Chazal liken *har* to *herayon*, pregnancy (*Bereishis Rabbah* 68:2).

Dr. Chaim Tawil: the Aramaic *tur*, mountain, is related to *tzurim* (Bamidbar
23:9), tzadi and tes interchanging.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Devarim 12:2): *har* is also related
to *horaah*, teaching, since a mountain is a landmark seen from afar that shows
the traveller his way. So *har* can stand for both parent and teacher, alluding
to Chazal's likening of one's parents to one's teachers.

### givah (גבעה)

Raised ground that stays flat on top. Here the female, receptive force: it
remains open and casts as wide a net as it can, to receive the greatest flow of
raw influence (see *Berachos* 61a), and the wide plateau rather than the small
point is what makes the *givah* fit to stand for it. Rabbi Eliyahu Margules ties
*givah* to *gaviah*, a goblet: as a goblet is a receptacle holding the drink
inside it, so the *givah* is the receiving one.

The receptive force also engulfs the influential one (see Yirmiyahu 31:21), as a
range of *givaos* surrounds the *har*, and this is why a *kallah* circles her
*chosson* seven times under the *chuppah*.

### pisgah (פסגה)

The third and least common word. The commentators explain that a *pisgah* is
taller even than a regular mountain, which is what makes it suited to serve as a
lookout point where a *har* or a *givah* would not.

## Rendering guidance

Where the two stand together, "mountain" and "hill" carry the pair, and the
order in the pasuk is *har* first. The trap is that English "hill" says only
"smaller," which is Malbim's reading and not the Vilna Gaon's: on his reading
the *givah* is distinguished by being flat-topped, and a rendering that leans on
size alone loses the shape the mystical reading needs. Where an entry has to
choose, "hill" for *givah* and "plateau" only where the flat top is the point.
*Pisgah* is a peak or a summit; where the pasuk has it as a vantage, "lookout"
carries the sense the commentators give it.

For Shir HaShirim 2:8, "skipping over the *harim* and jumping over the
*givaos*," *Rosh Hashanah* 11a reads the first of the merit of the *avos* and
the second of the merit of the *imahos*, which the mystical reading then ties to
the point and the plateau.
