# Pile: areimah / chamarim / gadish (omer)

## Bottom line

*Areimah* is the standard word for a **pile** or **heap**, in Tanach and in
Mishnaic Hebrew alike. *Chamarim* (doubled in Shemos 8:10, *chamarim chamarim*) is
**heaps upon heaps**, a mass gathered together. *Gadish* is a **stack** of grain
still in the sheaf; *omer* is the **bundle** or **sheaf** itself. Rambam seems
to equate *areimah* and *gadish*, but Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim separates them: the difference is threshed grain (*areimah*)
against unthreshed (*gadish*). Rabbi Klein reports that he has found no source that
distinguishes *areimah* from *chamarim* in meaning.

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

## Distinctions

### areimah (ערמה)
Root ע-ר-ם. Eleven times in Tanach in the sense of "pile" beyond Shemos 15:8,
including Yirmiyahu 50:26, Chaggai 2:16, Rus 3:7, Nechemiah 3:34 and 13:15, Shir
HaShirim 7:3, and a cluster in Divrei HaYamim II 31:6-9; in Mishnaic Hebrew at
*Terumos* 2:1, *Maasros* 1:5-6 and 5:7, and *Beitzah* 4:1. The same root also
carries "cleverness" (Bereishis 3:1, Iyov 5:12, Mishlei 19:25) and "nakedness."
- *Machberes Menachem* lists all three senses without intimating a connection
  between them.
- Rav Hirsch (1808-1888, to Bereishis 2:25) ties "pile" to "cleverness": a pile
  is typically many items, each worth little alone, that can become something
  important when heaped together, and cleverness is like a pile of thoughts a
  man has considered, no one of which looks like much until they are joined.
  Having explained those two, he admits that he does not know how "nakedness"
  fits the picture, and derives that word instead from ע-ו-ר (*ohr*, skin);
  elsewhere (to Bereishis 13:13) he does connect the two, the clever man being
  unencumbered by the worldly considerations that often cloud a man's intellect,
  so that in being "naked" he can think straight, and Rabbi Aharon Marcus
  (1843-1916, *Keses HaSofer* to Bereishis 3:1) makes a similar point.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814) traces ע-ר-ם to the biliteral
  ע-ר, "revealing, unconcealed": nakedness exposes the body; in the typical
  agrarian practice a pile of grain is grain already taken out of its sheaves
  and so revealed; *eir* ("awake") is the man whose abilities, hidden while he
  sleeps, are suddenly revealed, and thence "cleverness," the mind that stays
  sharp and aware as though always awake. He adds *armon* (Bereishis 30:37), a
  tree (possibly the *Platanus orientalis*) with branches only at the top and
  none along its length, so that it looks "naked."

### chamarim (חמרים)
Seemingly from the root ח-מ-ר. Three times in Tanach for "piles": twice in
Shemos 8:10 (the dead frogs gathered *chamarim chamarim*) and once in Bamidbar
11:32. In the Mishnah at *Uktzin* 2:5, a heap of onions amassed into one
grouping (*chamran*).

- Rabbi Sherirah Gaon (906-1006) notes in commenting there that the verb used
  for amassing the onions cognates with the biblical *chamarim chamarim*, as
  does Rambam in his commentary there.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:22-23 and 11:3) reads ח-מ-ר as unification and
  conglomeration of many components, which is why *chomer* is "matter,"
  *chamarim* are piles of like items, and *cheimar* is the mortar that unifies
  bricks (Bereishis 11:3, 14:10, Shemos 2:3).

### gadish (גדיש) and omer (עמר)

- Rambam (to *Peah* 6:2 and *Eduyos* 4:4) defines a *gadish* as an *areimah* of
  *amarim*, that is, a stack made of sheaves; Rabbi Klein reads him as having
  chosen those two related words on purpose because they are indeed synonymous,
  citing Rabbi Yaakov Emden's *Mishneh Lechem* to *Peah* 5:1.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim distinguishes them: a *gadish* is grain still inside
  the sheaf, an *areimah* is grain already threshed out and so revealed. Rabbi
  Klein notes that this runs contra Rambam, who seems to equate *gadish* and
  *areimah*.
- Rav Hirsch groups *omer/amar* ("bundling" stalks) with *chamarim* and with
  *amar* ("statement," many words gathered on one theme), all through the
  interchange of aleph, ches and ayin.

### The relation between areimah and chamarim
Rabbi Klein proposes, without a source, that the connection "does seem tenable"
in two steps: ע-ר-ם appears to relate to ע-מ-ר ("bundling") by metathesis, and
ע-מ-ר seems related to ח-מ-ר by the interchange of ayin and ches, so that
*areimah* and *chamarim* are ultimately related. He states plainly that he has
not seen any commentator who explicitly links the two, nor found anyone who
offers a way of differentiating their meanings. This is his own suggestion, not
a received distinction.

## Rendering guidance

- ערמה: "pile" or "heap." In an agricultural sugya, "pile of grain."
- חמרים חמרים: keep the doubling; "heaps upon heaps" or "in heap after heap."
  Do not flatten it to a single "piles."
- גדיש: "stack" (of sheaves), not "pile," so that it stays distinct from
  *areimah* on the page.
- עמר: "sheaf" or "bundle."
- Trap: English "pile" will happily absorb *areimah*, *chamarim* and *gadish*
  at once. Where a passage turns on Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's threshed against
  unthreshed, or where *gadish* and *areimah* stand in one sentence, force the
  split with pile / stack.
- Trap: נערמו מים (Shemos 15:8) is contested, and the English must choose.
  *Mechilta* (to Shemos 14:16), which says the waters became *areimos areimos*,
  Rashbam, who writes that they piled up high like a heap of wheat, Rasag
  (892-942), and Ibn Ezra in both of his commentaries to Shemos, all read it from
  *areimah*. Targum Onkelos renders *chakimu mayim*, "the waters became smart,"
  and this also seems to be Rashi's preferred reading; Chizkuni clarifies that the
  waters were intelligent enough to pursue the Egyptians and drown them (Rabbi
  Klein adds that this has nothing to do with added electrolytes), Rabbi Chaim
  Paltiel that they could tell Jew from Egyptian, and the Tosafistic *Sefer
  HaGan* that they suddenly gained the knowledge to sing Hashem's praise alongside
  the Jews. Donash Ibn Librat (920-985) objected to Rasag's reading, but there is
  an omission in the printed text of his work and his own preferred explanation is
  missing; Ibn Ezra, whose *Sefas Yeser* defends Rasag against Donash, records
  only that Donash differs and that his reading should be considered *drash*
  rather than *pshat*, and Rabbi Klein ventures his own guess that Donash may have
  preferred the Onkelos and Rashi approach. A third reading, in *Esther Rabbah*
  3:15, hears *arum* ("naked") in the word: one opinion there says Hashem strips
  the wicked of their outer clothes in Gehinnom, and another adds that He punished
  the Egyptians likewise while they were naked, as the first word of *ne'ermu
  mayim* seems to hint.
