# Gift: minchah / matanah / shai / teshurah / eshkar / doron

## Bottom line

*Minchah* is a gift of **homage or tribute**: it serves the giver, who is
showing his dependence on the receiver. *Matanah* is a gift that serves the
**receiver's need**, given with nothing coming back, which is why it is the word
for *matanos la'evyonim* and why Rabbi Mecklenburg holds it is never used of an
offering to Hashem. *Shai* is a **weighty gift**, on Bedersi's reading
substantial in itself and on Wertheimer's weighty in the giver's eyes though not
in the receiver's; in Tanach it is always given to Hashem. *Teshurah* is the
**showpiece gift** brought when one comes
before a great man. *Eshkar* is obscure and disputed. *Doron* is not Hebrew at
all; it is the Greek word for "gift" that the Targum uses. The trap: *minchah*
is not "meal-offering," it is "gift," and only the sacrificial context narrows
it.

## Distinctions

### minchah (מנחה)
The commonest of the six, over two hundred appearances, and the word the Torah
uses four times for Yaakov's tribute of five hundred and fifty animals to Esav
(Bereishis 32:14, 32:19-22). Rav Hirsch: a *minchah* is a gift of homage, by
which the giver shows his subservience; it serves the giver's interest in
demonstrating his dependence. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer takes it
differently, as a gift that gains the giver atonement. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi
Mecklenburg: a *minchah* can be an outright tax or tribute paid by a subject to
his master, as the subjugated Moavim paid David (Shmuel II 8:2) and as Hoshea,
the last king of Yisrael, paid his Assyrian overlord (Melachim II 17:3). Radak
and others root it in *nach*/*nachah*, "placed, rested," since the gift is meant
to settle and placate the recipient.

Rabbi Mecklenburg presses further: the more submission a gift shows, the more it
deserves the name *minchah*. Hence *Tefillas Minchah*, said as the sun is on its
way down, bowing, as it were, before Hashem; and hence the poor man's offering,
the *Korban Minchah*, in which the giver shows plainly that he is handing over
the little he has.

### matanah (מתנה)
Some twenty appearances. Wertheimer: *matan* and *matanah* are formed from the
verb of giving itself, and the word fixes on the giving. The column notes that
English *gift* and *give* likewise share an etymology, on the interchange of
the f and v sounds. A *matanah* is given where the recipient needs something and
the giver gains nothing from it beyond having filled that need, which is why it
builds friendship and camaraderie, and why it is mandated on Purim, *matanos
la'evyonim* (Esther 9:22). Rabbi Mecklenburg writes that
*matanah* is not used of an offering to Hashem and leaves the reason unstated;
the article supplies it, that a *matanah* fills a need and Hashem, being
complete, has none.

### shai (שי)
Three appearances: Yeshayahu 18:7, Tehillim 76:12, Tehillim 68:30. Rabbi Avraham
Bedersi HaPenini: *shai* is *yesh* ("there is") reversed, because a *shai* is a
gift of real substance. Wertheimer: it is a gift the giver holds to be
significant while for the receiver it is nothing special, which is why every
appearance of *shai* in Tanach is a gift to Hashem and the word never occurs in
any other setting.

### teshurah (תשורה)
A *hapax legomenon*, Shmuel I 9:7. *Midrash Shocher Tov* (Tehillim 87) expounds
it as the sort of tribute that people look at and sing praises over. Wertheimer
reads the Midrash as splitting the root two ways: *shur*, a kind of seeing, and
*shir*, a song. A *teshurah* is the most honorable and eye-catching present one
can bring. Malbim narrows the setting: it is the gift presented on greeting an
eminent figure, a kind of *minchah*, given when one comes to a navi or a holy man
for a berachah or a prophecy. Its purpose is to settle the receiver's body so
that he can rise past his physical limits and let his spiritual influence flow
back to the giver, so that giver and receiver trade places. This is why Yitzchak
asked Esav for a meal before blessing him (Bereishis 27).

### eshkar (אשכר)
Twice in Tanach (Tehillim 72:10, Yechezkel 27:15) and obscure. Radak is unsure
whether the root is all four letters or only the last three (ש-כ-ר, "beer" or
"drunk"). Rabbi Avraham ben Chaim Ibn Ramoch: a *minchah* is gold, silver, and
precious stones while an *eshkar* is choice fruits, which the article reads as
inedible against edible, and which would explain the link to *sheichar*.
Wertheimer: an *eshkar* is a gift whose worth is aesthetic rather than monetary
or useful, a gift that bribes the recipient into overrating it, leaving him as
though intoxicated (*shikur*) and unable to weigh its real value. Malbim relates
*eshkar* to *sachar*, "reward" or "payment," the shin shifting to a sin.

### doron (דורון)
It does not appear in Tanach at all. Targum Yonasan renders the *minchah* Yaakov
sent Esav as *doron*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) notes that
*doron* is not Aramaic but Greek for "gift"; it stands in Hebrew as a loanword.
The column adds that it is close in sense to the name Theodore, "God's gift" in
Greek, which is the parallel to the Hebrew name Yonasan, "Hashem's gift."

## Rendering guidance

- *Minchah* outside the korbanos: **tribute** where submission is the point
  (Shmuel II 8:2, Melachim II 17:3), **gift** where it is Yaakov to Esav. Inside
  the korbanos: **meal-offering**, but that is the context speaking, not the
  word, per Rabbi Mecklenburg's note.
- *Matanah*: **gift**, plain. *Matanos la'evyonim* stays transliterated.
- *Shai*: **tribute** in the pesukim where it is brought to Hashem, and never
  "little present"; on Bedersi's reading the word means the opposite, and even
  on Wertheimer's the smallness is only in the receiver's eyes.
- *Teshurah*: **presentation gift**, or "a gift of greeting" where Malbim's
  setting is in view.
- *Eshkar*: leave it transliterated and gloss the machlokes; there is no settled
  English.
- Trap: English "gift" will absorb *minchah*, *matanah*, *shai*, and *teshurah*
  at a stroke, and the whole distinction is who the gift serves. If a passage
  contrasts them, hold the axis: tribute (serves the giver) against gift (serves
  the receiver).
- Trap: "offering" reads as sacrificial in English. Do not use it for a
  *minchah* sent between men.
