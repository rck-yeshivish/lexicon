# Veil: tza'if / redid

## Bottom line

*Tza'if* is the **veil**: the covering a woman spreads over her face, the word
used of Rivkah and of Tamar. *Redid* is the **shawl** or wrap, a thin garment
worn over the head and shoulders, and in Rambam's usage the outer wrap without
which a woman would not appear in public. Targum Yonasan renders *tza'if* as
*redida* and Onkelos as *eifa*, so the two Hebrew words are close, but the
difference to hold is that *tza'if* is defined by what it covers, the face,
while *redid* is defined by how it hangs, thin and draped downward.

## Distinctions

### tza'if (צעיף)
Three times in Chumash, all in Bereishis: Rivkah covers herself with a *tza'if*
on seeing Yitzchak (24:65), and Tamar puts one on when she sets aside her
widow's clothing (38:14) and removes it afterward (38:19). The classical
lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak) assign it the root צ-ע-פ.

Several read the word as a contraction. Rabbi Menachem ben Shlomo (*Midrash
Sechel Tov* to Bereishis 24:65) parses it as *mutza al paneha*, spread over her
face; Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehudah*) as *tza* (from
*mutza*, spread) plus *af* (from *apayim*, face); Rabbi Saadia al Damari
(*Midrash HaBeiur* there) as a notarikon of *tzaf al paneha*, floats over her
face.

On Onkelos' *eifa*: Rabbi Avraham Vilner, son of the Vilna Gaon (*Tirgem
Avraham* to Bereishis 24:65), explains it as *tza'if* with the initial tzadi
dropped. Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev (*Me'at Tzari* to Bereishis 38:14) accounts for
the dropping: a Hebrew tzadi commonly becomes an Aramaic ayin (as *tzemer*
becomes *amar*), and since *tza'if* already has an ayin after its tzadi, the
first letter simply fell away. Rabbi Menachem ben Shlomo explains that *eifa* in
Aramaic means doubling or folding (Targum Onkelos to Shemos 26:9, 28:16),
because the *tza'if* was so sheer that it had to be folded over to be opaque
enough to hide the hair beneath it. Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg adds that this
Aramaic ע-פ is close to the Hebrew ע-ב (*av*, thick), peh and beis being
interchangeable. Rabbi Yitzchak Ratzabi explains the folding differently, that
the *tza'if* was worn with its edges folded to fit the wearer, or that it was an
extra covering over another; he also reads the initial tzadi as a hint to *tzei*
(go out), the *tza'if* being what a woman wore when going outside.

Rav Hirsch has two comments. To Bereishis 24:65 he relates צ-ע-פ to ש-א-פ,
drawing into oneself, as one draws the *tza'if* close and wraps it snugly. To
Bereishis 40:6 he relates it to the phonetically similar ז-ע-פ (troubled): a
*tza'if* veils the face from without, while a troubled countenance is a veiling
from within, the liveliness gone out of the expression.

### redid (רדיד)
Twice in Tanach: the city watchmen strip the *redid* from the woman searching
for her beloved (Shir HaShirim 5:7), and *redidim* appear among the finery of
the daughters of Tzion (Yeshayahu 3:23). Once in the Mishnah, on how much of a
string hanging from a *redid* counts as part of it for tumah (Keilim 29:1).
*Sefer HaAruch* defines a *radid* as a thin *tza'if* that women use to cover
their heads; Ibn Ezra (to Yeshayahu 3:23 and Shir HaShirim 5:7) compares it to a
tallis, and Radak calls it a thin garment.

Rambam gives the social sense: women had the practice of not appearing in
public, even with their hair covered, unless wrapped in a *redid* (*Hilchos
Ishus* 13:11, 24:12), and the suspected sotah is brought before the onlookers
without her *redid* as part of her degradation (*Hilchos Sotah* 3:5). It appears
once more in his code where a judge disqualified himself because a litigant had
removed a feather from his *redid* (*Hilchos Sanhedrin* 23:3).

On the name: *Metzudas Tzion* (to Yeshayahu 3:23) takes it from the thinness of
the cloth, since *merudad*, from ר-ד-ד, is what has been beaten down thin
(Targum Onkelos to Shemos 39:3). Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras begins the
same way and then offers a notarikon of *ra'ah* (see) and *dod* (beloved), a
*redid* being the ornament whose wearing draws closeness.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *redid* to the biliteral ר-ד, whose core sense is
descending: *yeridah* (going down, hanging down), *marud* (a lowered, embittered
state), *rodeh* (ruling, since a ruler lowers others beneath him), *mered*
(rebellion, shaking off being lowered), *tardemah* (a sleep that falls upon a
man and overpowers him), and *nerd*, the spice of Shir HaShirim 1:12 and 4:14,
which falls as sap from the plant, or overpowers by its scent, or brings on
sleep. He then gives three ways *redid* returns to that core: it hangs down from
head or shoulders; it was the inner covering, "lowered" in rank beneath an outer
decorative one (a *tzanif*, or the *tza'if* itself); or it was the light covering
a woman wore while sleeping, which is what ties it to *tardemah*.

### sudar (סודר)
Not part of the pair, but worth knowing: in the Mishnah (Shabbos 3:3, 20:2, Yoma
6:8, Sanhedrin 6:1, 7:2-3, Tamid 7:3, Keilim 29:1) the *sudar* is the shawl or
cloth worn by a man, and it can also mean a cloth waved as a signal. It is a
loanword from the Greek *soudarion*, from the Latin for a cloth to wipe off
sweat.

## Rendering guidance

- *Tza'if*: **veil**. "Shawl" and "headcloth" are both defensible, but "veil" is
  what carries the two Chumash narratives, Rivkah covering herself before
  Yitzchak and Tamar concealing her identity from Yehudah.
- *Redid*: **shawl**, or **wrap**. In Rambam's halachos, "wrap" reads better
  than "veil," since the point is going out into the street properly covered,
  not concealing the face.
- Trap: English "veil" will absorb both, and "shawl" will absorb both the other
  way. Where *tza'if* and *redid* stand together (as they do wherever Onkelos
  and Targum Yonasan are compared on one pasuk, or in Rabbi Pappenheim's account
  of an outer and an inner covering), give "veil" to *tza'if* and "shawl" to
  *redid* and hold it.
- *Eifa* in Onkelos is not a third garment; it is Onkelos' Aramaic for *tza'if*.
  Translate it "veil" and note the Aramaic if the sense of folding is at issue.
- *Sudar* is a man's, so do not render it with the same English used for either
  of the women's coverings; "cloth" or "shawl" by context.
