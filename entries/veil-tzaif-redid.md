# Veil: tza'if / redid

## Bottom line

The Biblical *tza'if* is variously translated **veil**, **shawl**, or
**headcloth** (in Modern Hebrew it is a scarf or a mask), and every Biblical
*tza'if* and *redid* is a "shawl" worn by a woman. *Tza'if* is the covering of
Rivkah and of Tamar; several parse it as spread or floating "on her face"
(*Midrash Sechel Tov*, Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras, Rabbi Saadia al
Damari), and Gesenius (cited approvingly by Shadal) takes it from a root of
wrapping. *Redid* is glossed as a thin *tza'if* for the head (*Sefer
HaAruch*), a thin garment (Radak), something like a tallis (Ibn Ezra, on the
Arabic), and in Rambam the wrap without which women by custom did not appear in
public. Targum pseudo-Yonasan and Targum Yerushalmi always render *tza'if* as
*redida*, and Onkelos consistently as *eifa*.

## Distinctions

### tza'if (צעיף)
Three times in Tanach, all in Bereishis: Rivkah bashfully covers herself with a
*tza'if* on meeting Yitzchak (24:65), and Tamar, disguising herself, removes her
widow's clothing and covers herself in one (38:14), and afterward removes it
(38:19).

Several read the word as a contraction.
- Rabbi Menachem ben Shlomo (*Midrash Sechel Tov* to Bereishis 24:65) parses it
  as *mutza al paneha*, spread upon her face;
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehudah*) as *tza* (from *mutza*,
  spread) plus *af* (from *apayim*, face);
- Rabbi Saadia al Damari (*Midrash HaBeiur* there) as a notarikon of *tzaf al
  paneha*, floats on her face.

The classical lexicographers (including Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, and Radak) all
trace it to the root צ-ע-ף. Wilhelm Gesenius argues that צ-ע-ף is a permutation of ע-ט-ף, wrapping, by the interchange of tes
and tzadi together with metathesis; Shadal (to Bereishis 24:65) cites this
approvingly.

On Onkelos' *eifa*: Targum pseudo-Yonasan and Targum Yerushalmi (to 24:65,
38:14, 38:19) consistently render *tza'if* as *redida*, but Onkelos there
consistently renders it *eifa* (ע-פ-א).
- Rabbi Avraham Vilner, son of the Vilna Gaon (1765 to 1808, *Tirgem Avraham* to
  Bereishis 24:65), explains it as *tza'if* with the initial tzadi dropped.
  Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev (*Me'at Tzari* to Bereishis 38:14) explains that this may
  result from a Hebrew tzadi very often becoming an Aramaic ayin (as *tzemer*
  becomes *amar*): since *tza'if* already has an ayin after its tzadi, the first
  letter was simply dropped. (*Eifah*, with a final hei, is also the name of
  Avraham's grandson through Midyan, Bereishis 25:4, Divrei HaYamim I 1:33.)
- Rabbi Menachem ben Shlomo explains that *eifa* in Aramaic means doubling,
  folding, or wrapping (see Targum Onkelos to Shemos 26:9, 28:16), because the
  *tza'if* was so thin as to be fairly see-through and had to be folded over to
  be opaque enough to obscure the wearer's hair. Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the
  Veromemanu Foundation adds that this sense of the Aramaic ע-ף is similar to
  the Hebrew ע-ב (*av*, thick), and the two roots might be connected by the
  interchange of peh and beis.
- Alternatively, Rabbi Yitzchak Ratzabi (a prominent Yemenite rabbi in the Neve
  Achiezer neighborhood of Bnei Brak) explains that the *tza'if* was worn with
  its edges or sides folded to fit the wearer, and suggests that perhaps it
  relates to doubling because it was an extra head covering worn in addition to
  another. He also reads the initial tzadi as a hint to *tzei* (go out),
  understanding the *tza'if* as what a woman primarily wore when going outside
  (contrast *redid*, under Rabbi Pappenheim below).

Rav Hirsch has two comments.

- To Bereishis 24:65 he suggests that צ-ע-ף is related to ש-א-ף, by the
  interchange of tzadi with shin and of ayin with aleph: that root is drawing
  into oneself by inhaling, as one draws the *tza'if* close by wrapping it
  snugly.
- To Bereishis 40:6 he relates it to the phonetically similar ז-ע-ף (upset,
  troubled): a *tza'if* veils the face from without, while a troubled
  countenance is veiled from within, looking like that of a sleeping or dead
  person, the life missing from the expression.

### redid (רדיד)
Twice in Tanach: the city watchmen beat the woman searching for her beloved and
strip her *redid* from her (Shir HaShirim 5:7), and *redidim* appear among the
adornments the daughters of Tzion haughtily wore (Yeshayahu 3:23). A 1984 essay
in *Beit Mikra* by the Bible scholar Samuel Shaviv takes the second as Yeshayahu
borrowing from Shir HaShirim, which would make Shir HaShirim the earlier book and
lend credence to the traditional view that Shlomo HaMelech wrote it, against the
popular academic view that places it in the Persian period after the
destruction of the First Temple.

Once in the Mishnah, on how much of a string hanging from a *redid* counts as
part of it for tumah (Keilim 29:1).

- In that context Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) defines a *radid* as a
  thin *tza'if* that women use to cover their heads.
- Ibn Ezra (to Yeshayahu 3:23 and Shir HaShirim 5:7) explains that in Arabic
  *redid* is something akin to a tallis, a shawl, and Radak (there) similarly
  calls it a thin garment.
- Radak relates the Hebrew *redid* to the Arabic *arid*; *Sefer HeAruch* and
  others connect it with the Arabic *rida*, a fabric for wrapping the upper
  body.

Rambam mentions a custom that women did not appear in public, even with their
hair covered, unless wrapped in a *redid* (*Hilchos Ishus* 13:11, 24:12); by
contrast the suspected sotah is made to appear before the onlookers without her
*redid* as part of her degradation (*Hilchos Sotah* 3:5). It appears once more
in his code, where a judge disqualified himself because a litigant had removed a
feather from the judge's *redid*, a favor that compromised his impartiality
(*Hilchos Sanhedrin* 23:3).

On the name:

- *Metzudas Tzion* (to Yeshayahu 3:23) implies that the thinness of the cloth
  gives it its name, since *merudad*, from ר-ד-ד, is what has been beaten down
  or subdued until very thin (see Targum Onkelos to Shemos 39:3).
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras first offers something similar and then
  suggests parsing *redid* as [*ra'ah*](seeing-raah-hibit-tzofeh.html#%D7%A8%D7%90%D7%94) (see) and [*dod*](uncle-dod-misaref.html#%D7%93%D7%95%D7%93) (beloved), a *redid* being
  the ornament whose wearing exudes closeness by beautifying the wearer.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *redid* to the biliteral ר-ד, whose core sense is
descending: *yeridah* (going down, hanging down), *marud* (a lowered, embittered state), *rodeh* (ruling, since a ruler lowers
others beneath him), *mered* (rebellion, shaking off being lowered), *tardemah*
(a sleep that falls upon a man and overtakes him), and *nerd*, the spice
mentioned three times in Tanach (Shir HaShirim 1:12, 4:13, 4:14), which falls as sap from the plant, or overpowers by its
scent, or brings on sleep. He then gives three ways *redid* returns to that
core: it hangs down from head or shoulders (Rabbi Ratzabi offers a similar
explanation); many women wore two head coverings, an outer decorative one (a
*tzanif*, or perhaps a *tza'if*, as above) and an inner one covering the hair,
the *redid*, "lowered" in rank beneath the outer one (so *rodeh*); or it was a
head covering women might wear lightly while sleeping, which ties it to
*tardemah*. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds a suggestion of his own, a "sort of feminist
interpretation": that forcing women to cover their hair might be a form of
ruling over them, which would fit the root's sense of ruling.

### sudar (סודר)
Not part of the pair. All Biblical cases of *tza'if*
and *redid* refer to a shawl worn by a woman, while the Talmud has a term for a
shawl worn by a man, *sudar*. It appears multiple times in the Mishnah (Shabbos
3:3, 20:2, Yoma 6:8, Sanhedrin 6:1, 7:2-3, Tamid 7:3, Keilim 29:1) and can
also mean a cloth waved as a signal. Rabbi Ernest Klein's etymological
dictionary of Hebrew derives it as a back-formation from a supposed plural
*sudarim*, from the Greek *soudarion*, borrowed from the Latin *sudarium*, a
cloth for wiping off perspiration, from *sudor*, sweat, which goes back to the
Proto-Indo-European root *sweid-* or *svoid-*, to sweat. From the same root come English *sweat*, *sweater*, and *exude*, the Yiddish
*shvitz*, and the Modern Hebrew *sveder*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Tza'if*: **veil**. "Shawl" and "headcloth" are both defensible (Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein himself says "shawl"), but "veil" is what carries the two Chumash
  narratives, Rivkah covering herself before Yitzchak and Tamar concealing her
  identity from Yehudah.
- *Redid*: **shawl**, or **wrap**. In Rambam's halachos, "wrap" reads better
  than "veil," since the point is going out into the street properly covered,
  not concealing the face.
- Trap: English "veil" will absorb both, and "shawl" will absorb both the other
  way. Where *tza'if* and *redid* stand together (as they do wherever Onkelos
  and the other Targumim are compared on one pasuk, or in Rabbi Pappenheim's
  account of an outer and an inner covering, where the outer one is a *tzanif*
  or perhaps a *tza'if*), give "veil" to *tza'if* and "shawl" to *redid* and
  hold it.
- *Eifa* in Onkelos is his Aramaic for *tza'if*, so there is no third garment to translate.
  Translate it "veil" and note the Aramaic if the sense of folding is at issue.
- *Sudar* is a man's, so do not render it with the same English used for either
  of the women's coverings; "cloth" or "shawl" by context.
