# Bed: mitah / eres (arisah)

## Bottom line

*Mitah* is the ordinary **bed**, close to thirty appearances in Tanach, from a
root meaning to incline or stretch out, since a man inclines his body to lie
down. *Eres* is the rarer word, nine appearances, and most of the explanations that
distinguish the two make it the more particular one: a fitted or special bed, an iron-framed one, a
crib, a bed of woven or cross-fastened planks. In many instances Targum renders
both by *arsa*.
Ramchal holds they are genuine synonyms paired for poetic effect; the other views
cited do not treat them as fully synonymous.

## Distinctions

### mitah (מטה)
The common word. The root is disputed:

- Menachem Ibn Saruk gives mem-tes.
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) gives mem-tes-tes or נ-ט-ה.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*, *Cheshek Shlomo*) gives tes-hei.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus suggests the single letter tes, which he reads as movement
  to the side, gathering under it *stiyah* (deviation), *natah* (to incline),
  *titah* (turn aside), *tata* (broom), *tach* (plastering), *taah* (erred), and
  more.

Every one of these yields the same sense: to incline or stretch out, which is
what a man does with his body to lie down. The same phenomenon is found in
Greek, where *kline*, a bed, derives from *klino*, to incline, slant or bend,
and this Greek root underlies the English *recline*, *incline*, *climax*,
*climate* and *clinic*.

- Malbim, in one place: *mitah* is any piece of furniture one lies or reclines
  on, a sofa, couch or loveseat included, while *eres* is specifically what one
  sleeps on.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer suggests that *mitah* is the wooden-framed bed.

In Rabbinic usage *mitah* can also mean a bier or coffin.

### eres (ערש / ערס)
Nine appearances in Tanach, the famous one being Og's iron *eres* at Devarim
3:11. In many instances the Targumim render both Hebrew words by the cognate
*arsa*, which would suggest that they mean the same thing, and that *mitah* is
primarily Hebrew while
*eres* is an Aramaic word borrowed into Hebrew; the Hebrew generally spells it with a sin
and
the Aramaic with a samech, letters that often interchange. Readings:

- Rashbam (to Devarim 3:11): Og's *eres* was his crib. The point of its being
  iron is that Og as an infant was already so strong that an ordinary crib would
  have broken, and an infant, less aware of his own movements, needs the sturdier
  bed more than a grown man does. Modern Hebrew partially follows this, *eres* a cradle and
  *arisah* a crib.
- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*): the root ע-ר-ש/ע-ר-ס, from which *eres*
  is ostensibly derived, is an expression of mixture. Building on that,
  other commentators explain that *arisah*, dough (Bamidbar 15:21), the biblical
  equivalent of the
  Mishnaic *isah*, belongs to the same root, dough being ingredients kneaded
  together. Rabbi Yaakov
  Tzvi Mecklenburg and Rav Hirsch point out that in some sources *arisah* is
  conjugated as a
  verb (see Berachos 37b), which implies the root is primarily an act of mixing
  or mutual
  connection; on that basis they explain an *eres* as a bed whose sides are
  fastened to each other by strips
  or bars running through the middle, or built of woven or layered planks, so
  that the bed itself shows the marks of mixing and mutual connection.
- Dr. Richard C. Steiner ("The Case for Fricative-Laterals in Proto-Semitic,"
  American Oriental Series 59, 1977, pp. 130 ff.) explains the lamed of *arsal*,
  from *eres*, by positing that sin was once pronounced with something like an
  *l* sound, or was at least heard that way by the Greeks, hence the *sl* in
  *arsala*.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer suggests the difference is material, not
  structure or configuration.
  *Eres* is the iron- or metal-framed bed, *mitah* the wooden one.
- Malbim: *mitah* is generic, *eres* is a custom-fitted bed, whether for the sick
  (Tehillim 41:4), for a child (Og's case), or for a pampered woman (Mishlei
  7:16). Talmudic Aramaic *arsa* can also mean a coffin (Kesubos 103a), which,
  as Rabbi Klein observes, in certain ways sits well with Malbim's reading,
  though *mitah* too is used that way.

### the mussar and kabbalistic readings
- Rabbi Aviad Sar-Shalom Basilea (*Emunas Chachamim*) posits that *mitah* is
  typically the word for the bed of husband and wife, and so stands for the
  right balance of influencer and receiver, the man the influencing and
  initiating force, the woman the receiving and building one; it is therefore
  the word thematically evocative of *tzaddikim*, who keep that balance by
  recognizing their own receptive role before Hashem. *Eres*, by contrast,
  connotes the bed of the wicked, a connotation seen best in its use for the
  wicked Og.
- Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Kaidanover (*Kav HaYashar*) likewise associates *eres* and
  *arisah* with the forces of evil, connecting *eres* with an ayin to its
  homophone *eres* with an aleph, poison, an allusion to the poisonous snake in
  Gan Eden that first brought sin into the world by enticing Chavah.

In short, on these readings *mitah* is the bed of the righteous and *eres* the
bed of the wicked.

The Torah's use of *arisah* for the dough of *challah* (Bamidbar 15:21) has been
read by some as a deliberate echo of *eres*: as one consecrates his dough and
sets it aside for the Kohen, so must a man consecrate his bed, either by giving
himself to Hashem's service the moment he wakes, before he rises, or by keeping
what is done in that bed holy and pure.

## Rendering guidance

- מטה: "bed," or "couch" where the source is Malbim's broader sense.
- ערש: "bed" in plain prose; "bedstead" where the frame is the point (Og's iron
  *eres*, Devarim 3:11); "crib" or "cradle" where Rashbam's reading is being
  followed.
- Trap: "bed" absorbs both, and so does Targum's *arsa*. If a source is
  darshening the choice of word (Og's *eres* against a *tzaddik*'s *mitah*), the
  translation has to keep the Hebrew visible or the derashah is lost.
- Where the two stand together in one verse, take Ramchal's option only if the
  context is plainly poetic parallelism; otherwise render *mitah* "bed" and
  *eres* "couch" or "bedstead" so the pair does not collapse.
- עריסה of *challah* is "dough," not "bed"; keep the connection for a note, not
  for the body text.
