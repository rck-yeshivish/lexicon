# Donkey: chamor / aton / pere / ayir / pered

## Bottom line

These are different animals, not stylistic variants. *Chamor* is the
domesticated donkey and, in Biblical Hebrew, of either sex (Radak): **donkey**.
*Aton*, Bilaam's animal throughout Bamidbar 22, is the she-donkey according to
Radak: the **jenny** or **she-ass**. Shadal holds that it first meant the female
of the wild *pere* and later took in the domestic female too, and Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim rejects the feminine reading and makes it the donkey at its full
strength. *Pere* (Aramaic *arud*) is the undomesticated **wild ass**. Radak
classes the *chamor* as a *behemah* and the *pere* as a *chayah*. *Ayir* is the
young one, a **foal**, and Zecharyah 9:9 puts *chamor* and *ayir* in one pasuk.
*Pered* is the **mule**.

## Distinctions

### The English words, before the Hebrew ones
English has many overlapping names for what is often just called the domestic
donkey. The older word is *ass*, from Latin *asinus*, with cognates across Indo-European:
Greek *onos*, and possibly the Sumerian *anse*; English keeps others in
*onager* (Middle English for a wild donkey), *easel* (a wooden frame that looks
like a donkey) and *asinine*. *Donkey* is late, first attested in the 1780s, of
uncertain etymology and with no clear cognates; it displaced *ass* over the
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries as *ass* turned pejorative. Rabbi Ernest
Klein proposes that *donkey* comes from Old English *dun*/*dunn* (Welsh *dwn*),
"brown," with the *k* and *y* as double diminutives, and that the spelling was
influenced by *monkey*. By sex and age: an adult male is a *jack* or *jackass*,
an adult female a *jenny* or *jennet*, a young one of either sex a *foal*. The
two hybrids matter for the last section here: a *mule* is the offspring of a
male donkey and a female horse, while a *hinny* (Latin *hinnus*) comes of a male
horse bred to a female donkey.

### chamor (חמור)
First at Bereishis 12:16, among Pharaoh's gifts to Avraham, and roughly a
hundred times after. Grammatically masculine, but Radak (*Michlol*, *Shaar
Dikduk HaPe'alim*, and to Shmuel II 19:27) holds it covers a donkey of either
sex. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Vayikra 22:28)
supports him from *peter chamor* (Shemos 13:13, 34:20): the firstborn comes from
the womb of a *chamor*, and the mother is necessarily female. Rabbinic Hebrew
coined the explicit feminine *chamorah*, once in the Mishnah (Bechoros 1:2) and
several times in the Gemara (Moed Katan 12a and others), never found in Tanach.

*Chamor* is basic enough to have cognates in all the major Semitic languages:
Aramaic and Syriac *chamara*, Ugaritic *hmr*, Arabic *himar*, Akkadian *imeru*.
That said, the Hebrew lexicographers all agree it derives from the triliteral
ח-מ-ר, which also gives wine, brown, clay and mortar, a pile, and *chomer*, a
unit of area.

- Rabbi Aharon Marcus takes "brown" as the core, so the word names the
  reddish-brown hue of many donkeys.
- As a pack animal the *chamor* represents the wholly material side of creation,
  hence the tie to "clay and material".
- others tie it to "wine," the dull donkey's intellect matching a drunkard's.

Note also the personal name Chamor, father of Shechem, thirteen times in Tanach
(Bereishis 33-34; Yehoshua 24:32; Shoftim 9:28).

### aton (אתון)
Thirty-four occurrences, densest at Bilaam's she-donkey (Bamidbar 22:21-23,
22:25, 22:27-30, 22:32-33). It stands beside *chamor* in Pharaoh's gifts
(Bereishis 12:16), and Devorah's song praises the judges who "ride on white
*asonos*" (Shoftim 5:10). Radak: while *chamor* is either sex, *aton* is
specifically female. Rabbi Ernest Klein's etymological dictionary makes *aton* a
quintessentially Semitic word, with parallels in Aramaic and Syriac (*atana*),
Ugaritic (*atnt*), Arabic (*atan*) and Akkadian (*atanu*), all meaning "jenny".
It remains an open question why Semitic has two separate root-words for the male
and female donkey when the male and female of other animals often take one root,
as with *par* and [*parah*](cattle-shor-par-egel-bakar.html#%D7%A4%D7%A8), *egel* and *eglah*, [*keves*](sheep-and-goats-seh-keves-ayil-eiz.html#%D7%9B%D7%91%D7%A9) and *kivsah*.

- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (*Ohr Chadash* to Bereishis 12:16) proposes that
  *aton* derives from *es*, the particle that generally carries no meaning of
  its own but is typically attached to another word: male donkeys being known
  for an elevated libido (see Yechezkel 23:20), the *aton*'s role is to be
  attached to the male.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 12:16, Shemos 14:27) links א-ת-ן to א-ד-ן, *tav* and
  *dalet* interchanging, so the name marks her load-bearing capacity, as an
  *eden*, one of the silver sockets holding up the beams of the Mishkan, holds
  up what stands on it.

Two dissents, both resting on *chamor* itself being usable for a female.

- Shadal (to Bereishis 12:16): *aton* is a separate species, the female of the
  *pere*, and the pasuk separates *chamor* from *aton* with servants and
  maidservants, not how one lists a male and female of one kind. But the
  Shunamis rode an *aton* (Melachim II 4:24), and he finds it highly unlikely
  that a woman would ride a wild ass; so he concludes the word later widened to
  the domesticated female too, its young being an *ayir* (Bereishis 49:11).
- Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) rejects the feminine reading for the same reason
  and offers a stage of life: from ת ("sign, boundary") comes א-ת, the joining
  of separate units, whence *es* and [*atah*](coming-biah-halichah-atah.html#%D7%90%D7%AA%D7%94); *eisan* is strength from parts
  tightly joined, and *aton* is the donkey at its full strength.

### pere (פרא) and arud (ערוד)
Ten occurrences, with final *aleph* or *hei*: the undomesticated wild ass, and by
transfer a man of unbridled conduct, as Yishmael was foretold to be a *pere
adam* (Bereishis 16:12). Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 16:11) groups פ-ר-א
with פ-ר-ע, פ-ר-ח, פ-ר-ה, ב-ר-א, ב-ר-ח and ב-ר-ה, all turning on freedom and
unhindered movement; the man likened to the animal lacks the boundaries a man
should have. Biblical Aramaic has *aradaya* once (Daniel 5:21), and Targumic
Aramaic always renders *pere* as *arud*. Mishnaic Hebrew uses *arud*, not *pere*:
crossing a *chamor* with an *arud* is forbidden despite their likeness (Kilayim
1:6), and Rabbi Akiva sets the time for *Shema* by when one can tell an *arud*
from a *chamor* (Berachos 9b). The Mishnah calls an *arud* a [*chayah*](animal-behemah-chayah.html#%D7%97%D7%99%D7%94) (Kilayim
8:6), matching Radak, and *arud* for the wild ass runs through the Bavli as well
(Berachos 9b; Rosh Hashanah 3a; Bava Kamma 37a; Chullin 59a; Menachos 103b). The
*arud* of Berachos 33a and Chullin 127a is an unrelated homonym, a poisonous
lizard taken as a cross between a snake and a turtle.

### ayir (עיר) and sayach (סיח)
Eight occurrences: Yaakov's tribute to Esav (Bereishis 32:16), the thirty sons of
Yair the Giladi on thirty *ayarim* (Shoftim 10:4), and the forty sons and thirty
grandsons who rode on seventy *ayarim* at Shoftim 12:14. Riding one evidently marked rank, and
Zecharyah calls Mashiach "riding on a *chamor* and on an *ayir* the son of
*asonos*" (Zecharyah 9:9). Where *ayir* is paired with *aton*
(Bereishis 32:16, 49:11, Zecharyah 9:9) it is her offspring, a foal; Radak
(*Sefer HaShorashim*) allows it for the young of a *chamor* or of a *pere*.
The classical lexicographers trace *ayir* to the triliteral ע-י-ר, but
Pappenheim treats the *yud* as inessential and reads ע-ר, "revealing" (so *ir*,
a city with squares open to the public; *ohr*, the skin that is revealed;
*ervah*, nakedness; [*taar*](razor-taar-morah.html#%D7%AA%D7%A2%D7%A8), the razor that reveals the skin; *ar*, an enemy who
reveals his enmity; and *eir*, awake, since a sleeper's abilities show only on
waking): as [*naar*](youth-naar-almah-elem.html#%D7%A0%D7%A2%D7%A8) is the stage at which a youth's potential suddenly reveals
itself, *ayir* is the adolescent donkey whose strength as a pack animal is now
being revealed. In rabbinic usage the value shifts: Rashi (to Rosh Hashanah 3a)
takes *ayir* as a young horse, and Rashbam (to Bava Basra 78b) gives *sayach*
for a young donkey, though in Modern Hebrew *sayach* means a young horse, a
colt.

### pered (פרד) and yeimim (ימים)
*Pered* is the mule, feminine *pirdah*; root פ-ר-ד, separating.
- Rav Hai Gaon, cited by Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim*: the mule is separated
  from other animals by being unable to reproduce.
- Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg compares it to its human counterpart the *mamzer*,
  whose name is tied to *zar*, often kept outside on account of his parentage;
  on his reading the *pered*, by dint of its tainted, intermixed lineage, may
  have been seen as something separate from other animals, a kind of pariah.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein proposes an alternative of his own from the Mishnah's
  ruling that a *pered* may not be mated with a horse or a donkey (Kilayim 1:6),
  which is the novel case, horse-and-donkey needing no statement: the root may cover its opposite as well, joining together what ought to
  stay separate, which would fit the *pered*'s parentage.

The resemblance between *pered* and the German and Yiddish *pferd*, "horse,"
gives rise to a common misconception of borrowing in one direction or the other;
linguists trace the German word to the Proto-Indo-European root *reidh-*, "to
ride," by way of the Latinate *paraveredus*, "a courier's horse."

*Yeimim* is a *hapax*: Anah found the *yeimim* in the desert while pasturing his
father Tzivon's donkeys (Bereishis 36:24).
- Rashi: he discovered, or invented, mules by mating a male donkey with a mare,
  which is pointed because Anah was himself a *mamzer*, born of the incestuous
  union of Tzivon and Tzivon's own mother.
- The Yerushalmi (Berachos 8:5) cites a slightly different version of the
  tradition, in which the *yeimim* were born of a male horse and a female
  donkey. According to Al-HaTorah, the comment in Rashi giving a donkey father
  and a horse mother does not appear in all manuscript versions of Rashi; Rabbi
  Reuven Chaim Klein finds that this makes sense, since it is the converse of
  the Yerushalmi.
- Shadal (to Bereishis 36:24) alludes to Rashi's explanation and rejects it
  because he sees no difference between *yeimim* and its apparent synonym
  *pered*, and follows the Yerushalmi, so that *pered* is a mule and *yeimim*
  are hinnies.
- Targum Onkelos renders *yeimim* as giants, and Ibn Ezra follows, noting that
  Onkelos calls the Eimim people giants too (Devarim 2:10-11).

On the name: Rashi (to Bereishis 36:24), following Chullin 7b, connects it to
[*eimah*](fear-eimah-pachad-yirah-daagah.html#%D7%90%D7%99%D7%9E%D7%94), fear, although the word has no *aleph*, because the *yeimim* were
dangerous animals people feared; Pappenheim similarly ties it to the biliterals
י-ם and ה-ם, which he defines as wildness or chaos; and Rabbi Yehuda ben Simon
in the Yerushalmi reads it as Greek, a form of *hemionos*, from *hemi-*, half,
and *onos*, donkey, which gives etymological backing to the tradition that Anah
discovered the mule.

## Rendering guidance

- Zecharyah 9:9: "riding on a *chamor*, and on an *ayir*, the foal of *asonos*."
  Three words, three renderings. One repeated "donkey" makes the pasuk read as a
  stammer instead of a description.
- Trap: English "ass" and "donkey" between them absorb *chamor*, *aton*, *pere*
  and *ayir*. Fix "donkey" to *chamor*, "she-donkey" to *aton*, "wild ass" to
  *pere*, "foal" to *ayir*, and hold to it across the passage.
- Bamidbar 22: Bilaam's animal is an *aton* every time. "His donkey" passes as
  narrative English, but where a *derashah* turns on her being female, say
  she-donkey.
- Bereishis 16:12, *pere adam*: "a wild ass of a man." On Rav Hirsch's reading
  the force is unrestraint, not stupidity; "a wild man" loses the animal and the
  point together.
- *Pered* is "mule," and *yeimim* on Shadal are "hinnies." Where a piece quotes
  Rashi and the Yerushalmi both, name the disagreement rather than picking an
  English that settles it quietly; Onkelos read *yeimim* as giants.
- In a rabbinic text do not carry the Biblical values over: *ayir* there may be a
  young horse, and a young donkey is *sayach*.
