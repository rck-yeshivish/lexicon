# Trembling: chil / dachil / zachal

## Bottom line

*Chil* is the commonest of the three in Biblical Hebrew: **fear** and the
**trembling** that fear produces, the word Tanach often uses of a woman's labor
pangs. For Rav Hirsch it is a specific fear, of what one's place will be in a
changing order; for Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim the whole body spasming at something
frightening. *Dachil* is the Aramaic word: **fearsome, dread**, typically used by
Targum for *yirah*, *nora* and *pachad*. Rabbi Klein gives two possibilities:
*chil* and *dachil* may simply be Hebrew against Aramaic for one thing, or, in
light of Rav Hirsch, *chil* seems to be the specific fear and *dachil* the more
general one. *Zachal* carries two senses in Tanach, **crawling** and **fear**, and
the fear sense may well be the Aramaic *dachil* Hebraicized, dalet to zayin
(*Metzudas Tzion*, repeated by Rabbi David Golumb and Rabbi Asher Weiser). The
piyut *Yarasi Befitzchi Si* sets all three in rhyme.

## Distinctions

### chil (חיל), chal (חל), chalchalah (חלחלה)
The most common of the three in Biblical Hebrew, meaning fear and the bodily
tremors fear produces. Shiras HaYam: the nations heard and trembled
(*yirgazun*), *chil* seized the dwellers of Peleshes (Shemos 15:14). Of the
nations and Sichon: "they will tremble (*v'ragzu*) and be in dread (*v'chalu*)
before you" (Devarim 2:25).

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 15:14) gives *chil* a specific sense: the fear of the old
guard at how a changing paradigm will affect them. That, he says, is why *chil*
is a fear often associated in Tanach with childbirth (Yirmiyahu 6:24, 22:23,
50:43; Michah 4:9; Tehillim 48:7): the mother fears what her place will be in the
family after the birth of a new child, or perhaps even whether she will survive
childbirth itself. So too the Pelishtim who heard of Krias Yam Suf understood
that Eretz Yisrael was to be conquered, and worried what their situation would
be in that new reality.

Doubled as *chalchal*, the ח-ל string denotes a physical reaction to an
especially frightening situation, trembling or shaking from sheer fright
(Yeshayahu 21:3), as Yechezkel foretells of Cush (Yechezkel 30:4, 30:9). Esther,
hearing of Haman's decrees, *vatischalchal* (Esther 4:4), her body spasming and
shaking from fear; Chazal (Megillah 15a; *Esther Rabbah* 7:14, 8:3) say that on
the spot she became a niddah, or miscarried, or needed to relieve herself,
because she could not control her body in reaction to the news. In line with
this, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *chil* to the biliteral ח-ל, "all-around
movement," to which he traces over thirty terms, and explains *chil* as the
cramping or spasming of the entire body in reaction to something frightening.

### dachil (דחיל)
Root ד-ח-ל, absent from Biblical Hebrew but six times in the Aramaic parts of
Daniel (2:31, 4:2, 5:19, 6:27, 7:7, 7:19), most famously the fourth beast,
*dechilah*, "scary" (Daniel 7:7). Targum typically uses this root to render
Hebrew *yirah*, *nora* and *pachad* (for examples, Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur,
*Meturgeman*); Onkelos renders *chil* at Shemos 15:14 and *chalu* at Devarim 2:25
with *dachla*.

On *chil* against *dachil* Rabbi Klein offers two readings. It is quite possible
that they differ simply as native Hebrew from Aramaic, technically meaning the
same thing. That said, in light of Rav Hirsch, it seems that *chil* is a specific
fear, that of one contemplating his place in a new paradigm, and *dachil* a more
general term for all sorts of fear, hence Targum's use of it for a whole slew of
fear-words. The second reading is his inference from Rav Hirsch's comment on
*chil*. Rabbi Klein adds a speculation of his own, noting that
he has seen no source for it: the only consonantal difference between *chil*
and *dachil* is the initial dalet, so perhaps the two are etymologically related
and the latter took on an extra letter.

Two idioms. In many instances Targum uses *dachalah* for false gods: "gods of
silver and gods of gold" (Shemos 20:20), *elohei* in the Torah, is *dachalan* in
Onkelos. Rabbi Klein reads that as the pagan conception of gods as dreadful,
malevolent forces to be appeased, in stark contrast to the Jewish conception of
Hashem as a loving, kind Creator. And the kabbalistic formula before various
mitzvos, *b'dichilu u'rechimu*, "through fear and love." In Talmudic Aramaic
*dachlulei* is a scarecrow for warding off ravens (Bava Basra 27b with *Toras
Chaim*), Hebraicized in Modern Hebrew as *dachlil*.

### zachal (זחל)
Root ז-ח-ל, the source of the piyut's *azchil*. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*) says the root carries two discrete senses, fear and crawling, without
implying any connection between them. It appears once in Chumash and twice more
in Tanach. In *Haazinu*: "the rage of those who are *zochel* on the dirt"
(Devarim 32:24).
- Crawling: Rashi (there) reads it of poisonous snakes. In Mishnaic Hebrew
  *zochalin* is running or flowing water (*Eruvin* 10:6; *Bava Basra* 2:5, 3:6;
  *Eduyos* 7:3 to 4; *Mikvaos* 1:8, 5:4, 5:5; *Parah* 8:8), and on that basis
  Rashi clarifies that creatures crawling on the ground appear to move in a
  way that resembles water flowing along it. Compare *zachala*, a creeper or worm (the one
  that holed the mountain Og lifted over his head), in Targum Yonasan to Bamidbar
  21:35, and *zachal* in the same sense in *Tosefta Chullin* 3:25 and in Modern
  Hebrew, where a *zachlil* is a caterpillar and also a tank on caterpillar
  tracks, and a child who crawls is *zochel*. Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 32:24), on
  his theory of phonetic substitution (zayin/shin, ches/ayin), ties ז-ח-ל to
  ש-ע-ל, the sole of the foot or a step, both things related to movement.
- Fear: Ibn Ezra (to Devarim 32:24 and Iyov 32:6) agrees the pasuk is about
  snakebite but takes *zochel* itself as fear, all who meet such snakes fearing
  them. A similar Rashi/Ibn Ezra dispute occurs at the third occurrence, Michah
  7:17. Ibn Ezra compares Iyov 32:6, Elihu's "I am young in days and you are
  aged, therefore *zachalti* and I feared to express my understanding to you,"
  and there Rashi too reads fear.
- Fear as *dachil*: it might well be that *zochel* in the fear
  sense is not a separate word from *dachil* but its cognate, since in many cases
  Aramaic dalet answers to Hebrew zayin, as in *dachar*/*zachar* (male),
  *deroa*/*zeroa* (arm), *dahav*/*zahav* (gold); it is then quite possible that
  *zochel* is simply a Hebraicized *dachil*, the same meaning and the same
  etymology. The point is made by *Metzudas Tzion* (to Iyov 32:6) and repeated by
  rabbinic philologists such as Rabbi David Golumb and Rabbi Asher Weiser.
- Two more readings of Elihu's *zachalti*: Rabbi Yosef Kara (there), "I have
  delayed," Elihu explaining why he waited so long to speak, with a similar
  understanding in Ibn Janach's *Sefer HaShorashim*, perhaps, Rabbi Klein
  suggests, related to crawling as the slower way to arrive; and Sforno (there),
  "I have humbled myself as though I were an insignificant creature that crawls
  on its belly," which also harks back to crawling. So three readings of
  *zachalti*: fear, delay, self-abasement.
- Saturn: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*, s.v. ז-ח-ל) notes that in Arabic
  astrology the planet Saturn is called Zachal. Rabbi Shaul Goldman informed
  Rabbi Klein that some have argued the name comes from Zuhal, a pre-Islamic
  Meccan goddess of agriculture. Others note that the Arabic cognate of ז-ח-ל
  yields a verb meaning moving away, withdrawing, retiring, apt for Saturn, the
  farthest planet from the earth in medieval astronomy (Uranus, Neptune and Pluto
  being discovered later). Dr. Tzvi Langermann added in private correspondence
  that the parallel Arabic root can also mean lagging behind or becoming
  fatigued (not unlike Rabbi Yosef Kara's "I have delayed"). This is likewise
  apt, since Saturn's motion in longitude is the slowest of the seven planets.
- A further Rabbinic sense: when Rabbi Oshaya's face lit up in excitement, the
  Yerushalmi (*Sanhedrin* 11:4) says his face was *zachlin*, and the Midrash uses
  *zachalin* of Bar Pedaya (*Koheles Rabbah* 8:1). Rabbi Klein suggests that
  since Saturn appears in the sky as an illuminated body, perhaps its Arabic name
  is related to this sense of light.

## Rendering guidance

- *Chil*: "trembling," "shuddering," or "writhing" for the birth-pang pesukim.
  Not "fear" alone where the body's reaction is the point (Pappenheim); where
  Rav Hirsch's reading governs, the fear of one's place in a new order, say so.
- *Chalchalah* / *vatischalchal*: "convulsed," "was seized with trembling."
  "Was greatly distressed" is too mild, and Megillah 15a shows how strong it
  is.
- *Dachil*: "fearsome" or "dread" when descriptive, "fear" when it renders
  *yirah* or *pachad*. In *b'dichilu u'rechimu* keep the Aramaic and gloss
  "with fear and love" (the column's rendering) or "with awe and love."
- Trap: English "fear" absorbs *chil*, *dachil*, and *zachal*, and the piyut
  *Yarasi Befitzchi Si* (Rabbi Yekusiel bar Moshe, the chazzan's opening
  supplication in Shacharis Shemoneh Esrei on the first day of Rosh Hashanah)
  sets all three in rhyme, plus *nora*. The column translates "awesome" for
  *nora*, "fearsome" for *dachil*, "I trepidate" for *azchil*, "fear" for *chil*;
  a rendering that varies the English keeps the three apart.
- *Zochel* in Devarim 32:24: follow Rashi ("those that crawl in the dust") or
  Ibn Ezra ("those that are feared"), and say which; do not blend them.
