# Trembling: chil / dachil / zachal

## Bottom line

*Chil* is the Hebrew word and it is bodily: **trembling**, the shaking that
fear produces, the same word Tanach uses for a woman's labor pangs. *Dachil* is
the Aramaic word and it is general: **fearful, dread**, and Targum uses it to
render *yirah*, *nora*, and *pachad* alike. *Zachal* carries two senses in
Tanach, **crawling** and **fear**, and the fear sense is very likely the
Aramaic *dachil* with a dalet turned to zayin (*Metzudas Tzion*). The trap is
rendering all three "fear," which is exactly what the piyut that occasioned
this discussion refuses to do.

## Distinctions

### chil (חיל), chal (חל), chalchalah (חלחלה)
The common Biblical term, meaning fear and the bodily tremor fear produces.
Shiras HaYam: the nations heard and shuddered (*yirgazun*), *chil* seized the
dwellers of Peleshes (Shemos 15:14). After Sichon's defeat: "they shall tremble
(*v'ragzu*) and be in dread (*v'chalu*) before you" (Devarim 2:25).

Rav Hirsch (there) gives *chil* a specific sense: the fear of an established
party at a change of order, dread of what one's place will be in the new
reality. That, he says, is why *chil* is the word Tanach uses of childbirth
(Yirmiyahu 6:24, 22:23, 50:43; Michah 4:9; Tehillim 48:7), where the mother
fears both the birth itself and where she will stand in the household after it.
The Pelishtim who heard of Krias Yam Suf understood that Eretz Yisrael was to
be conquered, and feared what that would make of them.

Doubled as *chalchal*, the ח-ל string names the physical convulsion of terror
(Yeshayahu 21:3; Yechezkel 30:4, 30:9). Esther, hearing of Haman's decree,
*vatischalchal* (Esther 4:4); Chazal (Megillah 15a; *Esther Rabbah* 7:14, 8:3)
take that literally, that she lost control of her body on the spot. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim reduces the word to the biliteral ח-ל, movement in every
direction, and defines *chil* as the whole body seizing up in reaction to
something frightening.

### dachil (דחיל)
Root ד-ח-ל, Aramaic, absent from Biblical Hebrew but six times in the Aramaic
of Daniel (2:31, 4:2, 5:19, 6:27, 7:7, 7:19), most famously the fourth beast,
*dechilah* (Daniel 7:7). Targum uses this root as its all-purpose rendering for
Hebrew *yirah*, *nora*, and *pachad* (see Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur, *Meturgeman*);
Onkelos renders *chil* at Shemos 15:14 and *chalu* at Devarim 2:25 with
*dachla*. The natural conclusion is that *chil* and *dachil* differ chiefly as
Hebrew from Aramaic, but with Rav Hirsch's reading in hand the better account
is that *chil* is a specific fear and *dachil* the general one, which is why
Targum can spend it on so many Hebrew words.

Two idioms worth knowing. Targum often renders "gods" (*elohei*) as *dachalan*
(Shemos 20:20), naming the pagan conception of deities as dreadful forces to be
appeased. And the kabbalistic formula before a mitzvah, *b'dichilu u'rechimu*,
"with fear and love." In Talmudic Aramaic *dachlulei* is a scarecrow (Bava
Basra 27b with *Toras Chaim*).

### zachal (זחל)
Root ז-ח-ל. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) records two unrelated
senses, fear and crawling. The root appears once in Chumash, in *Haazinu*: "the
venom of those that *zochel* in the dust" (Devarim 32:24).
- Crawling: Rashi (there) reads it of poisonous snakes, and connects it to the
  Mishnaic *zochalin*, flowing water (*Eruvin* 10:6; *Bava Basra* 2:5, 3:6;
  *Eduyos* 7:3-4; *Mikvaos* 1:8, 5:4, 5:5; *Parah* 8:8), since a creature
  gliding on the ground looks like water running along it. Compare *zachala*, a
  creeping worm, in Targum Yonasan to Bamidbar 21:35, and *zachal* in *Tosefta
  Chullin* 3:25. Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 32:24), on his theory of phonetic
  substitution, ties ז-ח-ל to ש-ע-ל, the sole of the foot or a step.
- Fear: Ibn Ezra (to Devarim 32:24 and Iyov 32:6) agrees the pasuk is about
  snakebite but takes *zochel* itself as fear, all who meet such snakes
  dreading them. The same Rashi/Ibn Ezra split recurs at Michah 7:17. In Iyov
  32:6, Elihu's "I am young in days and you are aged, therefore *zachalti* and
  feared to speak my mind," Rashi too reads fear.
- The likely unification: *Metzudas Tzion* (to Iyov 32:6), followed by Rabbi
  David Golumb and Rabbi Asher Weiser, holds that Hebrew *zachal* in the fear
  sense is the Aramaic *dachil* with the regular Aramaic dalet answering to a
  Hebrew zayin, as in *dachar*/*zachar* (male), *deroa*/*zeroa* (arm),
  *dahav*/*zahav* (gold).
- Two other readings of Elihu's *zachalti*: Rabbi Yosef Kara (there) and Ibn
  Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), "I held back, I delayed," which may still be
  the crawling sense, crawling being the slow way to arrive; and Sforno
  (there), "I made myself low, like a creature that crawls on its belly."
- A further Rabbinic sense: when Rabbi Oshaya's face lit up, the Yerushalmi
  (*Sanhedrin* 11:4) says his face was *zachlin*, and the Midrash uses
  *zachalin* of Bar Pedaya (*Koheles Rabbah* 8:4).

## Rendering guidance

- *Chil*: "trembling," "shuddering," or "writhing" for the birth-pang pesukim.
  Not "fear" alone; the word names what the body does.
- *Chalchalah* / *vatischalchal*: "convulsed," "was seized with trembling."
  "Was greatly distressed" is too mild, and Megillah 15a shows how strong it
  is.
- *Dachil*: "fearsome" or "dread" when descriptive, "fear" when it renders
  *yirah* or *pachad*. In *b'dichilu u'rechimu* keep the Aramaic and gloss
  "with awe and love."
- Trap: English "fear" absorbs *chil*, *dachil*, and *zachal*, and the piyut
  *Yarasi Befitzchi Si* (Rabbi Yekusiel bar Moshe, the chazzan's reshus before
  Shacharis Shemoneh Esrei on Rosh Hashanah) sets all three in rhyme, plus
  *nora*. There the rendering must vary: "awesome" for *nora*, "dread" for
  *dachil*, "I tremble" for *azchil*, "trembling" for *chil*.
- *Zochel* in Devarim 32:24: follow Rashi ("those that crawl in the dust") or
  Ibn Ezra ("those that are dreaded"), and say which; do not blend them.
