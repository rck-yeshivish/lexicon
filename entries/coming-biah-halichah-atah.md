# Coming: bo (biah) / lech (halichah) / atah

## Bottom line

*Bo* is **come**: it looks at the destination, and, as Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
explains, it covers both the arriving and the being on the way. *Lech* is
**go**. Just how the pair divides is not settled: the Tosafists take the makkos
pesukim to suggest that *bo* implies entering where *lech* does not, and the
column offers two theories of its own, that *bo* fixes on the destination and
*lech* on the place of departure, or else that *halichah* names the act of
walking while *biah* names a change of place however it came about. Both of its
own theories the column flags as needing a concordance to test. *Atah* is
**arrive**: like *bo* it faces the destination, but it names only the arrival
and never the journey (Rabbi Pappenheim, cited by Malbim). *Bo* and *atah*
are not interchangeable; where a pasuk has both, *atah* is the one that has
landed.

## Distinctions

### bo / biah (בוא, ביאה)
The Tosafists (*Baal HaTurim* to Shemos 10:1, *Hadar Zekeinim* to Shemos 9:1,
*Moshav Zekeinim* to Shemos 10:1) observe a pattern in the makkos: when Hashem
sends Moshe to Pharaoh in his palace He says *bo el Pharaoh*, and when He sends
him to Pharaoh at the river He says *lech el Pharaoh*. This seems to suggest
that *bo* somehow implies going *inside* a building, while *lech* implies going
somewhere but not necessarily inside (see also Ramban to Shemos 8:15). The same
idea seems to be echoed by the Zohar (Bo 34a), which reads *bo el Pharaoh* as
Hashem bringing Moshe into some sort of Heavenly Inner Chamber somehow bound up
with the sea-creature that characterized Egypt; that mystical reading too seems
to presume that *bo* implies entering and *lech* does not.

Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk offers two further distinctions: *bo* implies coming
to Pharaoh repeatedly, *lech* a single occasion; and *bo* implies coming
willingly, while *lech* is the bare physical act, which can be done against
one's will.

Etymologically, *l'havi* ("to bring") is a conjugation of *bo*, since bringing
a thing is causing it to come; on that basis Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
suggests reading *bo el Pharaoh* as "bring [the following message] to
Pharaoh." Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
derives *navi* from the same two-letter root ב-א, since a navi brings Hashem's
words to his listeners. Rabbi Pappenheim adds that *biah* covers both
reaching the destination ("I came home") and the
approach to it ("I am coming home"). Hence Rachel is described as "coming" while
still on her way to the well (Bereishis 29:9), and Yosef as "coming" while his
brothers still saw him from afar (Bereishis 37:19).

Rabbi Pappenheim and Rabbi Aharon Marcus take *bo* back to the single letter
ב, "inside," the same sense the letter carries as a non-root prefix: one who
comes to a place goes inside it and stops coming, because he has arrived, even
though *bo*/*biah* can still be used of him while he is on the way. The column
adds that this may perhaps give a better understanding of the Tosafists' and the
Zohar's assumption that *bo* implies going inside. Rav Hirsch similarly connects
*bo* to the phonetically similar *po* ("here") through the interchange of beis
and peh: *bo* fixes on the final destination as *po* fixes on the present
location, and he adds *peh* ("mouth"), the terminus into which food is destined
to go.

The *Baal HaTurim* also reads *bo* as a gematria of three, alluding to the three
makkos remaining. Rabbi Menachem Mendel Kasher answers that this cannot be why
the Torah chose the word, since *bo* is used for Moshe going to Pharaoh twice
more (Shemos 7:26, 9:1) when three makkos did not remain.

### lech / halichah (הלך, הליכה)
The counterpart term throughout: on the Tosafists' reading, simply going
somewhere without the sense of entering; on Rabbi Elimelech's, without
repetition and without any implication of willingness. The column then offers
two theories of its own, both as an English speaker's temptation rather than as
a finding. First, that *bo* and *lech* answer to English "come" and "go": *bo*
fixes primarily on one's destination ("come to"), *lech* on one's place of
departure ("go from"). Alternatively, that *biah* denotes a change of place
however it came about, while *halichah* denotes specifically the act of walking.
The column closes the point by saying that a Biblical concordance would have to
be consulted to see how well any of these theories pans out.

### atah (אתה)
A biblical word for coming (Devarim 33:2, Yeshayahu 21:12), appearing in both
the Hebrew and the Aramaic portions of Tanach. Rabbi Pappenheim: *atah* is a
coming with finality. Like *bo* it is oriented to the destination, but unlike
*bo* it refers only to arriving, never to the journey there. The Malbim (*Yair
Ohr*) cites this distinction between *bo* and *atah* in Rabbi Pappenheim's name.

Rabbi Pappenheim derives *atah* from the single letter ת, which stands for
bordering, since it closes the alef-beis. A border links two bodies and
distinguishes them at once; hence *es*, the particle that links a verb to its
object and sometimes means "with," and *os* ("sign"), which links sign to
signified. *Atah* on this reading is the traveler finally joined to his
destination, as against *biah*, which can still have him on the road. The column
adds a parenthesis of its own: perhaps the finality of *atah* is hinted in its
root's being the first and *last* letters of the alef-beis, while *biah* has the
first and the second. The same root yields *tav* ("mark"), which serves as a
border distinguishing a thing from the rest: Yechezkel saw Hashem command an
angel to make a *tav* on the foreheads of the righteous who bemoaned the
abominations in Yerushalayim, which the Gemara (Shabbos 55a) explains to mean
writing the letter tav on them, and the *os* given to Kayin (Bereishis 4:15) is
what some explain to be that same letter. The column notes further that in the
original *Ivri* script the letter tav was written in the shape of the English
letter *x* (and the Greek *chi*), so that as X marks the spot and stands for
what is missing, as in algebra, the tav too marks a thing off as distinguished.

## Rendering guidance

- Keep the English pair honest: *bo* is "come," *lech* is "go." Where the same
  parashah alternates them, as the makkos do, hold the pair even when English
  idiom would prefer otherwise, because the Tosafists' reading of *bo el
  Pharaoh* depends on it.
- *Bo el Pharaoh* in a sefer that follows the Tosafists or the Zohar is better
  rendered "come in to Pharaoh," which keeps the sense of entering.
- *Atah*: "arrive," "has come," "came upon." Not a plain "come," which throws
  away the finality.
- Trap: English "come" absorbs both *bo* and *atah*, and English "go" will
  quietly absorb *bo* whenever the sentence is about travel. If a phrase has
  both (a prophecy of what will *atah* to one who *ba*), render the *bo* as
  coming and the *atah* as arriving, and do not smooth the two together.
- Participles: *ba* of a man still on the road is "coming," not "has come." The
  Rachel and Yosef pesukim are the test cases.
