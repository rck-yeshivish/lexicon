# Rock: even / sela

## Bottom line

*Even* is the general word: **stone**, any naturally occurring one, as against
*leveinah*, a man-made brick (Malbim). *Sela* is a **crag**, a hard rock still
fixed in the ground and often a rocky height; because it is so hard and cannot
easily be chipped, it became the recognized standard for weights and currencies,
and by Mishnaic times it is also the name of a coin. Rabbi Immanuel Chai Ricci
puts the difference in attachment
(*sela* joined to the ground, *even* loose enough to pick up); Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim puts it in hardness, with *even* the hypernym that includes *sela*.
The Mishnah at *Zevachim* 13:3 sets *even* and *sela* side by side.

## Distinctions

Rabbi Klein disclaims any expertise: he is no geologist and does not pretend to
be one. *Zevachim*
13:3 teaches that besides slaughtering a korban outside the Mikdash there is a
separate prohibition of offering one outside it. Rabbi Yosi holds this applies
only to one who erected an altar especially for the purpose and offered on it;
Rabbi Shimon disagrees and holds that even one who offered on an *even* or a
*sela* has violated the prohibition, though it was not an actual altar. What,
then, is the difference between an *even* and a *sela*?

### even (אבן)
Malbim: a stone formed by nature, set against *leveinah*, the brick a man makes
by mixing mortar. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814): *even* is the
umbrella term, the most general way of referring to a rock without being more
specific, and *sela* and the rest fall under it; it sometimes appears in
construct for specific kinds, *avnei hasadeh*, rocks of the field, *avnei
yakar*, precious stones, and *avnei shayish*, usually said to be marble, though
Rabbi Klein notes that in the Judean Formation limestone is a likelier candidate.
Pappenheim argues that the root is the two-letter ב-נ, building, since most rocks are
usable for construction and building is the commonest use of rock. Because rocks
are hard, *even* was then borrowed for any matter in a solid state, as in *avnei
barad*, rocks of hail, at Yehoshua 10:11: hail is not actually a rock but is
hard like one, although, Rabbi Klein adds, his geologist friend Aaron Kahn tells
him that scientifically speaking ice is technically considered a rock. This is
the word in these Yaakov Avinu
narratives: the stones he slept on (Bereishis 28:11), the stone he set up and
poured oil on (28:18), the stone he rolled off the well (29:10), and the phrase
he coined, *Even Yisrael* (49:24).

### sela (סלע)
Rabbi Immanuel Chai Ricci (1688-1743): a *sela* is attached to the ground and an
*even* is detached, so that one can simply pick it up. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim:
rocks are commonly sand, dirt and other mineral deposits tightly compressed
together, and those break easily when shattered with a hammer; other rocks are
harder and cannot be broken so easily, being made of different minerals glued
together with calcium carbonate (calcite) and generally whitish in complexion,
and that kind is a *sela*. To smash a *sela* one must apply intense pressure, so
that when it finally goes its pieces fly outwards and disperse (see Yirmiyahu
23:29).

In most instances in Tanach a *sela* is a hard rock protruding from the
ground, or even a rocky mountain. Because it is so hard and cannot easily be
chipped, it became the recognized standard for determining weights and
currencies, and in the time of the Mishnah *sela* is also the name of a specific
coin. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865), in *HaKesav VeHaKabbalah* on
Shemos 4:25 and Devarim 9:9, cites Rabbi Pappenheim's explanation and adds to it
a layer of exegetical interpretation about the Luchos.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) makes a fascinating
suggestion. He proposes that where a root looks like ס-ל, the samech stands
in for the ayin that follows it in the alef-beis, so the root is really ע-ל, the
root of *al* and *oleh*, what is on top and what rises. On that reading a *sela*
is the rock that *comes up* out of the ground.

## Rendering guidance

- *Zevachim* 13:3, Rabbi Shimon holding one liable for offering "on an *even* or
  a *sela*": "on a stone or a crag." Two English words are required; "on a rock
  or a stone" reads as a distinction without a difference, and the halachah
  turns on there being two cases.
- אבן: "stone," in every register, and especially opposite *leveinah*: "stone
  and brick." (Bereishis 11:3, where the builders of Bavel had brick serve them
  as stone, is our example; the column does not cite it.)
- סלע: "crag" or "rock" where it protrudes or is a height; "*sela*" transliterated
  where it is the coin, never "coin" alone, which loses why the coin has that
  name.
- Trap: English "rock" will take *even*, *sela* and (in the companion article)
  *tzur* and *chalamish* as well. Fix one English word per Hebrew word across a
  passage and hold to it, rather than choosing by what sounds well in each line.
- אבני ברד (Yehoshua 10:11): "hailstones," but if the passage is discussing why
  hail is called stone, render "stones of hail" so the point survives.
