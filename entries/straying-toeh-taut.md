# Straying and error: taah with a tav (תעה) / taah with a tes (טעה)

## Bottom line

The two are pronounced essentially alike and spelled differently. The general
assumption among the commentators is that **תעה (tav) is "lost, stray"** and
**טעה (tes) is "err, make a mistake"**, and throughout Rabbinic literature
*ta'us* always means a mistake. Whether they differ is disputed. Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur holds that the tav word is one who strayed off the path and ended up
lost, and the tes word a mistake of heart or intellect. Rabbi Avraham Aharon
Broide rejects that distinction: the tav word too means erring, and he notes
that Radak himself calls the two the same word. Radak writes, too, that the tav
is the preferred spelling in Scripture and the tes in Rabbinic sources. Rabbi
Yaakov Zev Lev reconciles the positions: in Hebrew the two words differ, but
Aramaic has only one, spelled with a tes, carrying both meanings. Rabbi Shlomo
Aharon Wertheimer also disagrees with HaBachur. Both words, he holds, can
describe one lost on the road, and they differ in how he is lost: the *to'eh*
with a tav is at a complete loss which way to go, while the *to'eh* with a tes
thinks he knows where he is going and has taken the wrong path for the right
one.

## Distinctions

### taah with a tav (תעה)
Words from this root appear some fifty times in Tanach. In the daily *Uva
LeTzion* we thank Hashem who created us for His honor and separated us from
those who are lost, *to'im*.

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (in his glosses to Radak's *Sefer HaShorashim*, as
  cited by Rabbi Broide): the tav word is a person who strayed off the path and
  ended up lost, as against the tes word, which is a mistake in heart or
  intellect.
- Rabbi Avraham Aharon Broide (circa early 19th century, of Vilna and later Rav
  of Strabin near Slutzk; *Bayis HaGadol* to Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer ch. 30)
  rejects that distinction: even the tav word means one who makes a mistake, and
  he cites Tehillim 95:10 and Hoshea 4:12 to that effect. He bolsters this by
  noting that Radak himself, in *Sefer HaShorashim* s.v. תף, explicitly writes
  that *ta'ah* with a tav and with a tes are the same word. The interchange of
  the two spellings was already noted by Radak's father, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi
  (1105 to 1170), in *Sefer Zikaron*, and by Rabbi Shlomo Ephraim Lenchitz (1550
  to 1619) in *Kli Yakar* to Bamidbar 5:12.

### taah with a tes (טעה)
In Tanach a cognate is spelled with a tes in only one place, Yechezkel 13:10,
where the false prophets *hit'u es ami*, and there the sense is ambiguous and
could fit both meanings: they led My people astray, or they led My people into a
mistake.

- Radak yet again writes that the tav and tes words both mean the same thing,
  the former being the preferred orthography in Scripture and the latter in
  Rabbinic sources. In Rabbinic literature *ta'us* always refers to a mistake.
- Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev of Sanhedria HaMurchevet (*Me'at Tzari*) reconciles the
  difference of opinions: in Hebrew there is a difference between the tav and
  tes words, but in Aramaic there is only one word, spelled with a tes, carrying
  both meanings. Rabbi Klein adds that Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur himself already
  acknowledged this in his *Meturgeman*, where he correctly asserts that
  wherever cognates of the tav word appear in Tanach, the Targumim replace the
  tav with a tes.
- Rabbi Yehoshua Alt suggests *to'eh*, mistaken, and *neta*, sapling, are one
  root, because a man's mistake can make him greater.

### How the wanderer is lost (Rabbi Wertheimer)
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935) also disagrees with Rabbi
HaBachur's characterization. Both words can refer to somebody lost on the road,
but he can be lost in different ways. The *to'eh* with a tav is a wayfarer who
has lost his way and is completely at a loss as to the direction he should
travel. The *to'eh* with a tes has lost his way and also thinks he knows where he is going, and has decided the improper path to be the proper one.

### The Targum on Hagar
Bereishis 21:14 says Hagar left *va'teisa*, strayed (tav), in the wilderness of
Be'er Sheva, and Onkelos renders it with a tes. Rabbi Broide's answer, given at
a gathering where no one else offered one: the Targumim generally render idolatrous
deities *ta'avasa* (tes), on the understanding that avodah zarah began as
mankind's mistake (Rambam, Hilchos Avodah Zarah ch. 1); so Onkelos hints at the
tradition (in Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer there and in other sources) that when
Hagar left Avraham's house she returned to the idolatry of her youth. She lost her way and also erred by returning to idolatry. The discussion
illustrates the general assumption that the tav word is "lost" and the tes word
"mistaken."

## Rendering guidance

- תעה: **stray**, **wander**, **be lost**. In *Uva LeTzion*, *to'im* is
  **those who are lost**, as Rabbi Klein renders it.
- טעה / טעות: **err**, **a mistake**. In a sugya, *ta'us* is **an error**,
  never "a straying."
- Causative forms are **mislead, lead astray** for either spelling; Rabbi
  Klein reads the one tes occurrence, Yechezkel 13:10, as open to both
  "led astray" and "led into a mistake."
- Trap: English "astray" and "err" each cover both Hebrew words, and "went
  astray" will quietly decide Yechezkel 13:10 for you. Where a source turns on
  the spelling (a derashah, a Targum comparison), name the letter: "*ta'ah*
  with a tav."
- Second trap: do not translate the tav word as "mistaken" in a passage that is
  arguing Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur's distinction, and do not translate it as
  merely "lost" in a passage arguing Rabbi Broide's. The rendering has to
  follow whichever view the passage is working with.
- Wertheimer's reading gives a usable middle: **lost and at a loss which way
  to go** (tav) against **lost and sure he knows the way** (tes).
