# Straying and error: taah with a tav (תעה) / taah with a tet (טעה)

## Bottom line

The two are essentially pronounced alike and spelled differently, and the
general assumption among the commentators is that **תעה (tav) is "lost,
stray"** and **טעה (tet) is "err, make a mistake"**; throughout Rabbinic
literature *ta'us* always means a mistake. The article does not settle whether
they differ. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur draws the line as straying off the path
against an error of heart or intellect. Rabbi Avraham Aharon Broide rejects
that: the tav word too means erring, and Radak writes that the two are the same
word, the tav being the preferred orthography in Scripture and the tet in
Rabbinic sources. Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev reconciles the positions: Hebrew has two
words, Aramaic only one. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer, also disagreeing with
HaBachur, holds that both can mean one lost on the road and differ in how he is
lost: the *to'eh* with a tav is at a complete loss which way to go, the *to'eh*
with a tet thinks he knows where he is going and has taken the wrong path for
the right one. Our working advice, not the article's: do not build an argument
on the spelling alone, and where a source does turn on it, Wertheimer's
formulation is the easiest to carry into English.

## Distinctions

### taah with a tav (תעה)
Words from this root appear some fifty times in Tanach. In the daily *Uva
LeTzion* we thank Hashem who created us for His honor and separated us from
those who are lost, *to'im*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (in his glosses to Radak's
*Sefer HaShorashim*, as cited by Rabbi Broide): the tav word is a person who
strayed off the path and ended up lost, as against the tet word, which is a
mistake in heart or intellect. Rabbi Avraham Aharon Broide (circa early 19th
century, of Vilna and later Rav of Strabin near Slutzk; *Bayis HaGadol* to
Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer ch. 30) rejects that distinction: even the tav word
means one who makes a mistake, and he cites Tehillim 98:10 as the article
prints it (presumably 95:10; Tehillim 98 has only nine pesukim) and Hoshea
4:12 to that effect. He bolsters this by noting that Radak himself, in *Sefer
HaShorashim* s.v. תף, explicitly writes that *ta'ah* with a tav and with a tet
are the same word. The interchange of the two spellings was already noted by
Radak's father, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (1105 to 1170), in *Sefer Zikaron*, and by
Rabbi Shlomo Ephraim Lenchitz (1550 to 1619) in *Kli Yakar* to Bamidbar 5:12.

### taah with a tet (טעה)
In Tanach a cognate is spelled with a tet in only one place, Yechezkel 13:10,
where the false prophets *hit'u es ami*, and there the sense is ambiguous and
could fit both meanings: they led My people astray, or they led My people into
a mistake. Radak yet again writes that the tav and tet words both mean the same
thing, the former being the preferred orthography in Scripture and the latter
in Rabbinic sources. In Rabbinic literature *ta'us* always refers to a mistake.
Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev of Sanhedria HaMurchevet (*Me'as Tzari*) reconciles the
difference of opinions: in Hebrew there is a difference between the tav and tet
words, but in Aramaic there is only one word, spelled with a tet, carrying both
meanings. The article adds that Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur himself already
acknowledged this in his *Meturgaman*, where he correctly asserts that wherever
cognates of the tav word appear in Tanach, the Targumim replace the tav with a
tet.

### How the wanderer is lost (Rabbi Wertheimer)
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935) also disagrees with Rabbi
HaBachur's characterization. Both words can refer to somebody lost on the road,
but he can be lost in different ways. The *to'eh* with a tav is a wayfarer who
has lost his way and is completely at a loss as to the direction he should
travel. The *to'eh* with a tet has not only lost his way but thinks he knows
where he is going, and has decided the improper path to be the proper one.

### The Targum on Hagar
Bereishis 21:14 says Hagar left *va'teisa*, strayed (tav), in the wilderness of
Be'er Sheva, and Onkelos renders it with a tet. Rabbi Broide's answer, given at
a gathering where no one else offered one: the Targumim render idolatrous
deities *ta'avasa* (tet), on the understanding that avodah zarah began as
mankind's mistake (Rambam, Hilchos Avodah Zarah ch. 1); so Onkelos hints at the
tradition (in Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer there and in other sources) that when
Hagar left Avraham's house she returned to the idolatry of her youth. She did
not only lose her way, she also erred by returning to idolatry. The article
brings the discussion as an illustration of the general assumption that the
tav word is "lost" and the tet word "mistaken."

## Rendering guidance

- תעה: **stray**, **wander**, **be lost**. In *Uva LeTzion*, *to'im* is
  **those who are lost**, as the article renders it.
- טעה / טעות: **err**, **a mistake**. In a sugya, *ta'us* is **an error**,
  never "a straying."
- Causative forms are **mislead, lead astray** for either spelling; the
  article reads the one tet occurrence, Yechezkel 13:10, as open to both
  "led astray" and "led into a mistake."
- Trap: English "astray" and "err" each cover both Hebrew words, and "went
  astray" will quietly decide Yechezkel 13:10 for you. Where a source turns on
  the spelling (a derashah, a Targum comparison), name the letter: "*ta'ah*
  with a tav."
- Second trap: do not translate the tav word as "mistaken" in a passage that is
  arguing Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur's distinction, and do not translate it as
  merely "lost" in a passage arguing Rabbi Broide's. The rendering has to
  follow whichever view the passage is working with.
- Wertheimer's reading gives a usable middle: **lost and at a loss which way
  to go** (tav) against **lost and sure he knows the way** (tet).
