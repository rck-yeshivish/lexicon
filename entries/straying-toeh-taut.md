# Straying and error: taah with a tav (תעה) / taah with a tet (טעה)

## Bottom line

The two are pronounced alike and spelled differently, and the working
assumption of the commentators is that **תעה (tav) is "stray, be lost"** and
**טעה (tet) is "err, make a mistake"**; in Rabbinic Hebrew *ta'us* always means
a mistake. Radak holds outright that they are one word, the tav being the
orthography of Tanach and the tet that of Chazal, so do not build an argument
on the spelling alone. Where a source does distinguish them, follow Rabbi
Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: both men are lost on the road, but the *to'eh* with a
tav knows he is lost, while the *to'eh* with a tet is confident he is on the
right road and is not.

## Distinctions

### taah with a tav (תעה)
Some fifty appearances in Tanach; the daily *Uva LeTzion* uses it of the
nations, *shelo samanu k'mishpechos ha'adamah... im hato'im*. Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur (in his glosses to Radak's *Sefer HaShorashim*): the tav word is a man
who wandered off the path and ended up lost, a bodily straying, as against the
tet word, which is an error of heart or mind. Rabbi Avraham Aharon Broide
(*Bayis HaGadol* to Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer ch. 30) rejects that division: the
tav word also means erring, and he brings Tehillim 95:10 (the article prints
98:10), *am to'ei leivav heim*, a people erring in heart, and Hoshea 4:12,
where a spirit of harlotry *led them astray*. Radak himself, in *Sefer
HaShorashim* s.v. תף, writes that the two spellings are the same word; his
father Rabbi Yosef Kimchi had already said so in *Sefer Zikaron*, as does *Kli
Yakar* to Bamidbar 5:12.

### taah with a tet (טעה)
In Tanach the tet spelling occurs exactly once, Yechezkel 13:10, where the
false prophets *hit'u es ami*, and there the sense is genuinely open: they led
My people astray, or they led My people into error. In Chazal the tet spelling
is the normal one and always means mistake. The Targumim go further: every
cognate of the tav word in Tanach is rendered with a tet, as Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur notes in his *Meturgaman*. Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev (*Me'as Tzari*)
reconciles the dispute this way: Hebrew has two words, Aramaic has only one,
spelled with a tet and carrying both senses.

### The Targum on Hagar
Bereishis 21:14 says Hagar left *va'teisa*, strayed (tav), in the wilderness of
Be'er Sheva, and Onkelos renders it with a tet. Rabbi Broide's answer, given at
a gathering where no one else offered one: the Targumim render idolatrous
deities *ta'avasa* (tet), on the understanding that avodah zarah began as
mankind's mistake (Rambam, Hilchos Avodah Zarah ch. 1); so Onkelos hints at the
tradition of Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer that Hagar returned to the idolatry of her
youth. She did not only lose her way, she erred.

## Rendering guidance

- תעה: **stray**, **wander**, **be lost**. In *Uva LeTzion*, *hato'im* is
  **those who go astray**.
- טעה / טעות: **err**, **a mistake**. In a sugya, *ta'us* is **an error**,
  never "a straying."
- Causative forms are **mislead, lead astray** for either spelling; that is
  where the two come closest.
- Trap: English "astray" and "err" each cover both Hebrew words, and "went
  astray" will quietly decide Yechezkel 13:10 for you. Where a source turns on
  the spelling (a derashah, a Targum comparison), name the letter: "*ta'ah*
  with a tav."
- Second trap: do not translate the tav word as "mistaken" in a passage that is
  arguing Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur's distinction, and do not translate it as
  merely "lost" in a passage arguing Rabbi Broide's. The rendering has to
  follow whichever view the passage is working with.
- Wertheimer's reading gives the useful middle: **lost and knows it** (tav)
  against **lost and sure he is not** (tet).
