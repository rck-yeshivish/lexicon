# Palace: zvul / armon / apadna / tirah (paltin)

## Bottom line

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein offers a way of differentiating them. *Armon* is the
general word and covers both English senses, **palace** and **castle**. *Apadna*
seems to line up with the luxury sense: a **palace**, or in the Gemara a grand
hall or room. *Tirah* seems to be associated with the defensive sense: a
**fortress** or fortified enclosure. *Paltin* is the late Hebrew for a
**palace** in the imperial sense. *Zvul* is a **lofty abode** (Rabbi Dovid Tzvi
Hoffmann's suggestion), the dwelling that perfectly suits the one it is meant
for (Rav Hirsch), and it is used most often of the Beis HaMikdash. Where it sits
relative to the other four Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein leaves open: the term
*zvul*, he writes, "somehow fits in to all of this, I'm just not sure exactly
how."

## Distinctions

### zvul (זבול)
Named at Leah's naming of Zevulun, "this time my husband will dwell with me
(*yizbeleini*)" (Bereishis 30:20). Two further names in Tanach seem to be
related: Zevul, the city official in Shechem who stayed loyal to Avimelech
(Shoftim 9), and Izevel, the Tyrian princess who married Achav and pushed him
toward avodah zarah.

- Rav Hirsch (there): *zvul* is not a mere dwelling but a home that fully
  answers every purpose of the one it is meant for. Hence its use for the Beis
  HaMikdash (so *Rosh Hashanah* 17a; and see Melachim I 8:13, Divrei HaYamim II
  6:2, Yeshayahu 63:15): the fitting place for the Shechinah to rest. He
  connects the Mishnaic *zevel* (fertilizer), which likewise readies the ground
  to be a fitting dwelling for seed so that it may flourish there.
- When Chavakuk, looking back at Yehoshua stopping the sun and moon (Yehoshua
  10:13), says that the sun and moon stood in their *zvul* (3:11), the
  commentators, Targum and Radak among them, explain it as the place where they
  dwell, that is, they stood still in their tracks.
- The Gemara (Chagigah 12b) gives Zevul as the name of one of the seven heavens.

In Ugaritic, Akkadian and other Semitic languages, cognates of *zvul* (*zabalu*)
mean to lift or to elevate. On that basis Rabbi Dovid Tzvi Hoffmann suggests
that the biblical *zvul* means specifically a lofty abode set in a high place:
the Beis HaMikdash on its mountain, the luminaries in the sky. He notes too an
Arabic cognate that means butter or grease, oily food rising to the top when
mixed with water.

### armon (ארמון)
The most common word, 32 times in Tanach.

- Ibn Janach and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim put the root at ר-ו-ם, height, palaces
  being tall buildings.
- Radak cites that and then concludes that *armon* has a root of its own, א-ר-ם.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk assigns it the four-letter root א-ר-מ-ן.
- Ibn Janach, invoking the interchange of lamed and reish, holds that *almon*
  (Yechezkel 19:7, Yeshayahu 13:22) is the same word.

*Armon* appears both for a king's residence
(Melachim I 16:18, Melachim II 15:25, Yirmiyahu 49:27) and for a fortified
stronghold (Mishlei 18:19, Eichah 2:5, Michah 5:4). It therefore straddles both
English words.

### apadna (אפדנא)
Once in Tanach (Daniel 11:45), where the meforshim explain it as a palace. In
the Gemara it is sometimes not a whole palace but a grand or kingly room: Rashi
(Bava Metzia 35a, Bava Basra 6b, Shabbos 77b) defines *apadna* as a royal
triclinium, a fine dining room. It appears in Rava's dream that his *apadna* and
Abaye's had fallen (Berachos 56a), in the debtor who owned two of them (Kesubos
91b), and in the cases where Rav Nachman let creditors seize a man's *apadna*
(Bava Kamma 21a, Bava Metzia 35a, Kesubos 50b).

Its origin is disputed.

- Rabbi Ernest Klein takes *apadna* from Old Persian, a language of the
  Indo-European family.
- Dr. Chaim Tawil traces it instead to the Akkadian *appadanu*, which he
  understands as a colonnaded audience hall, and holds the word to be of Semitic
  origin and not Indo-European.

An Arabic cognate, *fadan*, means palace or high tower.

Rabbi Pappenheim traces *apadna* to the biliteral פ-ד, redemption or freedom
(as in *pidyon*, *pedus*). From that root he takes *eifod*, the vestment worn by
those who were free and unindentured (Shmuel I 2:18, Shmuel II 6:14); an
*apadna* is accordingly the luxurious home of a free man. He extends this,
suggesting that *padan* is a region with its own sovereignty or exempt from
imperial tax, so that
*Padan Aram* (Bereishis 31:18, 35:26, 46:15) is the free state of Aram. The
classical readings of *padan* are "field, plain," or the twin cities of Aram
Naharayim and Aram Tzova.

### tirah (טירה)
Seven times in Tanach (Bereishis 25:16, Bamidbar 31:10, Yechezkel 25:4, 46:23,
Shir HaShirim 8:9, Tehillim 69:26, Divrei HaYamim I 6:39), apparently an
enclosed fort, perhaps a fortified village. Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis 25:16) says it means
the same as *armon*.

- Rabbi Pappenheim puts its root at ט-ר, a straight line, which yields *tur*
  (row), *matarah* (target, shot at straight), and *matar* (rain, falling
  straight down); a *tirah* is typically built by arranging rows of boards.
- Radak likewise derives *tirah* from *tur*, adding that such structures are
  typically built of rows of hewn stone.
- Rabbi Pappenheim offers a second route from the same root, through *netirah*
  (guarding, keeping watch), since watching over something requires keeping
  one's thoughts straight on it without deviating; related are *tahor* (pure, a
  state kept only by watching) and *iter* (shut, so closely guarded that it is
  locked up entirely). On this second reading a *tirah* is an impenetrable
  fortress that none can enter or leave.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche writes similarly that *tirah* is from ט-ר in the
  sense of guarding, a fort built to protect a king's subjects, generally walled
  about, and so akin to *atarah* (crown), the ornament that goes around a royal
  head, which he derives from a combination of the roots ע-ט, cover, and ט-ר,
  guard.
- Rabbi Chelouche, the late Chief Rabbi of Netanya, also claims that the English
  *tower* comes from *tirah*, though linguists derive *tower* from the Latin
  *turris*.

### paltin / paltrin (פלטין)
The late Hebrew word (Sanhedrin 2:3, 10:5), the cognate of the English *palace*.
English *palace*, *palatium*, in the sense of a vast and luxurious residence
belonging to an important person, comes ultimately from the proper name
Palatium, one of the hills of ancient Rome on which the imperial palaces were
built. A renowned language scholar derives the late Hebrew
*paltrin* rather from the Latin *praetorium*, the official residence of a Roman
governor or general, apparently through the interchange of lamed and reish.

English *castle*, by contrast, related to the French *château* and ultimately
from the Latin *castellum*, originally meant an encampment or a village, and
only later came to mean a building or group of buildings put up for defense,
that is, a fortress or stronghold. The two often converge in one building, but
*palace* names a royal residence and the opulence that goes with wealth, while
*castle* names a fortified structure whatever its lavishness.

## Rendering guidance

- Default *armon* to **palace** in a royal context and **stronghold** or
  **fortress** in a military one; the word carries both, so let the pasuk
  decide. Mishlei 18:19 and Eichah 2:5 are the fortress cases.
- *Tirah*: **fortress**, **citadel**, or "fortified encampment." Do not render
  it "palace," even though Ibn Ezra equates it with *armon*, unless you are
  translating Ibn Ezra.
- *Apadna*: **palace** in Daniel; in the Gemara often **grand hall** or
  **mansion**, and where Rashi's triclinium is meant, "banquet room."
- *Zvul*: in a pasuk about the Mikdash, **abode** or "lofty abode," and in
  Chavakuk 3:11 "their dwelling place." Not "palace," which loses Rav Hirsch's
  point that the word means a fitting dwelling and not a grand one. Rabbi Reuven
  Chaim Klein does not place *zvul* against the other four, so do not build a contrast
  between them into a translation.
- Trap: English "palace" will absorb *armon*, *apadna*, *paltin*, and even
  *zvul*. Where two of them stand in one passage, keep "palace" for the
  luxury word and move *armon* to "stronghold" or *tirah* to "fortress", and
  hold the assignment for the rest of the passage.
- The English pair itself is worth keeping straight: a *palace* is a royal
  residence and the wealth that goes with it, a *castle* is a fortified
  structure however plain. *Apadna* lines up with the first, *tirah* with the
  second, and *armon* with both.
