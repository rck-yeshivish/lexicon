# Palace: zvul / armon / apadna / tirah (paltin)

## Bottom line

*Armon* is the general word and covers both English senses, **palace** and
**castle**, so it is the safe default. *Apadna* is the luxury sense alone: a
**palace**, or in the Gemara a grand hall or room. *Tirah* is the defensive
sense: a **fortress** or fortified enclosure, never a byword for opulence.
*Paltin* is the late Hebrew for a **palace** in the imperial sense. *Zvul* is
not really in this set: it is a **lofty abode**, the dwelling that perfectly
suits the one it is meant for, and it is used almost always of the Beis
HaMikdash.

## Distinctions

### zvul (זבול)
Named at Leah's naming of Zevulun, "this time my husband will dwell with me
(*yizbeleini*)" (Bereishis 30:20). Rav Hirsch (there): *zvul* is not a mere
dwelling but a home that fully answers every purpose of the one it is meant for.
Hence its use for the Beis HaMikdash (Melachim I 8:13, Divrei HaYamim II 6:2,
Yeshayahu 63:15): the fitting place for the Shechinah to rest. He connects the
Mishnaic *zevel* (fertilizer), which likewise readies the ground to be a fitting
dwelling for seed. When Chavakuk says the sun and moon stood in their *zvul*
(3:11), Targum and Radak explain it as the place where they dwell, that is, they
stood still in their tracks. The Gemara (Chagigah 12b) counts Zevul among the
seven heavens, the one that holds Yerushalayim and the Beis HaMikdash.

Rabbi Dovid Tzvi Hoffmann understands the biblical *zvul* as specifically a
lofty abode set in a high place: the Beis HaMikdash on its mountain, the
luminaries in the sky.

### armon (ארמון)
The most common word, 32 times in Tanach. Ibn Janach and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
put the root at ר-ו-ם, height, palaces being tall buildings. Radak cites that
and then concludes that *armon* has a root of its own, א-ר-מ; Menachem Ibn Saruk
assigns it the four-letter root א-ר-מ-נ. Ibn Janach, invoking the interchange of
lamed and reish, holds that *almon* (Yechezkel 19:7, Yeshayahu 13:22) is the
same word.

What settles its rendering is usage: *armon* appears both for a king's residence
(Melachim II 16:18, Melachim I 15:25, Yirmiyahu 49:27) and for a fortified
stronghold (Mishlei 18:19, Eichah 2:5, Michah 5:4). It therefore straddles both
English words.

### apadna (אפדנא)
Once in Tanach (Daniel 11:45), where the meforshim explain it as a palace. In
the Gemara it is often not a whole palace but a grand or kingly room: Rashi
(Bava Metzia 35a, Bava Basra 6b, Shabbos 77b) defines *apadna* as a royal
triclinium, a fine dining room. It appears in Rava's dream that his *apadna* and
Abaye's had fallen (Berachos 56a), in the debtor who owned two of them (Kesubos
91b), and in the cases where Rav Nachman let creditors seize a man's *apadna*
(Bava Kamma 21a, Bava Metzia 35a, Kesubos 50b).

Rabbi Pappenheim traces *apadna* to the biliteral פ-ד, redemption or freedom
(as in *pidyon*, *pedus*). From that root he takes *eifod*, the vestment worn by
those who were free and unindentured (Shmuel I 2:18, Shmuel II 6:14); an
*apadna* is accordingly the luxurious home of a free man. He extends this to
*padan*, a region with its own sovereignty or exempt from imperial tax, so that
*Padan Aram* (Bereishis 31:18, 35:26, 46:15) is the free state of Aram. The
classical readings of *padan* are "field, plain," or the twin cities of Aram
Naharayim and Aram Tzova.

### tirah (טירה)
Seven times in Tanach (Bereishis 25:16, Bamidbar 31:10, Yechezkel 25:4, 46:23,
Shir HaShirim 8:9, Tehillim 69:26, Divrei HaYamim I 6:39), apparently an
enclosed fort or fortified village. Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis 25:16) says it means
the same as *armon*.

Rabbi Pappenheim puts its root at ט-ר, a straight line, which yields *tur*
(row), *matarah* (target, shot at straight), and *matar* (rain, falling
straight down); a *tirah* is built by arranging rows of boards. Radak likewise
derives *tirah* from *tur*, adding that such structures are built of rows of
hewn stone. Rabbi Pappenheim offers a second route from the same root, through
*netirah* (guarding, keeping watch), since watching over something requires
keeping one's thoughts straight on it without deviating; related are *tahor*
(pure, a state kept only by watching) and *iter* (shut, so closely guarded that
it is locked up entirely). On this second reading a *tirah* is an impenetrable
fortress that none can enter or leave. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche writes
similarly that *tirah* is from ט-ר in the sense of guarding, a fort built to
protect a king's subjects, generally walled about, and so akin to *atarah*
(crown), the ornament that goes around a royal head.

### paltin / paltrin (פלטין)
The late Hebrew word (Sanhedrin 2:3, 10:5), corresponding to the imperial
palace of Rome and cognate with the English *palace*.

## Rendering guidance

- Default *armon* to **palace** in a royal context and **stronghold** or
  **fortress** in a military one; the word carries both, so let the pasuk
  decide. Mishlei 18:19 and Eichah 2:5 are the fortress cases.
- *Tirah*: **fortress**, **citadel**, or "fortified encampment." Do not render
  it "palace," even though Ibn Ezra equates it with *armon*, unless you are
  translating Ibn Ezra.
- *Apadna*: **palace** in Daniel; in the Gemara often **grand hall** or
  **mansion**, and where Rashi's triclinium is meant, "banquet room."
- *Zvul*: in a pasuk about the Mikdash, **abode** or "lofty abode," and in
  Chavakuk 3:11 "their dwelling place." Not "palace," which loses Rav Hirsch's
  point that the word means a fitting dwelling and not a grand one.
- Trap: English "palace" will absorb *armon*, *apadna*, *paltin*, and even
  *zvul*. Where two of them stand in one passage, keep "palace" for the
  luxury word and move *armon* to "stronghold" or *tirah* to "fortress", and
  hold the assignment for the rest of the passage.
- The English pair itself is worth keeping straight: a *palace* is a royal
  residence and the wealth that goes with it, a *castle* is a fortified
  structure however plain. *Apadna* lines up with the first, *tirah* with the
  second, and *armon* with both.
