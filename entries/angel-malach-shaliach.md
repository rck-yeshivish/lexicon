# Angel / messenger: malach / shaliach / tzir

## Bottom line

*Malach* is a **messenger** who stands in for the sender on a mission the
sender would do himself (Pappenheim, Malbim); of Hashem's messengers it is an
**angel**, and the same Hebrew word covers both. *Shaliach* is the plain
**agent** or **emissary**, one who is sent (Rav Hirsch: merely a messenger; the
Lissa Rav: one who chose to accept the mission). *Tzir* is an **envoy**, a
diplomatic messenger who reports back (Malbim). The one thing not to get
wrong: *malach* is not automatically "angel"; where the Midrash disputes
whether Yaakov's *malachim* were angels or men, the English must leave the
question open.

## Distinctions

### malach (מלאך)
Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the root is ל-א-ך and the mem is not radical, which
the column calls somewhat perplexing since no other Hebrew word has this root. It
adds that archaeology has proven Radak correct: texts found at the ancient city
of Ugarit, in modern-day Lebanon, are written in a Semitic language closely
resembling Hebrew, and there the root of the verb for sending is not ש-ל-ח as in
Hebrew but exactly ל-א-ך. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814), in an
approach the column calls similar to Radak's:
from ל-ך, "go." Since "go" and "send" are near-identical, the difference from
*shaliach* lies in the mission, and Pappenheim and Malbim agree: a *malach* is
charged with the kind of task the sender would do himself, so the assignment is
prestigious and the *malach* holds a position of honor rather than
subservience; Malbim adds that a *malach* is not required to report back. From
this primary sense the word is applied to the angels Hashem *sends* to work
miracles or carry word to prophets. Ralbag, throughout his commentary to Tanach,
consistently tries to explain every *malach* mentioned as a prophet and not an
angel (and see Rambam, Moreh Nevuchim 2:6, for his view of angelology).
Mecklenburg:
*malach* is kin to *melachah*, creative labor, an agent carrying out a task.
Rav Hirsch: *melachah* is the thinking man's power to realize his ideas through
work; *malach* is that power personified in a proxy; and the word sometimes
names Hashem Himself, who is ever engaged in the *melachah* of creation. On
Yaakov's *malachim* to Eisav (Bereishis 32:4), Bereishis Rabbah 75:4 records a
dispute, angels or men; Rashi takes angels.

### shaliach (שליח)
Not in Tanach, but clearly a conjugation of *shalach*, "send." On Rav Hirsch's
approach a *shaliach* simply denotes a messenger, while *malach* denotes the
messenger's role in replacing the one who sent him. Rabbi Yaakov Lorberbaum of
Lissa: a *shaliach* is given the
choice to accept the mission and, having accepted, acts of his own volition;
Hashem's angelic forces are named by task, a *malach* for benevolent missions
and a *mashchis* for malevolent ones, so a *malach* is a kind of *shaliach*.

### tzir (ציר)
Fewer than ten occurrences. The Vilna Gaon (to Mishlei 13:17) implies that a
*tzir* is specifically an agent who has been used many times, a professional
courier, which slightly separates the word from *shaliach*. The column adds that
*tzir* is always used of a messenger in a diplomatic setting, an envoy,
ambassador or attaché. Pappenheim: the word refers to the idea of attaching
distant political entities, and he finds other words related to *tzir* that carry
the concept of connection. Malbim: a *tzir* must not only relay a message for his
dispatcher but report back to him (contrast *malach*).

Aramaic has two words for sending: *shagar* (whose primary meaning seems to be
"flow", and from which Modern Hebrew takes *shagrir*, ambassador) and *shadar*,
which entered the Hebrew vernacular as a contraction of *shaliach d'rabbanan*,
emissary of the rabbis, used of rabbinic envoys sent to collect charitable
donations for public institutions. In Modern Hebrew *shadar* means to broadcast,
in the radio and television sense.

## Rendering guidance

- מלאך of Hashem, or where the context is plainly celestial: "angel." מלאך
  sent by a man: "messenger," "envoy." Where the identity is disputed
  (Yaakov's *malachim*; the three at Avraham's tent by some readings), write
  "messengers" and let a note carry the dispute.
- שליח: "agent" in halachah (*shaliach* of a get, *shelichus*); "emissary,"
  "messenger" in narrative; transliterate *shaliach* when the halachic
  concept is the subject.
- ציר: "envoy," "emissary"; in Mishlei 13:17 (*tzir emunim*) "a faithful
  envoy."
- משחית: "destroyer," "destroying angel"; never "angel" bare.
- Trap: English "messenger" absorbs *malach*, *shaliach*, and *tzir*; and
  "angel" absorbs *malach* and *mashchis*. When two stand together, or when a
  derashah asks why the pasuk says *malach* and not *shaliach*, keep each
  word its own English: *malach* stands in for the sender, *shaliach* is sent,
  *tzir* carries word both ways.
