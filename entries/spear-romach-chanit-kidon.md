# Spear: romach / chanit / kidon (alunki)

## Bottom line

*Romach* is the **spear** or **lance** that Pinchas took (Bamidbar 25:7), the
only one of the three words in the Chumash. It is commonly translated "spear,"
and HALOT glosses it "lance." *Chanit* is the **spear**, HALOT's gloss as well;
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim explains it as the weapon commonly found in the military
camp, from the root for encamping. *Kidon* is a small *romach* (Rambam), usually
thrown at the enemy (Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi), so a **javelin** or **dart**;
HALOT instead gives "hunting knife/scimitar," and Targum renders it *romcha*.
Rabbi Shmuel Krauss holds that *romach*, *chanit* and *kidon* all name the same
weapon, and that the rabbis had a fourth word for it, *alunki*, from Greek: the
rabbinic **lance**.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether these are three weapons or one.**
- Rabbi Shmuel Krauss (1866 to 1948) writes that *romach*, *chanit*, and *kidon*
  are all synonyms for the same sort of weapon, and adds that the rabbis used a
  fourth, *alunki*.
- Targum to Yehoshua 8:18 renders *kidon* as *romcha*, implying that those two
  weapons are very similar and their names can even be somewhat interchanged.
- HALOT gives each word its own English: *romach* "lance," *chanit* "spear,"
  *kidon* "hunting knife/scimitar."

**How *romach* is built.**
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970), Yonah Ibn Janach (990 to 1050), and Radak
  (1160 to 1235): a triliteral root REISH-MEM-CHES, used for nothing but this
  weapon.
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (1821 to 1898, *Otzar Nirdafim*): related to
  biliteral MEM-CHES, "erase, annihilate," for the weapon's destructive nature.
- *Ohalei Yehuda*: a single stab of a *romach* is fatal to anyone, exalted or
  lowly, so the word fuses REISH-(VAV)-MEM ("high, exalted") with MEM-CHES,
  lethal even to the highest-ranking. (Kozbi was a Midianite princess and Zimri
  the prince of the tribe of Shimon, yet both fell to Pinchas's *romach*.)
  Alternatively, *Ohalei Yehuda*: REISH-MEM ("high, lift, remove") with CHES-YUD
  (*chai*, "life"), a remover of life.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 15:1, Bamidbar 25:7) follows the triliteralists'
  REISH-MEM-CHES but hears in it the similar *ramah*, "throwing, shooting," CHES
  interchanging with HEI; in his view, it seems, the *romach* is used by
  throwing, not generally by stabbing.

**Whether *kidon* is a weapon or armor.**

- Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (13th century, in the Holy Land; *HaMadrich
  HaMaspik*): the word usually means a small *romach* thrown at the enemy, like
  an arrow or dart, but Golyas wearing "a copper *kidon* between his shoulders"
  (Shmuel I 17:6) implies it can also mean a helmet. From this Rabbi Reuven
  Chaim Klein suggests that *kidon* may perhaps be a general term for an
  infantryman's gear, weaponry or armor.
- Radak (to Shmuel I 17:6): even with Golyas the *kidon* is an offensive weapon,
  not armor, something resembling a *romach* that soldiers hid between their
  shoulders; at Yirmiyahu 50:42 Radak seems unsure which of the two it is.

**Where *chanit* comes from.**

- Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer HaShorashim*): triliteral
  CHES-NUN-HEI ("camping, resting, store"); Menachem Ibn Saruk, a biliteralist,
  does not list the word at all.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814), a biliteralist: CHES-NUN, camping as
  against travelling, the weapon commonly found in the *machaneh* and used for
  military purposes.
- Then three more: Rabbi Tedeschi-Ashkenazi, from CHES-NUN-HEI, the spear often
  stabbed into the ground where it would be "camped"; *Shoresh Yesha*, from the
  same root, the weapon military camps fear; and *Ohalei Yehuda*, related to
  *chadah*, "sharp, pointed," which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein supposes is
  probably by the interchange of NUN and DALET.

## Distinctions

### romach (רמח)
Once in the Chumash (Bamidbar 25:7), fourteen more times elsewhere in Tanach.
Commonly translated "spear"; HALOT, "lance." Tedeschi-Ashkenazi's and *Ohalei
Yehuda*'s derivations make it the lethal weapon (annihilating, life-removing,
effective even against the most exalted target); Rav Hirsch's makes it, it
seems, a thrown one; for the classical lexicographers the root names this weapon
and nothing else.

### chanit (חנית)
Forty-seven times in Tanach, never in the Chumash; HALOT, "spear." Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim builds a family from CHES-NUN: *machaneh*, a united party travelling
from place to place that has collectively halted and made camp, and so a
military camp, many individuals working together to capture enemy territory or
defend the homeland; *anachnu*, parsed as *ana* (cognate with *ani*, *anochi*)
plus *chnu*, "we who are camping together"; *chen*, favor, since one who finds
favor is granted room to "camp out" in another's thoughts; and *techinah* and
*tachanun*, petitions to be granted *chen*.

### kidon (כידון)
Nine times in Tanach: Yehoshua 8:18, 8:26; Shmuel I 17:6, 17:45; Yirmiyahu 6:23,
50:42; Iyov 39:23, 41:21. Never in the Chumash. HALOT, "hunting knife/scimitar."
- The classical lexicographers: root KAF-YUD-DALET; since *kid* from that root
  means "misfortune, suffering" (Iyov 21:20), Ibn Janach suggests the *kidon* is
  named for bringing misfortune on one's enemies.
- Rambam (to Keilim 11:8): a small *romach*, the weapon kings and aristocrats
  typically carried at all times. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests that perhaps
  Rambam read the word as *yad*, "hand," with the KAF a *kaf hadimyon*: a weapon
  always with its owner, as a hand is.
- The philologist Rabbi Ernest Klein (no relation to Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein):
  probably related to Arabic *kadda* and Ethiopic *kedda*, verbs for
  "thrusting."
- In a somewhat similar way Rabbi Yehoshua (Jeremy) Steinberg of the Veromemanu
  Foundation proposes a metathesis of DALET-KAF, "smashed," for how a *kidon*
  might smite the enemy.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: from biliteral KAF-DALET, a vessel with ear-like
  handles protruding sideways, whose most basic derivative is *kad*, a jug; in a
  borrowed sense the *kidon* is a *romach* with spikes protruding from its sides
  like a jug's handles (a third derivative is *neched*, "nephew," a side
  offshoot of the family tree).

### alunki (אלונקי)
Rabbinic only. Rabbi Shmuel Krauss counts it the rabbis' fourth synonym for this
weapon; the example given is Shmuel's statement that one struck by a Persian
*alunki* will not live (Gittin 70a). Rabbi Krauss derives *alunki* from Greek
*lonche*, the etymon of English "lance." In the Persian version the points were tipped with poison, so one struck by it is
unlikely to survive.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "spear" will swallow all three. Following HALOT, "spear" can be
  reserved for *chanit* and "lance" for *romach* (though the common rendering at
  Bamidbar 25:7 is "spear"); give *kidon* English marked as smaller, "javelin"
  or "dart," with Rambam and Rabbi Tanchum, or HALOT's "hunting knife/scimitar."
  Where *kidon* stands beside *chanit* in one verse (Shmuel I 17:45, Iyov
  39:23), separate English keeps the verse from reading as a repetition. For
  Rabbi Krauss the words are synonyms.
- Bamidbar 25:7 is the sole appearance of *romach* in the Chumash.
- Do not carry Modern Hebrew back into a pasuk: modern *kidon* means javelin,
  bayonet, and bicycle handlebar. The first two, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says, are clear
  outgrowths of the Biblical word, since javelin and bayonet are types of spear.
  For the handlebar, Dr. Raphael Sappan (1927 to 1995), an expert in the
  development of Modern Hebrew slang, writes in his additions to Even-Shoshan's
  dictionary that it comes from the phonetic similarity to the French word for a
  handlebar; alternatively, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's son Elimelech Klein argues that a
  biker's hands on the handlebar resemble a javelin thrower's on the javelin.
