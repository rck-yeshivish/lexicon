# The second month: Ziv / Iyar

## Bottom line

The Torah numbers the months rather than naming them, as it does not name the
days of the week either (Ibn Ezra to Devarim 16:1),
so the second month is "the second month." Where Tanach does name it, it is
**Ziv** (Melachim I 6:1, 6:37), a word meaning **radiance**, and specifically
radiance as something given off rather than the source itself (Rabbi Moshe
Shapiro). **Iyar** is the name in use since Bavel and appears nowhere in Tanach.
How to spell Iyar, with
one yud or two, is an unsettled halachic question in a *get*, and the poskim
cited conclude that one should optimally write two.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why the second month is called Ziv.** The Gemara (*Rosh HaShanah* 10b-11a)
ties this to the machlokes over when the Avos were born. All agree Yitzchak was
born in Nissan; Rabbi Eliezer holds Avraham and Yaakov were born in Tishrei,
Rabbi Yehoshua in Nissan.

1. **The splendor of the trees.** Rabbi Eliezer: the month is called Ziv for the
   radiance of the trees, which begin to blossom then. The Yerushalmi (*Rosh
   HaShanah* 1:2) gives a similar reason, that the flora have budded and become
   recognizable.
2. **The splendor of the Avos.** Rabbi Yehoshua: Ziv points to the birth of the
   *zivsanei olam*, the radiant ones of the world, the Avos. Since on his view
   they were born in Nissan, Rashi touches on the apparent difficulty: by the time
   the second month comes around, the fathers born in the month before are
   already born.

Rabbi Klein's own reading joins
them through Rabbi Moshe Shapiro's sense of *ziv*: Nissan is the month of
*nissim*, the light itself; Iyar is what continues to shine from it and is,
after a fashion, a continuation of it, which is why
one who missed the korban Pesach in Nissan can bring Pesach Sheini in the second
month (Bamidbar 9:11), and why the buds are the tree's radiance without being the
trunk. On the same reading the Avos are called the Chariot of Hashem in the
sense that they are not Him, but vehicles for the spread of His will through
creation at large.

**The root of Ziv.** The month-name is spelled defective, while the ordinary
Aramaic word for radiance in Daniel (2:31, 4:33, 5:6, 5:9-10, 7:28) is
consistently spelled plene. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the root is ז-י-ו, even
though the month-name is never written with a yud in Tanach. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*): the root is the two-letter ז-ו, which he
also takes as the etymon of *zavis*, a corner, and the month is Ziv because in
it the sun somehow turns a corner; Rabbi Klein's own guess is that this
may be the entry of the new season of spring.

**Where the name Ziv came from.** Historians and Bible scholars tend to explain
Ziv as the Phoenician and Canaanite name for the second month, simply borrowed
into Tanach. Rabbi Klein notes that, even without resorting to that sort of
speculation, the word *ziv* is in any case Biblical, appearing six times in the
Aramaic of Daniel in the sense of radiance. Scholars trace the name Iyar to the
Akkadian month-name *ayyaru*, meaning "light," which, he notes, seems awfully
close to the Hebrew *ohr*, also light.

**How to spell Iyar in a get.** Because the name is not in Tanach there is no
settled orthography, and the question is whether it takes two yuds (אייר) or one
(איר). The name does appear in the Mishnah (*Rosh HaShanah* 1:3), but the
spelling there is itself disputed: the Kaufmann manuscript, from late
eleventh-century Italy and the oldest vowelized manuscript of the Mishnah, and
the Parma manuscript both have two yuds, while many other popular editions,
*Ein Yaakov* to *Rosh HaShanah* 18a among them, have
one. Maharil (responsa 189), *Terumas HaDeshen* 233, Rabbi Yaakov Margolis
(*Seder HaGet* 17 and 19), *Shulchan Aruch* (*Even HaEzer* 126:23), and Rema
(*Even HaEzer* 126:7) all conclude that one should optimally write two yuds, while
acknowledging that some wrote one; Maharil himself brought a proof for one yud
from the text of Targum Yonasan he had, and Rabbi Margolis notes that our texts
of that Targum always have two. *Nachalas Shivah* rules for two yuds, both from
Targum Yonasan to Bamidbar 9:11, which names the second month Iyar with two, and
from the allusion he found in *Yalkut Chadash* in the name of *Megaleh Amukos*
121, that Iyar can be read as an acronym for Avraham, Yitzchak, Yaakov, and
Rochel, who are said to be the Merkavah of Hashem; *Beis Shmuel* (*Even HaEzer* 126:20) cites the same. Rema
records that on account of this doubt many batei din have the practice of not
writing *gittin* during Iyar at all (see *Aruch HaShulchan*, *Even HaEzer*
126:15).

**What Iyar means.**
- *Sefer HaKushyos*, the work of an anonymous talmid of Rabbi Meir of Rothenburg
  published by Rabbi Y. Y. Stahl, gives two accounts, both from *avir*, air: the
  month's air is commonly neither too hot nor too cold, or else the world was
  left hanging in the air from creation until Israel accepted the Torah in Sivan
  (*Shabbos* 88a), so the month before Sivan is named for air. The rabbinic
  *avir* is itself borrowed from the Greek *aer*, which is also the etymon of
  the English "air."
- Rabbi Yosef Kara (to Melachim I 8:2) and the Maharal (*Chiddushei Aggados* to
  *Yevamos* 62b) tie the name Ziv to that same temperate weather.
- Rabbi Eliezer Hagar of Vizhnitz (*Damesek Eliezer* to Vayikra 9:22) connects
  the one-yud spelling to *ya'er* of Birkas Kohanim (Bamidbar 6:25), an exact
  anagram of it.

## Distinctions

### Ziv (זו) and ziv (זיו)
The name appears twice, both in the account of Shlomo HaMelech building the Beis
HaMikdash: "in the month of Ziv, which is the second month" (Melachim I 6:1), and
"in the fourth year the House of Hashem was founded, in the month of Ziv"
(6:37). The common noun *ziv*, radiance, occurs six times in the Aramaic of
Daniel. Rabbi Moshe Shapiro fixes its sense from the Targumim: Onkelos renders
the rays of glory from Moshe's face (Shemos 34:29) with *ziv*, and Targum Yonasan
renders the glory of Hashem filling all creation (Yeshayahu 6:3) with *ziv*. So
*ziv* is the outward spread of something, light or honor, not the thing itself.
The Levites' Shabbos Mussaf song was Haazinu (Devarim 32:1-43), divided over
successive weeks by the mnemonic *haziv lach* (*Rosh HaShanah* 31a); Maharsha
explains that singing it on the day of the week Moshe died is a declaration of
his *ziv*, the light of his teaching still shining after him.

### Iyar (אייר)
Sefiras HaOmer runs through the whole of this month, which makes it the only
month on the calendar with a mitzvah attached to it from end to end.

The name is not in Tanach. The names of all the months now in use came up from Bavel, by
prophetic revelation according to the Yerushalmi (*Rosh HaShanah* 1:2) and
pseudo-Rashi to *Bereishis Rabbah* 48:9. It appears in the Mishnah (*Rosh
HaShanah* 1:3) and in Targum Yonasan to Bamidbar 9:11. Beyond the *avir*
readings above, three acronyms are attached to it: *Megaleh Amukos*, Avraham,
Yitzchak, Yaakov, Rochel; Rabbi Shimshon of Ostropoli, on Tehillim 6:11, whose
last four words open with the letters of Iyar spelled with two yuds (a check
of the Base HaSefer database, developed by Moshe Escott of
Taryag Analytics, confirms this is the only pasuk in all of Tanach whose words
form an acronym spelling Iyar); and the
Chasam Sofer (to *Shabbos* 147b), on "I will not place upon you all the illness
of Egypt, for I am Hashem your Healer" (Shemos 15:26), whose last three words
give Iyar with one yud. Rabbi Mordechai Nachman Aronovsky reads the pair
together: in Iyar Hashem brings down Israel's enemies and spares Israel itself,
and he cites *Seder Olam Rabbah* ch. 5 that the war with Amalek fell in Iyar. In
the name of Rabbi Dovid Jungreis he adds that the last eight words of Tehillim
6:11 spell *yom kaf Iyar*, the twentieth of Iyar, the day Israel set out from Har
Sinai (Bamidbar 10:11), when Moshe would say "Arise, Hashem, and let Your enemies
be scattered" (Bamidbar 10:35).

## Rendering guidance

- Render the Biblical name as "Ziv," with "the second month" in apposition where
  the pasuk itself supplies it, and keep "radiance" for the common noun.
- Trap: English "splendor" or "glory" will absorb *ziv* into *hod* and *kavod*.
  On Rabbi Moshe Shapiro's reading *ziv* is specifically the emanation from a
  source, so "radiance" is the safer word; reserve "glory" for *kavod*, which is
  what *ziv* is the radiance of in Yeshayahu 6:3.
- In a *get*, the month is written, not translated, and the spelling is the
  poskim's question, not the translator's. Two yuds is the ruling; note the
  minhag of some batei din to avoid Iyar altogether.
- Do not write "Iyar" into a translation of a pasuk that says "the second month";
  the identification belongs in a note, since the name is post-Biblical.
- Where a derashah turns on the letters of Iyar, give the Hebrew, since no
  English can carry an acronym on a Hebrew name.
