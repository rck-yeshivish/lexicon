# Red: adom / shani / chamutz (and the rest)

## Bottom line

*Adom* is plain **red**, and on Radak's comparison at Zechariah 6:2-3 the
stronger red against *chamutz* / *amutz*. *Shani* (*tola'as shani*) is
**crimson**, the worm-dye of the Mishkan. *Chamutz* and *amutz* are the weaker red: **red-stained**
in Yeshayahu, **chestnut** or burgundy in Zechariah. *Seruk* is **pink** on
Malbim's reading, though Radak takes it only as "colorful"; *chum* is a dark red
near black, which later sources read as **brown**; *chachlilei* is a drinker's
**bloodshot** eye, though Ramban ties that word to blue instead. In Aramaic
*sumak* is everyday red and *zehoris* the Targum's word for *shani*.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What *seruk* means.** Radak (to Zechariah 1:8 and *Sefer HaShorashim*) takes
*serukim* as "colorful" in a generic sense, without saying which color; Malbim
(to Zechariah 1:8) explains it as "pink" or "light red." The column reports both
and does not choose.

**Whether *chachlilei* is red.** Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi makes it the
reddened white of the eye, from *kalil*, "crown"; the column notes that others,
like Ramban (to Bereishis 49:12), connect the word instead to *kachol*, blue.
Again the column leaves both standing.

## Distinctions

### adom (אדום)
Root א-ד-ם. The adjective appears nine times in Tanach (Esav's lentil stew,
Bereishis 25:30; the beloved's complexion, Shir HaShirim 5:10; *parah adumah*,
Bamidbar 19:2), with *admoni*, "ruddy," three times, of Esav and of Dovid
HaMelech (Bereishis 25:25; Shmuel I 16:12, 17:42), and *adamdam*, "reddish," six
times of *tzaraas* discolorations in Vayikra 13-14. The verb forms, "to redden"
or "to dye red," appear ten times, a plurality of them the red-dyed hides of the
Mishkan (Shemos 25:5, 26:14, 35:4, 35:23, 36:19, 39:34), with Yeshayahu's
metaphor of sins red as crimson (Yeshayahu 1:18) and Nachum's shields reddened
in battle (Nachum 2:4, see also Eichah 4:7 and Mishlei 23:31). Among the nouns:
*odem*, ruby or carnelian, the red stone of the choshen (Shemos 28:17); *madim*,
Mars, the red planet; *adamdemes*, rubella in Modern Hebrew; and Edom, the second
name of Esav and his descendants, on account of the red lentils (Bereishis
25:30).

### shani (שני) and tola'as shani (תולעת שני)
The dyed wool of the *parah adumah* (Bamidbar 19:6), of the metzora's taharah
(Vayikra 14:4, 14:6, 14:49, 14:51-52), and of the Mishkan throughout Shemos; also
*tola'as hashani*, bare *shani* (Bereishis 38:30; Yehoshua 2:18, 2:21; Shmuel II
1:24; Yeshayahu 1:18; Yirmiyahu 4:30; Shir HaShirim 4:3; Mishlei 31:21), or bare
*tola* (Yeshayahu 1:18, and Eichah 4:5 according to Ibn Ezra and Mahari Kara).
That the shade is crimson, a deep rich red with a slight bluish or purplish
undertone, is made explicit by Rabbi Saadia Gaon, Ibn Ezra, and Rabbi Avraham
Maimuni (in their commentaries to Shemos 25:4), Raavad and Rash M'Shantz (to
*Toras Kohanim*, Metzora), Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*), and Meiri. The dye comes
from a worm, since *tola'as* is the Hebrew for worm (see Yerushalmi *Kilayim* 9:1
and Malbim to Yeshayahu 1:18). Pseudo-Rashi (Divrei HaYamim II 2:6) writes that
*carmil*, or *carmel* in Ibn Janach's and Radak's *Sefer HaShorashim*, refers to
the same crimson cloth as *tola'as shani*, and Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi
sees that word as related to *rimah*, another kind of worm.

The English *crimson* runs on the same idea. The column traces it to the
Proto-Indo-European root *kwrmi-*, "worm" or "mite," reaching English by way of
Old Spanish *cremesin*, from Arabic *qirmiz*, and ultimately from Sanskrit
*krimija*, "produced by a worm," used of a red dye made from scale insects such
as kermes and cochineal. Although Arabic is Semitic and Sanskrit Indo-European,
the column says it *seems* that Arabic borrowed *qirmiz* from Farsi
(*gharmaz*/*germez*), itself Indo-European. Related English words are
*vermilion*, a bright vivid red with a slight orange tint, from Latin
*vermiculus*, "little worm," and *carmine*, a purplish red. The column adds an
aside: he used to think the Ghermezian family name pointed to Ashkenazi origins
in the town of Worms (Germaiza), but on this showing it more likely comes from
the Iranian word for red, which is itself bound up with worms, just not the
German town of that name.

Why *shani* should share the root of *sheini*, "two," is a question the column
takes up. Rabbi Yaakov Hibbert of Manchester *conjectures*, from the wording of
the *Ohr HaChaim* (to Bereishis 38:28), that perhaps crimson relates to "two"
because it was a string of that color that marked Zerach as the *second* son born
to Yehudah and Tamar. The column then reports a different explanation it found in
both Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi's *Otzar Nirdafim* and Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim's *Cheshek Shlomo*: cloth dyed this color was often dyed *twice*, so
that the color would hold more strongly, and that is the link between *shani* and
*sheini*.

### chamutz (חמוץ) and amutz (אמוץ)
Yeshayahu pictures Hashem taking revenge on Edom as one soiled from a bloodbath:
"Who is this who comes from Edom, [the one with] soiled (*chamutz*) clothes from
Batzrah... why are your garments red (*adom*), and your clothes like one who
tramps in the winepress?" (Yeshayahu 63:1-2). Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*), Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), and Radak (in his
*Sefer HaShorashim* and to Yeshayahu 63:1) all write that *chamutz* means "red,"
Radak noting that the meaning can be gleaned from the context, since that is the
color of something stained in blood or wine. In that sense the word, a *hapax
legomenon*, *seems* to have nothing to do with the other words from ח-מ-ץ, which
can mean "leavened," "vinegar," "anger," or "stealing." Probably because of that,
Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi writes that *chamutz* is really a metathesized
form of the root מ-ח-ץ, "stab," "smite," "lacerate," since what that verb
describes can leave a thing reddened with blood; and Radak himself agrees that
*timchatz* (Tehillim 68:24), with those consonants in that order, also refers to
becoming reddened.

Zechariah's *amutzim* horses (6:3) are the same word, since the guttural aleph
and ches are often interchangeable (Ibn Janach, Radak), and since that vision
also has *adumim* horses (6:2), Radak adds that *chamutz* / *amutz* differs from
*adom* in not being as strong a red. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi clarifies
that the shade of *amutz* resembles the color of a chestnut, what we might call
burgundy. He also *suggests* that the root א-מ-ץ in the sense of strength
(*ometz* / *amatz*) may derive from this meaning, since in girding one's strength
the red blood inside begins to boil in the red parts of the innards, a suggestion
the column reports while remarking that it is not sure what it means. Rabbi
Pappenheim instead traces *amutzim* to the biliteral מ-צ, which he defines as
sucking or squeezing, the root also of *mitz*, juice, *metzitzah*, sucking, and
*ometz*, power, in the sense that using that force means squeezing out every last
bit; red horses are *amutzim* because they exert so much effort that their blood
rises to the surface of the skin as if squeezed out, which turns even their hairs
red.

### zerach (זרח), shining and red
Rashbam and Chizkuni (to Bereishis 38:30) explain that the name Zerach means
"red," alluding to the red string tied on his hand at birth. The root ז-ר-ח
generally refers to shining or lighting and is most often associated with the
sun, but it can sometimes be used of something *shining red*: when Uzziah offered
the ketores unlawfully, Scripture reports that "*tzaraas* shined on his forehead"
(Divrei HaYamim II 26:19), and since *tzaraas* often shows as a reddish skin
discoloration, Rashbam holds that shining and red are connected. Rashbam and
Chizkuni also cite Melachim II 3:22 to that effect.

### seruk (שרוק) and the rouge words
*Seruk*: Zechariah's other vision of red horses calls them *susim adumim serukim*
(Zechariah 1:8); *sorek* is a high-quality red grape (Bereishis 49:11, Yeshayahu
5:2, 16:8, Yirmiyahu 2:21). The column notes that this word looks like *zerach*
in another way, since it *seems* cognate to the Arabic *sharaqa*, "shining
brightly," and the Persian *sorkh*, "red" or "scarlet," which again links shining
to red. *Sorek* is also twice a place-name: Delilah is the woman of Nachal Sorek
(Shoftim 16:4), a valley between Ashkelon and Gaza, and one of the kings of Edom
is Samlah of Masreikah (Bereishis 36:36, Divrei HaYamim I 1:47), a name seemingly
from *sorek*, which would make it semantically kin to Edom itself.

In Mishnaic Hebrew *sarak* is red paint or rouge, a woman's cosmetic (Kesubos
17a, Sanhedrin 14a); elsewhere the word is spelled with a samech rather than a
sin (Shabbos 95a, Moed Katan 9b). Rabbi Yosef Noach Wilkover notes that *sikra*,
the red ink or dye of the Mishnah (Shabbos 12:4, Gittin 2:3, Bechoros 9:7, Middos
3:1), is a metathesized form of *sarak*, the same consonants changing places, and
connects both with the biblical root ש-ק-ר, "leering," a *hapax legomenon*
occurring only of the promiscuous women of Yeshayahu 3:16; he seems to explain it
of those women adorning themselves with red cosmetics to draw the eye. The same
triliteral gives *sikur ayin*, the wandering of the eye, and the more modern
*sakranus*, curiosity.

### chum (חום) and shachum (שחום)
*Chum* appears only four times in Tanach, all of the redness or brownness of the
sheep when Yaakov shepherded Lavan's flock (Bereishis 30:32-33, 30:35, 30:40).
Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi explains it as a shade of red closer to black,
"dark red," though in some later sources it seems to mean what we now call brown
or bordeaux; the name is related to *chom*, heat, because it is the color of an
oven as it begins to heat up. Shadal (to Bereishis 30:32) writes that *chum* and
*shachum* are homogeneous terms.

### tzachar (צחר)
When Yechezkel lists the commodities exported from Damascus he mentions *tzachar*
wool (Yechezkel 27:18). The column has elsewhere treated *tzachar* as a cognate
of *tzachor*, "white," but another approach reads it as red: David J. A. Clines'
*A Dictionary of Classical Hebrew* defines *tzachar* as "reddish-grey colour,"
and Rabbi Ernest Klein writes that the Hebrew root צ-ח-ר is related to its Arabic
cognate *asharu*, "yellowish-reddish land," which is the etymon of the name
Sahara. There is even a version of Rashi (to Berachos 31b, as printed in the
Vilna Shas) that says *tzachor* means "red," and Marcus Jastrow's dictionary
defines *tzachor* as "glistening" and "light reddish."

### chamar (חמר)
Tehillim 75:9 describes wine with the adjective *chamar*. Since the Aramaic for
wine is *chamar* / *chamra*, and since here the word must describe some property
of wine rather than be a synonym for it (it would be a tautology to say "winey
wine"), Ibn Janach and Radak in their respective *Sefer HaShorashim*, and Radak
to Tehillim 75:9, explain that *chamar* here means "red," the word primarily
naming the color characteristic of wine. In Arabic too *'ahmar* means "red," and
the column notes that this is the origin of the Spanish place name Alhambra in
Granada, originally *al hamra*, "the red [palace]." Rabbi Ernest Klein writes
that Hebrew *chemar*, asphalt or bitumen, is so called for its reddish-brown
color, and explains *chomer*, matter or material, as originally referring to
clay, which has a reddish hue, a point Ibn Ezra makes similarly in his Long
Commentary to Shemos 2:3. Klein further *suggests* that *chamor*, donkey, and
*yachmur*, roebuck, derive from this word for red, in the sense of a red animal,
adducing as a parallel the Spanish *burro*, which he holds derives ultimately
from Late Latin *burrus*, "red," itself a corruption of Greek *purros*,
"yellowish red" or "flame-colored." (In his English etymological dictionary,
though, Klein does not link *roe* to the Indo-European root for red, deriving it
instead from *rei-* or *reoi-*, "streaky" or "spotted.") The column adds that a
grandson of Seir the Horite was named Chamran (Divrei HaYamim I 1:41), one more
red-associated name among the Edomites, and that the Torah gives him the
alternate name Chemdan (Bereishis 36:26), which can be viewed as equivalent
given the resemblance of reish and dalet.

### kana (קנא)
Shadal (to Shemos 20:5) notes that the Peshitta, the Syriac translation, twice
uses an obscure Aramaic word for "red" spelled ק-נ-א, once to render *chachlilei*
(Mishlei 23:29) and once to render *nichmaru* (Eichah 5:10). On the strength of
that rare Syriac word Shadal *proposes* that the core meaning of the Hebrew
*kana* is "red"; the column adds that *qani* in Classical Arabic describes a deep
crimson or dark red, often in poetic use. On this reading *kana* names the
physiological effect of jealousy, the heart heating up and the face going ruddy
with indignation that another has taken what is his. Applying the word to Hashem
is obviously a linguistic borrowing and no statement of anthropomorphism; Shadal's
point is that where Hashem is described as *kana* over avodah zarah, the more
accurate sense is that He is angry. The column puts it that Hashem is not visited
by the green-eyed monster of jealousy but is rather "seeing red."

### the Aramaic words: sumak (סומק), zehoris (זהורית), gichor (גיחור)
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur in *Meturgaman* gives a whole list of places where the
Targumim render the Hebrew *adom* and *chachlilei* by *sumak*, the standard
Talmudic Aramaic word for red. The famous instance is the rule that one may not
save his own life by killing another: what makes you see that your blood is
redder (*sumak*), perhaps that man's blood is redder (Pesachim 25b, Yoma 82b,
Sanhedrin 74a). The sun at sunrise and sunset is *samka* (Bava Basra 84a). Dr.
Alexander Kohut in *HaAruch HaShaleim* points out several personal names in
rabbinic sources that seem to derive from this word: Sheila bar Sumki (Gittin
69a), Rabbi Abba Samuka (Yerushalmi *Berachos* 9:1), and Rabbi Chelbo ben
Chalifei bar Simkay (*Bereishis Rabbah* 51:2).

For some of the other Hebrew words the Targumim prefer *zehoris*: it renders
*shani* (Bereishis 38:30, Yehoshua 2:18) and *tola* (Yeshayahu 1:18, Eichah 4:5),
and it also names the Mishnah's red string, the *lashon shel zehoris* tied to the
head of the *sa'ir hamishtaleach* (Shabbos 9:3, Shekalim 4:2, Yoma 4:2, 6:6). The
root ז-ה-ר refers variously to shining, illuminating, even the moon, and the
column says frankly that its specific connection to red is *somewhat tenuous*.
Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi, in his dictionary of Rabbinic Hebrew, *partially*
alleviates the problem by writing that *zehoris* is a sort of red that is
especially shiny, or at least more so than other colors.

*Gichor* first appears in the Mishnah (Bechoros 7:6), where it is a blemish that
disqualifies a kohen from serving in the Mikdash but does not disqualify an
animal from being brought as a korban. The Tosefta (*Berachos* 7:3) rules that
one who encounters a *gichor* recites *baruch meshaneh habriyos*, and when Chanah
davened for a son she asked that he be neither too tall nor too short, too thin
nor too fat, too white nor too reddish (*gichor*), too clever nor too foolish
(Berachos 31b). In all of these Rashi (to Berachos 31b, 58b, 59b, Bechoros 45b)
explains *gichor* as "very red," or perhaps deep red. Its etymology is obscure,
and does not seem to be an actual Aramaic or Hebrew word: Dr. Alexander Kohut in
*HaAruch HaShaleim* sees it as Indo-Iranian, connecting it with the Bactrian
*cukhra* and the Old Persian *cuhar*, both apparently meaning red, while Rabbi
Ernest Klein simply calls it "of unknown origin." The column adds, as an aside,
that one of the family heads who returned in the days of Zerubavel was named
Gachar (Ezra 2:47, Nechemiah 7:49), and that we can only speculate whether that
means he was of reddish complexion.

### argaman (ארגמן), and red clothing
Shadal (to Shemos 25:4) writes that *argavan* is a form of *al-gavan*, on the
interchangeability of reish and lamed, *al* serving as the definite article as it
does in Arabic, so that the word means "*the* color," red sticking out more than
other colors and so being the superlative color. Separately, various Yemenite
exegetes (*Midrash Chefetz* and *Meor Ha'Afeilah* to Shemos 25:4) follow the
approach that *argaman* is a shade of red and then must explain how it differs
visually from *tola'as shani*, which is also red: *argaman* is a yellowish red,
perhaps nearer what we would call orange, while *tola'as shani* is the stronger,
deeper red. (Shadal's point and the Yemenite exegetes' are two different claims;
only the latter is about the shade.)

Perhaps because red is so ostentatious and attention-grabbing, the Rema (*Yoreh
Deah* 178:1) rules that a Jew should not wear red clothes; and the Gemara
(Berachos 20a) records a rabbi who risked his life to stop a woman he took for
Jewish from wearing a red garment called a *karbalta*, literally a rooster's
crest, as *Sefer HaAruch* and Rabbeinu Chananel to Berachos 20a explain.

### Rashi's Old French
The column closes by noting that Rashi uses the Old French *russ* to render
*adom* (Bereishis 25:25, Vayikra 13:39), *gichor* (Berachos 31b, 58b, 59b,
Bechoros 45b), *shachum* (Bereishis 30:32, Avodah Zarah 33b), *sumka* (Bechoros
45b), and *tola'as shani* (Gittin 69b). That Romance word derives from the
Proto-Indo-European root *reudh-*, "red," the ancestor of English *red*, *rose*,
*rouge*, *rosy* and *rust*, and, through the Latin shift of the *dh* to a *b*, of
*ruby*, *rubric*, *rubella* and the rest.

## Rendering guidance

- תולעת שני is "crimson wool," not "red wool": the *parah adumah* is red, the wool tied with it crimson.
- Trap: English "red" swallows *adom*, *shani*, *chamutz*, *chum*, and *sumak*.
  Zechariah tests this twice: at 1:8 *adumim serukim* in one phrase, and at
  6:2-3 *adumim* against *amutzim*. "Red horses ... chestnut horses" keeps
  Radak's distinction at 6:2-3; "red and pink horses" at 1:8 follows Malbim,
  where Radak would give only "colorful," so say which you are following.
- Yeshayahu 63:1-2 pairs *chamutz* and *adom* in adjacent phrases: render
  *chamutz* "stained red" and *adom* "red," so the second is not a repetition.
- חכלילי is never plain "red." "Bloodshot," or "dark from wine," keeps the idiom.
- In a Gemara, סומק is simply "red"; זהורית is "crimson," or transliterated.
