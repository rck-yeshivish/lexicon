# Divine judgment: din / rachamim (tekiah / teruah)

## Bottom line

*Din* is **judgment** and *rachamim* is **mercy**; as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
explains them, the difference between them is where the attention rests. Under *rachamim*
the focus is on the Giver, so the flow is constant and unearned. Under *din* the
focus is on the receiver, so the flow is measured out by what he deserves. *Din*
is not punishment: Hashem gives under *din* too. The Divine Name of *din* is *Elokim*; the
four-letter Name is the Name of *rachamim*. In the shofar, *tekiah* (one
unbroken sound) answers to *rachamim* and *teruah* (a broken, fragmented sound)
to *din*.

## Distinctions

### din (דין)
The Mishnah (Rosh Hashanah 1:2) teaches that on Rosh Hashanah all
the inhabitants of the world pass before Hashem like the animals of a corral and
He judges the whole of Creation, which is why the day is *Yom HaDin*; but Yom
Kippur is called the Day of Judgment as well, so what separates them? Ramban:
Rosh Hashanah is *din b'rachamim*, judgment inside mercy, and Yom Kippur is
*rachamim b'din*, mercy inside judgment. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein calls the
distinction cryptic and explains only the Rosh Hashanah half, leaving Yom Kippur
for another time. He unpacks *din* through
a mashal of two philanthropists. Both give large sums, but the second vets every
applicant, weighs the cause, and sets the amount by what the man before him
merits. That is *din*: the receiver is the object of focus, so the influx can be
curtailed, delayed, or portioned. In Tanach the plain word *elohim* can also
denote a human judge, and calling Hashem *Elokim* invokes exactly that role,
the Judge of all Creation. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) traces
*din* to the two-letter root ד-נ, judicial decision-making and its execution,
whence *dan* (to judge), *din*, *dayan* (a judge), *madon* (litigation), *adon*
(a master, one with jurisdiction to make decisions about another), *Adonai*
(the Name referring to Hashem as the Master of all creation), and *medinah* (a
province, the area under one common court system).

### rachamim (רחמים)
The first philanthropist in the mashal hands out money without asking who is
receiving it. The focus stays on the giver, so the giving is steady and does not
depend on desert. That is *rachamim*, and tradition associates the four-letter
Name with it. The Kabbalists sometimes express the same pair as *duchra* and
*nukva*, and as *mashpia* and *mekabel*, influencer and recipient. In Rabbi
Klein's imagery the sun, always the same disc and always emitting its own
light, is the giver and so *rachamim*; the moon, which has no light of its own
and waxes and wanes, is the receiver and so *din*. Rosh Hashanah, first day of
a solar year and first day of a lunar month at once, is where the two systems
meet.

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 13:2) defines the primary sense of the root ר-ח-ם as
motherly protection, which joins *rechem*, the place in her body where a mother
protects her unborn child, to *rachamim*, the dominant trait of a protective
mother.

### tekiah (תקיעה) and teruah (תרועה)
A *tekiah* is one straight, continuous sound; a *teruah* is made of several
short broken sounds (whether three longer, nine shorter, or three followed by
nine is a halachic uncertainty). Klein: *teruah* is akin to the Aramaic *rauah*,
"broken," as in the Gemara's *sulam rauah*, a ladder with broken rungs. Hence
the pairing in Tehillim 47:6, "*Elokim* has ascended with the *teruah*, Hashem
with the sound of the shofar": the Name of *din* stands with the fragmented
blast, the Name of *rachamim* with the whole one. Because every *teruah* is
sandwiched between two *tekios*, all that shows from outside is *rachamim*; this
is the Kabbalistic *mesikas hadin*, "sweetening the judgment," and the same
principle in dipping the apple in honey, honey being sweet and so itself
*rachamim*. Rabbi Klein reads *tiku bachodesh shofar bakeseh l'yom chageinu*
(Tehillim 81:4) along the same line: the Rosh Chodesh side of the day, the side
that carries the *din*, is the concealed one.

Rabbi Klein asks what such concealment can accomplish, since we can
hardly be fooling anyone by closing our eyes to the *din*. His answer is that
the concealment works by moving the object of focus. If we are judged, we are
the object of focus, and with our sins that is a poor place to stand; so on Rosh
Hashanah we do not speak of sins at all, but of Hashem's greatness and His
Kingship, and in doing so we shift the focus from the receiver to the Giver,
which is the *rachamim* paradigm, under which He gives without our deserving it.
That is how the day is *din* immersed in *rachamim* and sweetened on the
outside.

## Rendering guidance

- *Din* / *rachamim* as a pair: **judgment and mercy**. Where the sense is the
  mode of Divine conduct rather than a verdict, "the attribute of judgment" and
  "the attribute of mercy" are safer.
- Ramban's formulas are best left transliterated with a gloss: *din b'rachamim*
  (judgment within mercy), *rachamim b'din* (mercy within judgment). Do not
  invert them; the inner word is the operative one.
- *Tekiah* and *teruah* stay transliterated in any halachic or shofar context.
  Where the drashah turns on the sound, gloss once: *tekiah* (an unbroken
  blast), *teruah* (a broken one).
- Trap: English "mercy" will also absorb *chesed*, *chanun*, and *rachum*, and
  English "judgment" will absorb *mishpat* and *din* alike (see the *din /
  mishpat / chok / dat* entry). If a passage sets *din* against *mishpat*, no
  single English word may cover both.
- Trap: *rachamim* is not softness. When *din* and *rachamim* stand together in
  one phrase, the contrast to carry into English is focus on the receiver
  against focus on the Giver, not harshness against gentleness.
