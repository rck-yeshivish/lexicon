# Spring: Gichon / Shiloach / gachon

## Bottom line

*Gichon* and *Shiloach* are, for the rabbinic sages the column cites, two names
for one body of water, the spring by Yerushalayim: Rashi and Radak say so
outright, and Targum renders Gichon as *Shilucha*. Later linguists disagree:
for Wilhelm Gesenius the Gichon is the fountain and the Shiloach the aqueduct
that channels it, for Avraham Even-Shoshan the Shiloach is a channel or pool the
Gichon flows into, and for Professor Carol A. Dray the Gichon lay outside the
walls and the Shiloach within. Render both as proper names, **Gichon** and
**Shiloach**; "aqueduct," "channel" or "pool" for Shiloach carries the second
view. The other trap is the *other* Gichon: the river of Bereishis 2:13 that
circles Kush is, for Rashi and Rabbi Avraham Maimuni, a different river from the
Yerushalayim spring, while Rav Saadia Gaon, Ibn Ezra and the Ramah identify the
two; do not let one English "Gichon" decide that. *Gachon* (Bereishis 3:14) is
not a place at all but the serpent's **belly**, which Rav Hirsch and Rabbi
Pappenheim tie to the name Gichon.

## Distinctions

### Gichon (גיחון)
The Gichon of Eden (Bereishis 2:13) has been variously identified by scholars as
the Ganges, the Nile, the Amu Darya, or the Orontes. A Gichon at Yerushalayim
appears at Shlomo's coronation (Melachim I 1:33, 1:38, 1:45), in Chizkiyahu's
blocking and rediverting of its waters (Divrei HaYamim II 32:30), and in
Menashe's fortifying of the walls just beyond it (Divrei HaYamim II 33:14).

Root ג-ו-ח. Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer HaShorashim*) take the
core sense as "exiting": the same root yields the verb for a baby coming forth
from the womb (Iyov 38:8, Michah 4:10, Tehillim 22:10) and for a current leaving
its source. So Radak explains that the Gichon of Bereishis is named for going
forth from its fount and flowing around the whole land of Kush; in his
commentary (to Bereishis 2:13) he adds that it splits into many tributaries that
"exit" the parent river. Radak cites his father Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (1105 to 1170)
that the Gichon of Bereishis is the Nile, whose waters go out from the riverbank
to water the land; on that basis Radak notes that the Shiloach wellspring in
Yerushalayim, far smaller, is also called Gichon (Melachim I 1:33), because its
waters likewise go out into channels to irrigate the gardens around it. Targum
to Melachim I 1:33 renders Gichon as *Shilucha*, a variation of Shiloach,
possibly, the column suggests, to convey this very notion (and Targum to
Yeshayahu 8:6 renders *Mei Shiloach* as *Mei Shilucha*).

Two rivers or one: Rashi (to Kerisus 5b) says the Gichon of the coronation was a
small wellspring near Yerushalayim, not the great Gichon that flows from Eden;
on Chizkiyahu's stopping of the Gichon (to Berachos 10b) he stresses again that
it was near Yerushalayim and not the Gichon of Bereishis, adducing Targum's
*Shilucha* (to Melachim I 1:33) in support. Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 2:11), following
Rav Saadia Gaon (882 to 942), identifies the Gichon of Bereishis with the one
near Yerushalayim, and the Ramah (Rabbi Meir Abulafia, 1170 to 1244) is cited by
*HaKosev* (to *Ein Yaakov*, Horayos 12a) as saying the same. Rabbi Avraham
Maimuni disagrees with Ibn Ezra and sides with Rashi that they are two discrete
bodies of water: in some way we are no longer privy to, the spring outside
Yerushalayim resembled the great river, and so was given its name. He also
writes that in his time the spring was called *Brachah*; the column is unsure
whether he meant *Brachah*, "blessing," or *Breichah*, "pool" (perhaps what is
now called Chizkiyahu's Pool), and Rabbi Moshe Maimon of Jackson, NJ, in his
glosses to Rabbi Avraham Maimuni's commentary, presumes the former.

Chizkiyahu blocked the waters so that the Assyrian army besieging Yerushalayim
would not have easy access to water (Divrei HaYamim II 32:4). According to the
Mishnah (*Pesachim* 4:9) the sages of his time did not agree, presumably, the
column suggests, because it showed a lack of trust in Hashem, who had promised
through Yeshayahu to save Yerushalayim; an alternate tradition sees his act as
noble (Radak to Divrei HaYamim II 32:30; Radal to *Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer* 9:30).
The column closes with the story of Rabbi Chaim Vital, whom the Arizal faulted
in a dream for not reopening the waters of the Gichon, which would have
rectified Chizkiyahu's sin and brought the Final Redemption (in line with
Zechariah 14:8).

### Shiloach (שילוח)
When the Mishnah records Chizkiyahu's blocking of the Gichon it uses the name
Gichon, as Tanach does (*Pesachim* 4:9), but elsewhere it refers to the Shiloach
as a stream or spring near the Beis HaMikdash (*Sukkah* 4:10; see Rashi to
Sukkah 48a). In Tanach *Mei Shiloach* occurs once, Yeshayahu 8:6; a similar
name, the *Shelach* pool, appears at Nechemiah 3:15. Rashi (to Melachim I 1:33,
Yeshayahu 8:6) writes explicitly that the Gichon was also called Shiloach, and
so does Radak (to Melachim I 1:33).

The later linguists and philologists the column cites hold that the two names
do not refer to exactly the same place. Wilhelm Gesenius (1786 to 1842): the
Gichon is the fountain that supplies the water, while the Shiloach, explained as
a cognate of ש-ל-ח, "sending," is the aqueduct that channels it. Avraham
Even-Shoshan (1906 to 1984), in his concordance of Biblical Hebrew: the
Shiloach was a channel or pool into which the waters of the Gichon spring
flowed. Professor Carol A. Dray of the University of Cambridge: the Gichon was
outside the walls of Yerushalayim, the Shiloach within. (The column notes that
today the Gihon Company is Yerushalayim's public waterworks.)

The Septuagint renders Shiloach *Siloam*; the column suggests the final *m* is
possibly Semitic mimation. An inscription found at what is believed to be the
site of the Shiloach is known in English as the Siloam Inscription, and it
seems the Greek name was later borrowed into Arabic, the *m* becoming *n*, to
give *Silwan*, the name of the neighborhood just outside the Old City walls.

Halachically, a *get* must identify the city where it was written by naming the
nearest river, and the prevailing custom for *gittin* written in Yerushalayim is
to name the Shiloach. Rabbi Moshe Ibn Chaviv (1654 to 1696; *Get Pashut*, Even
HaEzer 128:37) explains that although Tanach more often calls it Gichon, we use
Shiloach so there is no confusion with the great Gichon of Eden (following Rashi
that they are two rivers). Rabbi Yoel Teitelbaum of Satmar (1887 to 1979;
*Divrei Yoel*, Even HaEzer 125) objects that this is not enough, since a river
with two names requires both in the *get* (see Rama, Even HaEzer 128:3), and
prefers to answer that since no one today calls it Gichon, that name has been
halachically uprooted and Shiloach is its only name.

### gachon (גחון)
The serpent's belly, in the curse "upon your belly shall you go" (Bereishis
3:14). Rav Hirsch (there) ties *gachon* to crawling, progress made not in a
straight line but by squirming along a twisted path, and suggests that perhaps
this notion of a twisted path is the basis of the name Gichon. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim offers a similar explanation, tying both *gachon* and *Gichon* to the
biliteral ג-ח. Both relate this to the birth sense of the root: squeezing
through a narrow opening takes bending and crawling, as a snake crawls.

### negichah (נגיחה)
Rabbi Pappenheim derives "goring" from the same biliteral ג-ח: a horned animal
cannot gore without bending its body and neck like a snake so the horns reach
the mark. Rashi (to Bereishis 2:13) had already linked the name Gichon to
*negichah*, as though the river's flow resembled the outward jab of a goring
ox.

## Rendering guidance

- Keep both names as proper names. "Aqueduct" (Gesenius) or "channel, pool"
  (Even-Shoshan) for Shiloach carries their view that it is a different site
  from the Gichon spring; use such a gloss only where that view is meant, and
  not where the text follows Rashi and Radak, for whom the names are one water.
- Where a text says Gichon and the context is Shlomo's coronation (Melachim I
  1:33, 1:38, 1:45), Chizkiyahu's diversion (Divrei HaYamim II 32:30), or
  Menashe's wall (Divrei HaYamim II 33:14), add "the Gichon spring by
  Yerushalayim" on first mention if the reader might otherwise hear Eden.
- Trap: English "Gihon" absorbs both rivers. If the passage turns on Rashi's
  distinction, or on Ibn Ezra's identification, name the two explicitly (the
  Gichon of Eden, the Gichon of Yerushalayim) rather than trusting context.
- גחון in Bereishis 3:14 is "belly," never "Gichon." Only where a source is
  building the wordplay (Rav Hirsch, Rabbi Pappenheim) should the English keep
  both in view, and then by a note, not by the rendering.
- Talmudic idiom: Kerisus 5b rules that kings must be crowned at a wellspring,
  symbolizing that their kingship should continue to flow like its current, and
  brings the pesukim of Shlomo's coronation at the Gichon. Render *maayan* there
  as "wellspring"; the point is the continuous current.
