# Anger: kaas / cheimah / charon / evrah / zaam / ketzef / rogez / af

## Bottom line

*Kaas* is the plain, generic word: **anger**. *Cheimah* is heated anger held in the
heart: **wrath**. *Charon* is anger past heat to burning: **burning anger**; the
idiom *charon af*, **fierce anger**. *Evrah* overflows its object onto others:
**fury**. *Zaam* is angry contempt that breaks into cursing: **indignation**.
*Ketzef* is anger at the boil, outward and visible: **rage**. *Rogez* is the body
shaken out of its calm: **agitation**. *Af* is anger on the face: **anger**. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim ranks them by intensity (kaas, zaam, cheimah, charon, charon af,
ketzef, evrah).

## Distinctions

The words are counted three ways: Rabbeinu Efrayim finds eight, Rabbi
Avraham Bedersi HaPenini ten, and Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino fifteen. Some of the
words mark different points on the spectrum from annoyed to incensed, others fix
on the object of the anger, others on its effects, and others on its cause.

### kaas (כעס)
Root כ-ע-ס. The commonest, most generic term (Shmuel I 1:6, Iyov 6:2). Rabbeinu
Efrayim: a metathesis of *eches*, poison in the heart. Malbim: anger pent up in the
heart and never brought out into action. Pappenheim: the simplest, lowest grade.
Rashi (to Shabbos 62b) runs the metathesis the other way, taking *eches* as a
metathesis of *kaas* because a snake emits its venom only when it becomes angered.
Either way, the link is already in the Gemara's exposition of the name of Kalev's
daughter Achsah (Yehoshua 15:16-17), "because anyone who sees her becomes angry
(*kaas*) at his wife," resting on the metathesis of ע-כ-ס, the supposed root of
Achsah and seemingly of *eches*, and כ-ע-ס.

### cheimah (חמה)
Yeshayahu 27:4, Esther 7:10.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: from *cham*, hot, since an angry man gets heated up. Bedersi
  supports this from the verb "burning" used with it (Esther 1:12), and notes
  that all the words for anger in Tanach apply to human anger alone except
  *cheimah*, which is used of an animal's anger too (Devarim 32:33, Tehillim
  140:4, 58:5).
- Malbim: anger held in the heart a long time without any act of rage.
- The Vilna Gaon, against *af*: *cheimah* is the developed stage where *af* is
  the beginning; anger in action where *af* is anger in thought; reserved for
  real enemies and those who would harm him, where *af* reaches even those who
  merely fail to follow one's wishes.

### charon (חרון) and charon af (חרון אף)
Yechezkel 7:12, Zecharyah 10:3.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: from *chor*, a hole, the heat piercing the body.
- Bedersi: greater than *cheimah*, for *cheimah* is "hot" while *charon* is
  "burnt" (Yechezkel 24:10).
- Pappenheim departs from that trend: from *nechirim*, nostrils, the angry man's
  heightened exhaling of hot air.
- Rashi (to Devarim 29:19, Shmuel II 22:9, Yeshayahu 48:9, Tehillim 18:9,
  Berachos 56b): *charon af* is anger because a person's anger can be discerned
  on his nose as it fumes in fury.
- Peirush HaRokeach: *charon* alone is "upset"; *charon af* is anger.
- Rambam (cited by his son Rabbi Avraham Maimuni): *charon* arouses the thirst
  for vengeance; *atzvus* does not.

### rogez (רוגז)
Iyov 3:17, Melachim II 19:27. Elsewhere "shaking" and "fear."
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: anger that arouses the whole body, as shaking and fear do.
- Bedersi: not always anger; sometimes the shaking is out of fear.
- Rokeach: the tranquil man who suddenly bursts into anger.
- Malbim: anger that harries a person and allows him no respite to live in
  tranquility.
- Shadal (to Bereishis 45:24, Shemos 15:14): primarily physical shaking or
  trembling, then applied metaphorically to any emotional agitation or
  disturbance, even if not from fear.
- Rashbam (to Bereishis 45:24): in Biblical Aramaic *rogez* denotes specifically
  anger or wrath. In rabbinic usage a *ragzan* is a person prone to anger
  (Shabbos 156a, Kiddushin 40b).

(The Yiddish *broiges*, of two people in a fight, comes from this word.)

### evrah (עברה)
Tehillim 78:49, Devarim 3:26.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: from *oveir*, to pass over or violate; the angry man
  violates his own and his Creator's expectations of him.
- Malbim: anger that passes beyond its bounds to fall on those who did not act
  against him.
- Peirush HaRokeach relates it to *me'uberes*, pregnant, though Rabbi Klein says
  the connection to anger remains unclear and offers two guesses of his own:
  perhaps the angry man's body swells like a pregnant woman's, or perhaps his
  wrath stays with him a long time as a pregnancy runs nine months.
- Pappenheim ranks it highest: the body puffed up and distended with fury.

### zaam (זעם)
Yirmiyahu 15:17, Zecharyah 1:12.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: from ז-ע, shaking and moving, since the very angry man
  trembles and so breaks out in sweat (*zei'ah*, from the same root).
- Ibn Janach and Bedersi: anger that stems from disgust or contempt for another.
- Malbim: the word does not primarily mean anger at all; its literal sense is
  "to curse", and in an anger context it names an effect of rage, the angry man
  cursing his object as long as the anger lasts.
- Vilna Gaon: suppressed anger, held back from showing outwardly.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 7:12) relates ז-ע-ם to ז-ה-ם, *zuhamah*, filth, by
  the interchange of hei and ayin: *zaam* is the rejection of another in the eyes
  of the beholder, as *zuhamah* too is something to be rejected.

### ketzef (קצף)
Bamidbar 17:11, Zecharyah 1:2; a very intense anger, the angry man having
reached a boiling point.
- Ibn Ezra (Esther 1:12) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim* s.v. קצף) note that the
  word also names the bubbles that rise from boiling water (Hoshea 10:7). The
  Aramaic *ritcha*, boiling, is also used in Rabbinic Hebrew as a synonym for
  anger (Berachos 7a, Rosh Hashanah 32b, Sanhedrin 48b).
- Rokeach (Shemos 16:20): the anger is visible in the sweat on the face, as
  bubbles in boiling water come up plainly to the surface.
- Eitz Yosef (Eichah Rabbah 2:3): the most extreme and dangerous form,
  associated with the archangel Gavriel, who represents Middas HaDin.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim seems to relate it to the scum or froth atop water, an
  allusion to the invariably unimportant thing that set the anger off.
- Malbim: outward anger coolly formulated by the intellect and by reflection,
  not a fit of emotional rage, and it can even answer something done against a
  third party rather than against oneself.
- Pappenheim: foam at the lips.

In Modern Hebrew *katzefet* is whipped cream.

### af (אף) and the lesser words
*Af* (Shemos 34:6, Devarim 6:15) is nose or face; Rabbeinu Efrayim: the angry
man's nose emits proverbial smoke. Malbim: anger physically visible in the face,
typically born of emotion not intellect, at times roused spontaneously.

*Anaf* (Devarim 1:37, Yeshayahu 12:1, Tehillim 2:12) is disputed against *af*:
Rabbeinu Efrayim holds it is the same word as *af* with an extra nun, while Radak
writes the exact opposite, that the true root of *af* is א-נ-ף and the nun
sometimes drops out. Rokeach: *anaf* is anger that spurs one to immediate revenge.
Pappenheim, as an aside, traces *anaf* instead to the biliteral נ-פ, movement, the
body twitching and convulsing out of sheer anger. The non-kosher *anafah* of
Vayikra 11:19 and Devarim 14:18 is explained by the Gemara (Chullin 63a) as a
*dayah ragzanit*, an angry bird.

*Ashan*, smoke (Shmuel II 22:9, Tehillim 18:9 and 80:5, Devarim 29:19): Radak
explains this as a borrowed term that came to mean anger because smoke is a
byproduct of a burning fire, and the words for anger are metaphorically tied to
boiling, heat and fire. *Panim*, face (Yirmiyahu 3:12, Bereishis 32:21), is
likewise borrowed, since a man's emotional state can often be readily seen on his face.

Pappenheim: *zaaf* (Michah 7:9, Divrei HaYamim II 26:19) joins ז-ע (shaking,
moving) and א-ף (face), a level of anger that brings on a furious facial twitch.
*Naatz* (Bamidbar 14:11, 14:23, 16:30) is a machlokes: Pappenheim traces it to
the one-letter root צ, "goes out", so that a man tolerates what he holds in
contempt only so long before his annoyance goes out into openly expressed anger,
while Malbim takes the opposite approach, that *naatz* is anger that leads one to
reject and push another away as though he were disgusting.

*Atzvus* (Melachim I 1:6, Yeshayahu 63:10, Tehillim 78:40) in many places means
"upset" or "disappointed," feelings that can lead to anger but are not anger; in
other places it came to mean sadness or melancholy, a slightly different concept.
*Raam* (Yechezkel 27:35, Shmuel I 1:6) also means noise or roar (Tehillim 77:19,
81:8, Iyov 26:14, 39:25), since the angrier a man is the likelier he is to lash
out by yelling; Rashi (to Shemos 15:24 and Yechezkel 27:35) connects the Mishnaic
*taromes* (Bava Metzia 4:6, 6:1), often mispronounced *taarumos*, meaning
complaint or grievance, to the same root, both being verbal expressions of
dissatisfaction.

*Shetzef* (Yeshayahu 54:8) appears only once in Tanach and its meaning is not
completely clear, which is why, Rabbi Klein says, it makes sense that it does
not reach Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino's list of fifteen. In its one occurrence it is
paired with *ketzef*.
- Menachem and Rabbeinu Tam: just as *ketzef* means anger, so does *shetzef*.
- Targum Yonatan there, Donash, Ibn Janach, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi, and Radak all
  argue it means "a little bit of"; Rashi to Yeshayahu 54:8 cites both
  possibilities.
- Donash also suggests it means the same as *rega*, a fleeting moment, and
  Peirush HaRokeach similarly reads it as an expression of speed, Rabbi Klein
  adding that this probably recalls how people act in haste when they blow their
  fuse.

## Rendering guidance

- Standalone כעס: "anger," the hypernym; do not dress it up. חרון אף: "burning
  anger" or "fierce anger," one phrase for the idiom. Bare חרון: "burning anger"
  (Bedersi: hotter than *cheimah*).
- אף וחמה together (Devarim 9:19): "anger and wrath," in that order; the Vilna Gaon
  reads *af* as the earlier stage, so the English pair must ascend.
- באף ובחמה ובקצף גדול (Devarim 29:27): "in anger, in wrath, and in great rage,"
  ascending. עברה וזעם (Tehillim 78:49): "fury and indignation."
- רוגז: check whether the pasuk means anger at all (Bedersi: sometimes fear or
  trembling); "agitation" is safe when the sense is uncertain.
- Trap: "wrath" swallows *cheimah*, *charon*, *evrah*, and *ketzef* alike, and
  "anger" swallows all sixteen; where the passage lists, pairs, or darshens the
  words, give each its own English in Pappenheim's order.
- Trap: *zaam* is not "wrath"; it carries contempt (Ibn Janach, Bedersi) and cursing
  (Malbim), so "indignation." *Atzvus* beside *charon* is "upset."
