# Sheep and goats: seh / tzon / eiz / tayish / gedi / seir / rachel / ayil / keves / kesev / tleh / atud

## Bottom line

The words sort by species. General (either species):
*seh* = **a lamb or kid**, one animal; *tzon* = **flock**. Goats: *eiz* =
**she-goat**, *tayish* = **he-goat**, *gedi* and *seir* = **kid**. Sheep:
*rachel* = **ewe**, *ayil* = **ram**, *keves* and *tleh* = **lamb**. *Kesev* is,
on one reading, an exact metathesis of *keves*, and on others a slightly older or more general sheep. *Atud* is the word whose meaning is
most obscured: it might be any goat, or an extra-large adult goat, or a fat young
sheep, or any adult male of either species.

## Distinctions

Tanach uses fifteen or so words for sheep (ovines) and goats
(caprines): some used only for sheep, some only for goats, and some for both
species indiscriminately or collectively, known as "ovacaprines." Some mark
a gender or an age, others are general.

### seh (שה)
Gender-neutral, a single young animal of either species. The Torah often pairs
*seh* with a more specific word that says which species is meant (Shemos 12:5;
Bamidbar 15:11; Devarim 14:4).

### tzon (צאן)
The collective, most commonly a flock or herd, sheep and goats together. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the tzadi element meaning "goes out," since these
lighter animals tend to go out even in winter, where the heavier cattle tend to
stay in.

### eiz (עז)
She-goat, possibly only an adult one, a nanny goat or she-goat in English,
plural *izzim*. Hebrew *eiz* is related to the Arabic word for goat, *enzu*, with the nun dropped in
Hebrew as often happens. Rabbi David Ibn Zimra (1479-1589): absent a stated age,
*eiz* covers any female goat from eight days old until two years. Pappenheim
traces it to the two-letter ayin-zayin element of what is stable and
unwavering, the same element behind *azus* (brazenness) and *oz* (strength),
because the goat's unbending legs give it footing to climb and leap.

### tayish (תיש)
Adult male goat, a billy or buck. Pappenheim traces the root to tav-shin,
"weakening" (*netishah*, abandonment, the weakening of a bond; *tash* and
*tashash*, energy failing; *yatush*, a fly, the weakest of all creatures), and
reads *tayish* as the polar opposite, an animal so strong it is the very
opposite of weakness.

### gedi (גדי) and seir (שעיר)
Both are young goats. *Gedi* / *gediyah* is a kid up to a year; *seir* /
*seirah* covers young goats at any age. Abarbanel (Vayikra 16:5) links *seir* to
*tzair*, young; Rav Hirsch (Devarim 32:2) links it to *se'ar*, hair, the kid
being hairy. Aramaic *tzfir* (Daniel 8:8, 8:21) is *seir*. The texts of the
Rambam (*Hilchos Maaseh HaKorbanos* 1:14) divide: one version holds that *seir*
specifically names a young goat in its second year (so Rashi to Menachos 91b) and
is the only term for such a beast, the other that the only term is *seir izzim*.
*Aruch HaShulchan HeAsid* discusses the point further.

### rachel (רחל)
Adult female sheep, a ewe. Hebrew *rachel* is related to
the Akkadian *lahru* by metathesis, the same consonants in reverse order. Rashi
(Menachos 107b): a *rachel* is in its second year of life or older.

### ayil (איל)
The ram, plural *eilim*, the *rachel*'s counterpart (Menachos 13:7); *Parah* 1:3
sets it at a male sheep more than a month into its second year. Pappenheim
traces it to the aleph-lamed element of power, the ram being the strongest of a
species that otherwise tends to be flimsy. Rabbinic Hebrew sometimes says *zachar shel rechalim*,
"a male of the ewes," instead (*Parah* 3:3; *Bechoros* 5:3; Bava Kama 50a;
Yevamos 121b); the difference between that term and *ayil*
is not readily apparent. Rabbi Yisrael David Miller of Grodno (1839-1913)
suggests Chazal use it where a reader might otherwise confuse *ayil* with *ayal*,
a deer or hart; others have suggested it distinguishes a castrated from an
uncastrated ram. The Gemara (Rosh Hashanah 26a) points out that *yovel* too means
ram, borrowed then for the ram's horn (Yehoshua 6:4-5) and then for the fiftieth
year when such a horn is blown; the English "jubilee" derives from *yovel*.

### keves (כבש) and kesev (כשב)
*Keves* / *kivsah* is a sheep within its first year (*Parah* 1:3). *Pesikta
Rabbasi* expounds *keves* as though from *kevisah*, laundering, the sacrificial
lamb washing away sin. In fourteen places the Torah writes *kesev* / *kisbah*
instead. Three readings:
- Ibn Parchon, Rabbi Yishaya of Trani, Radak, Ibn Ezra and other authorities cite
  the pair as a quintessential case of metathesis, the two words meaning exactly
  the same thing. (Even so, *Turei Zahav*, *Orach Chaim* §143:2, rules a sefer
  Torah unfit if one is written for the other.)
- Some Tosafists (*Panei'ach Raza* and *Baal HaTurim* to Vayikra 3:7, *Peirush
  HaRokeach* to Vayikra 4:32): *kesev* implies a bigger or older sheep than
  *keves*. Rabbi Naftali Tzvi Yehuda Berlin, the Netziv (1816-1893), in *HaEmek
  Davar* to Vayikra 1:10, also follows this approach.
- Rabbi Shmuel Strashun (1794-1872), the Malbim (1809-1879), and Rabbi Meir
  Simcha of Dvinsk (1843-1926) take a slightly different approach: *keves* always
  implies a young ovine, while *kesev* is a more general term that can cover
  sheep of all ages.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) takes *keves* from washing, as in the Midrash
above, and *kesev* from *kisah* / *mechuseh*, covered, sheep being covered in
wool.

### tleh (טלה)
Another young sheep. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 30:32): it can name a young human as
well, like English "kid" (see Rashi to Megillah 5b). Pappenheim traces it to the
tes-lamed element behind *tal*, dew: as vapor grows heavy enough to be drawn
down, so the newborn's own weight helps open the way at birth. Rabbi Meir Mazuz
derives from Shemos 23:19 and Shmuel I 7:9 that *tleh* and *gedi* name animals
still nursing.

### atud (עתוד)
The word whose meaning is most obscured. As some commentators
have it, *atud* denotes a he-goat larger and stronger than usual (see Ibn Ezra to
Bamidbar 15:25 and Yeshayahu 1:11), and it is used especially of the male goat
that leads the flock (Yirmiyahu 50:8). Targum Onkelos renders it *tayish*, adult
male goat, in one place (Bereishis 31:10) and *gedi*, young goat, in another
(Bamidbar 7:17). *Peirush HaTur HaAruch* (Bereishis 31:10) and Rabbi Yosef
Chiyyun (Tehillim 50:13): *atudim* are fattened *kevasim*, young sheep.
Alternatively Ramban (Bereishis 31:10) argues they are any adult male of either
species. So *atud* might be any goat,
or an extra-large adult goat, or a fat young sheep, or any male of either
species. Related fatling
words: *karim* (Devarim 32:14; Yechezkel 27:21; Yirmiyahu 51:40), perhaps male
sheep specifically, which Pappenheim traces to kaf-reish, digging, after the
ditches that bordered rich pasture; and *meri* / *meriim*, fattened sheep and
goats per Rashi to Yeshayahu 1:11 but fattened cattle per Ibn Ezra there, traced
by Pappenheim to reish-vav, quenching or satisfying.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "sheep" absorbs *seh*, *tzon*, *keves*, *kesev*, and *tleh*. Where a
  pasuk lists a species pair, keep the pair visible: שה כשבים ושה עזים (Shemos
  12:5) is "a lamb of the sheep or a kid of the goats," not "a sheep or a goat."
- *Seh* on its own in a korban context is "lamb" only where the context has
  already said sheep; otherwise "an animal from the flock" is safer than
  choosing a species the Hebrew left open.
- *Tzon* is a collective. "Sheep" for *tzon* loses the goats in it; "flock" keeps
  them.
- *Keves* and *kesev*: unless the passage turns on the difference, render both
  "lamb." If it does turn on it (a derashah on the fourteen *kesev* places, or a
  count of the two spellings), follow the majority of the later commentators and
  make *keves* the young one and *kesev* the general or older term, and say
  whose reading you are following.
- *Ayil* is "ram," never "deer"; *ayal* is the deer. In an unpointed text check
  the context before choosing.
- *Atud*: "he-goat" is the safe default. Where the pasuk is about leading the
  flock, "leader of the flock" carries the sense the commentators give it.
