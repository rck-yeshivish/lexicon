# Sheep and goats: seh / tzon / eiz / tayish / gedi / seir / rachel / ayil / keves / kesev / tleh / atud

## Bottom line

Sort by species before reaching for an English word. General (either species):
*seh* = **a lamb or kid**, one animal; *tzon* = **flock**. Goats: *eiz* =
**she-goat**, *tayish* = **he-goat**, *gedi* and *seir* = **kid**. Sheep:
*rachel* = **ewe**, *ayil* = **ram**, *keves* and *tleh* = **lamb**. *Kesev* is
either an exact metathesis of *keves* or a slightly older or more general sheep,
depending on whose reading you follow. The trap is English "sheep," which will
swallow *seh*, *tzon*, *keves*, *kesev*, and *tleh* together and lose the
species in the bargain.

## Distinctions

### seh (שה)
Gender-neutral, a single young animal of either species. The Torah regularly
pairs *seh* with a word that specifies which species is meant (Shemos 12:5;
Bamidbar 15:11; Devarim 14:4), which is itself the proof that *seh* alone does
not decide sheep against goat.

### tzon (צאן)
The collective: a flock or herd, sheep and goats together. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim traces it to the tzadi element meaning "goes out," since these
lighter animals go out to graze even in winter, where the heavier cattle stay
in.

### eiz (עז)
Adult she-goat, plural *izzim*. Rabbi David Ibn Zimra: absent a stated age,
*eiz* covers any female goat from eight days old until two years. Pappenheim
traces it to the two-letter ayin-zayin element of what is stable and
unwavering, the same element behind *azus* (brazenness) and *oz* (strength),
because the goat's unbending legs give it footing to climb and leap.

### tayish (תיש)
Adult male goat, a billy or buck. Pappenheim traces the root to tav-shin,
"weakening" (*netishah*, the loosening of a bond; *tash* and *tashash*, energy
failing; *yatush*, the frailest of creatures), and reads *tayish* as the pole
opposite, the animal of conspicuous strength.

### gedi (גדי) and seir (שעיר)
Both are young goats. *Gedi* / *gediyah* is a kid up to a year; *seir* /
*seirah* covers young goats at any age. Abarbanel (Vayikra 16:5) links *seir* to
*tzair*, young; Rav Hirsch (Devarim 32:2) links it to *se'ar*, hair, the kid
being hairy. Aramaic *tzfir* (Daniel 8:8, 8:21) is *seir*. The texts of the
Rambam (*Hilchos Maaseh HaKorbanos* 1:14) divide over whether *seir* alone names
a goat in its second year (so Rashi to Menachos 91b) or whether the term is only
*seir izzim*.

### rachel (רחל)
Adult female sheep, a ewe. Rashi (Menachos 107b): a *rachel* is in its second
year or older.

### ayil (איל)
The ram, plural *eilim*, the *rachel*'s counterpart (Menachos 13:7); *Parah* 1:3
sets it at a male sheep more than a month into its second year. Pappenheim
traces it to the aleph-lamed element of power, the ram being the strongest of an
otherwise flimsy species. Rabbinic Hebrew sometimes says *zachar shel rechalim*,
"a male of the ewes," instead (*Parah* 3:3; *Bechoros* 5:3; Bava Kama 50a;
Yevamos 121b); Rabbi Yisrael David Miller of Grodno suggests this avoids
confusion with *ayal*, a deer, and others distinguish a castrated from an
uncastrated ram. *Yovel* too means ram (Rosh Hashanah 26a), then the ram's horn
(Yehoshua 6:4-5), then the fiftieth year announced by that horn.

### keves (כבש) and kesev (כשב)
*Keves* / *kivsah* is a sheep within its first year (*Parah* 1:3). *Pesikta
Rabbasi* expounds *keves* as though from *kevisah*, laundering, the sacrificial
lamb washing away sin. In fourteen places the Torah writes *kesev* / *kisbah*
instead. Three readings:
- Ibn Parchon, Rabbi Yishaya of Trani, Radak, and Ibn Ezra: pure metathesis,
  identical in meaning. (Even so, *Turei Zahav*, *Orach Chaim* 143:2, rules a
  sefer Torah unfit if one is written for the other.)
- *Panei'ach Raza* and *Baal HaTurim* to Vayikra 3:7, *Peirush HaRokeach* to
  Vayikra 4:32, and the Netziv (*HaEmek Davar* to Vayikra 1:10): *kesev* is the
  bigger or older animal.
- Rabbi Shmuel Strashun, Malbim, and Rabbi Meir Simcha of Dvinsk: *keves* is
  always young, *kesev* is the general term at any age.
Rabbi Aharon Marcus takes *keves* from washing and *kesev* from *kisah* /
*mechuseh*, covered, sheep being covered in wool.

### tleh (טלה)
Another young sheep. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 30:32): it can name a young human as
well, like English "kid" (see Rashi to Megillah 5b). Pappenheim traces it to the
tes-lamed element behind *tal*, dew: as vapor grows heavy enough to be drawn
down, so the newborn's own weight helps open the way at birth. Rabbi Meir Mazuz
derives from Shemos 23:19 and Shmuel I 7:9 that *tleh* and *gedi* name animals
still nursing.

### atud (עתוד)
The least settled word here. Ibn Ezra (Bamidbar 15:25; Yeshayahu 1:11): a
he-goat larger and stronger than usual, especially the male that leads the flock
(Yirmiyahu 50:8). Targum Onkelos renders it *tayish* in one place (Bereishis
31:10) and *gedi* in another (Bamidbar 7:17). *Peirush HaTur HaAruch* (Bereishis
31:10) and Rabbi Yosef Chiyyun (Tehillim 50:13): *atudim* are fattened *kevasim*.
Ramban (Bereishis 31:10): any adult male of either species. Related fatling
words: *karim* (Devarim 32:14; Yechezkel 27:21; Yirmiyahu 51:40), perhaps male
sheep specifically, which Pappenheim traces to kaf-reish, digging, after the
ditches that bordered rich pasture; and *meri* / *meriim*, fattened sheep and
goats per Rashi to Yeshayahu 1:11 but fattened cattle per Ibn Ezra there, traced
by Pappenheim to reish-vav, quenching or satisfying.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "sheep" absorbs *seh*, *tzon*, *keves*, *kesev*, and *tleh*. Where a
  pasuk lists a species pair, keep the pair visible: שה כשבים ושה עזים (Shemos
  12:5) is "a lamb of the sheep or a kid of the goats," not "a sheep or a goat."
- *Seh* on its own in a korban context is "lamb" only where the context has
  already said sheep; otherwise "an animal from the flock" is safer than
  choosing a species the Hebrew left open.
- *Tzon* is a collective. "Sheep" for *tzon* loses the goats in it; "flock" keeps
  them.
- *Keves* and *kesev*: unless the passage turns on the difference, render both
  "lamb." If it does turn on it (a derashah on the fourteen *kesev* places, or a
  count of the two spellings), follow the majority of the later commentators and
  make *keves* the young one and *kesev* the general or older term, and say
  whose reading you are following.
- *Ayil* is "ram," never "deer"; *ayal* is the deer. In an unpointed text check
  the context before choosing.
- *Atud*: "he-goat" is the safe default. Where the pasuk is about leading the
  flock, "leader of the flock" carries the sense the commentators give it.
