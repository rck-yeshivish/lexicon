# Names of Shlomo HaMelech: Shlomo / Koheles / Agur / Lemuel (Yedidyah, Yakeh, Isiel)

## Bottom line

Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi counts seven names for Shlomo HaMelech: **Shlomo**,
**Yedidyah**, **Koheles**, and the four proper names in the last two chapters of
Mishlei, **Agur**, **Yakeh**, **Isiel** and **Lemuel**. Three of them turn on
completeness and peace (Shlomo, from *shalem* or *shalom*), three on gathering
and giving out wisdom (Koheles and Agur gather; Yakeh spits out), and Isiel and
Lemuel both record what he said to himself about the Torah's limits on a king.
The one thing not to get wrong: Agur, Yakeh, Isiel and Lemuel read on the surface
as ordinary proper names, and every English translation of Mishlei treats them
so; the Midrashic identification is a second layer, not a replacement.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What *Shlomo* means.** The Midrash reads it from *shalem*, complete, because he
oversaw the completion of the Beis HaMikdash. *Targum Sheini* reads it from
*shalom*, because peace and quiet were to prevail for Israel in his days, which
is how the prophecy given to David before the birth puts it (Divrei HaYamim I
22:9). Both stand; *Midrash Agur* (§4) leans on the second when it counts
Shlomo, Koheles and Agur his three most precious names, all three alluding to the
peace of his reign.

**Why *Koheles*.** The Midrash: because his teachings were delivered in
gatherings, from *hakhel*, *kahal*, *kehillah*, as it says "then Shlomo gathered"
(Melachim I 8:1). Rashi (to Koheles 1:1): because he gathered up many forms of
wisdom into himself. The second reading pairs *Koheles* with *Agur* as two words
for the same act, which is what *Midrash Agur* does.

**Whether *Yakeh* is a reproach or a compliment.** The Midrashim take it as a
reproach: he was filled with wisdom and later spat it out (*heikiah*) and forgot
it all when he began to stray, like a bowl that can be filled with water and
just as easily emptied. Ralbag (to Mishlei 30:1) takes it as praise: he spits
out, that is, rejects, the sayings that are untrue, and sets down in Mishlei only
what is left. A third view has him regurgitating to the public whatever wisdom he
had amassed. The Bottom line leaves all three in play, since they read the same
verb in opposite directions and the choice is a matter of derashah.

**What *Lemuel* means.** Four readings, and they split sharply.

1. **The Midrash**: *nam la'El*, he spoke to Hashem, saying he could take more
   horses, silver and wives than a king may (Devarim 17:16-17) and still not
   sin. The NUN of *nam* becomes a LAMED, the two being dentals along with
   DALET, TES and TAV.
2. **A second version of that Midrash**, sharper still: *lamah li El*, "what do
   I need Hashem for", a king throwing off the yoke of Heaven.
3. **Ibn Ezra** (to Mishlei 31:1): *lemo El*, "to them, El", because in his days
   Israel served Hashem alone and no other.
4. **Ralbag**: "for Him, El", his election as Hashem's chosen king. **Rashi** (to
   Sanhedrin 70b and Mishlei 31:1) is close: "for him, for Hashem", that his
   deeds and his wisdom should go into Hashem's service and nowhere else.

The Bottom line keeps the Midrashic reading first, since it is what puts the name
on the list of seven at all, and records the rest.

**Whether *Alukah* is an eighth name.** Rabbi Yishaya of Trani (the Rid) reads
*Alukah* at Mishlei 30:15, literally a leech, as another name for Shlomo, who
sucked up and drank in every form of wisdom. Tosafos (to Eruvin 19a) and Tosafos
and Tosafos Shantz (to Avodah Zarah 17a) grant that *Alukah* is somebody's name
but hold it cannot be Shlomo's, precisely because it is not one of the seven the
Midrash lists. The Bottom line follows Tosafos and keeps the count at seven.

## Distinctions

### Shlomo (שלמה) and Yedidyah (ידידיה)
The two names given him by people: Shlomo by his parents, David and Batsheva,
and Yedidyah by Nosson HaNavi (Shmuel II 12:24-25). Shlomo was already fixed by
prophecy before he was born (Divrei HaYamim I 22:9). *Yedidyah* means beloved of
Hashem, and he was. The Gemara (Menachos 53) reads a cryptic saying through it:
"let the beloved, son of the beloved, come and build the beloved for the beloved,
in the portion of the beloved, so that the beloved may be atoned." Six *yedidim*,
each from its own pasuk: Shlomo (Shmuel II 12:25), descendant of Avraham
(Yirmiyahu 11:15), building the Beis HaMikdash (Tehillim 84:2) for Hashem
(Yeshayahu 5:1), in the portion of Binyamin (Devarim 33:12), so that Israel
(Yirmiyahu 12:7) be atoned. Both names, read this way, point at the Mikdash.

### Koheles (קהלת) and Agur (אגור)
The two gathering names, one from ק-ה-ל and one from א-ג-ר. *Midrash Agur* (§4)
puts them beside Shlomo as his three most precious names, because gathering is
what his reign was: the whole nation gathered together and no strife among them.

### Yakeh (יקה), Isiel (איתיאל) and Lemuel (למואל)
The three names attached to the closing chapters of Mishlei, and the three that
carry the account of how Shlomo reasoned about a king's limits. *Isiel* is read
as a compound of *iti*, with me, and *el* in the sense of power: the Torah warns
a king against many horses, much silver and gold, and many wives (Devarim
17:16-17), and Shlomo held that with him was the strength not to be turned by
them. In the end he was not able to hold the excess and remain what was expected
of him. *Lemuel* records the same reasoning as speech addressed to Hashem, and
*Yakeh* records what it cost him.

## Rendering guidance

- In the body of Mishlei, translate the four as plain proper names: "the words of
  Agur son of Yakeh," "the words of Lemuel the king." Substituting "Shlomo" for
  them silently makes the Midrash into the text.
- Trap: English "gathered" will take *hakhel*, *kahal* and *agar* alike, which is
  fine when the point is that the names converge, and wrong when a derashah turns
  on which word a given name is built from. Where both names appear, keep
  *Koheles* to assembling people and *Agur* to amassing wisdom.
- *Yedidyah* is best left transliterated with a gloss at first mention, "beloved
  of Hashem"; the Anglicised "Jedidiah" reads foreign in a sefer.
- Where a passage plays *heikiah* against *Yakeh*, English needs the same verb
  twice: "spat out" for both, or the wordplay disappears.

  and `names-yosef-yehosef-avreich-tzafnas-paneach.md`.
