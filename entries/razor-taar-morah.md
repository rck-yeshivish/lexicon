# Razor: taar / morah

## Bottom line

*Taar* is the plain word for a **razor**; the Torah uses it for the *nazir*
(Bamidbar 6:5). *Morah* is the word the Neviim use in the same formula (Shoftim
13:5, 16:17; Shmuel I 1:11), and it is contested: Radak renders it **razor**
throughout, while Targum and Rashi render it **scissors** by Shimshon and **the
fear of men** by Shmuel. Rabbi Klein writes that he has found no source that
clearly explains the difference between the two words, and that he has been
unable to work out why the Torah says *taar* and the Neviim say *morah*.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| תער | taar | razor | the Torah's plain word; lays the skin bare (Rav Hirsch) |
| מורה | morah | razor, scissors | the Neviim's word; razor (Radak), or scissors, and by Shmuel fear (Rashi) |

## Distinctions

### taar (תער)
Bamidbar 6:5, "a *taar* shall not pass over his head." Rav Hirsch (to Bamidbar
6:5) relates it to ע-ר-ה, laying bare or exposing. That also explains *mitaar*,
a sword's scabbard: as the razor takes off the hair and lays the skin bare, so
the sword appears suddenly when it is drawn out.

### morah (מורה)
The word in the Neviim for the same prohibition. Linguists admit they do not
know its etymological source, though some suggest that its original root is also
ע-ר-ה, with the initial *ayin* dropped, which would put it with *taar* on Rav
Hirsch's reading. Two further etymologies are given.

- Rashi (to Shoftim 13:5) ties *morah* to י-ר-ה, "shoots" or "throws away,"
  because the razor throws the hair away, so to speak.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the root as מ-ר, transferring or switching, the
  root of [*temurah*](exchange-temurah-chalifah.html#%D7%AA%D7%9E%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%94) (which attempts to transfer *kedushah* from one animal to
  another) and [*mumar*](heretic-min-apikores-kofer-meshumad.html#%D7%9E%D7%95%D7%9E%D7%A8) (one who rejects Judaism and switches to another
  religion): shaving away hair paves the way for a new batch to replace what was
  cut.

*Bamidbar Rabbah* 10:5 hears "fear" in the word: why is the *taar* called a
*morah*? Because hair is scared (*morah*) only of the razor, which shaves it with
a shaving of destruction, as it says "do not destroy the corner of your beard"
(Vayikra 19:27). Rabbi Klein says the deeper meaning of this Midrash seems
obscured, and treats it as an opening for the discussion.

### The dispute at Shmuel I 1:11

- Targum (to Shoftim 13:5 and 16:17) renders *morah* as scissors by Shimshon,
  and Targum (to Shmuel I 1:11) renders it "fear of men" by Shmuel; Rashi
  follows the same split in each place.
- Radak renders *morah* "razor" everywhere, and explains that Targum and Rashi
  are following Rabbi Yossi, who holds (*Nazir* 66a) that Shimshon was a *nazir*
  but Shmuel was not; on that view Chana's vow cannot be about a razor, so
  *morah* there must mean the fear of other men. Radak himself holds like Rabbi
  Nehorai, that Shmuel was a *nazir*, and so keeps "razor."

## Rendering guidance

- תער: "razor," always.
- מורה: "razor" by default, following Radak.
- Trap: at Shmuel I 1:11 the choice of English decides the *machlokes*. "No
  razor shall come upon his head" commits to Rabbi Nehorai and Radak; "no fear
  of man shall come upon him" commits to Rabbi Yossi, Targum and Rashi. Do not
  choose silently; where the passage bears on whether Shmuel was a *nazir*,
  give one reading in the body and the other in a note.
- Trap: English "razor" absorbs *taar* and *morah* both, which is usually
  harmless, but not where *Bamidbar Rabbah* 10:5 is being darshened. There the
  derashah runs on the sound of *morah* as fear, and the English must hold both:
  "the razor, the *morah*, of which the hair alone is afraid."
- Do not manufacture a distinction between the two words in a footnote. Rabbi
  Klein looked and found none, and could not account for why the Torah says
  *taar* and the Neviim say *morah*.
