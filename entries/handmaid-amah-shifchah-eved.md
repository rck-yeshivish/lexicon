# Handmaid: amah / shifchah (and eved)

## Bottom line

*Shifchah* is a **bondwoman**, the female counterpart of the *eved*, set to
whatever work the household needs. *Amah* is a **maidservant** or **handmaid** in
the higher sense: a nursemaid to her master's children (Bedersi) or a housekeeper
over the domestic work rather than under it (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim). *Eved* is
the one word for both the Jewish and the non-Jewish male **slave**. The shorthand
that *shifchah* means a non-Jewish woman and *amah* a Jewish one might hold
in Rabbinic usage, but it gives only a partial picture of Tanach, where both
words cross the line.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| אמה | amah | maidservant, handmaid | the dignified role: nursemaid (Bedersi) or housekeeper (Pappenheim) |
| שפחה | shifchah | bondwoman | full servitude, the female *eved* (Wertheimer) |
| עבד | eved | slave, bondsman | one word for Jewish and non-Jewish alike (Moshav Zekeinim; Rabbi Moshe Sherrow) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What amah adds.** Three readings, two of them from the same word.

1. **Nursemaid.** Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini: *amah* is from *eim*,
   "mother," a maid who takes on motherly duties and nurses her master's
   children. He adduces it from Hagar, who is called *shifchah* throughout
   Bereishis 16, and *amah* from the birth of Yishmael onward (Bereishis 21, but
   see 25:12). This understanding of *amah* is comparable to the English "nanny,"
   engaged to help raise her employer's children as though she were their mother.
2. **Housekeeper.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*), also from *eim*:
   a *shifchah* is put to menial work, an *amah* is more dignified and sees only
   to the ordinary running of the house. He derives the other senses from this
   one: the hand, which serves a man as the *amah* serves the household, and
   from the hand the cubit.
3. **From aimah, fear.** Rabbi Eliezer Reines (*Maftechos HaDamesek*): the
   Jewish handmaid is called *amah* because she is not yet mistress of the
   house, and the fear (*aimah*) of the real mistress is upon her.

**The root of shifchah.** Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: the root, which he
writes with a shin, sin or samech before peh and ches, denotes connection or
addition, so a *shifchah* is joined to the mistress of the house, both expected
to perform services for the master (compare Kesuvos 59b, that a woman who brings
a hundred *shefachos* into her marriage still owes certain chores herself);
*mishpachah*, a family, is otherwise separate individuals connected by familial
relations. Others, including Rabbi Aharon Marcus and Rabbi Zev Hoberman: the
root refers specifically to an extra appendage attached to something else,
whence *sapachas*, a type of tzaraas (Vayikra 13:2), and *sapach*, an appendix;
a *mishpachah* is the patriarch's children as secondary attachments ascribed to
him, and a *shifchah* an adjunct attached to the otherwise complete faculty of a
household.

**Whether the Jewish/non-Jewish split is in the words themselves.** Some argue
*shifchah* inherently means a non-Jewish slave woman and *amah* a Jewish one.
That may be true of rabbinic usage, but it is only a partial picture of Tanach:
Vayikra 25:44 uses *amah* of female slaves taken from the non-Jewish population
of the Holy Land, Avraham davened for Avimelech's wife and his *amahos*, whose
wombs had been closed as a punishment (Bereishis 20:17), also non-Jewish, and
Avigail calls herself Dovid's *amah* six times and his *shifchah* twice (Shmuel
I 25), which suggests *shifchah* applies to a Jewish handmaid as *amah* does.

## Distinctions

### amah (אמה)
See the three derivations above. Rabbi Moshe Sherrow adds the halachic reason
the Torah needs a separate word: a Jewish woman sold as an *amah* loses nothing
of her status, may certainly not marry a non-Jewish *eved*, and is expected to
marry her master or his son (Shemos 21:8-9), so she remains an ordinary Jewish
woman. Hence *amah* against *shifchah*, where the male has only *eved*.

### shifchah (שפחה)
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: a *shifchah* and an *eved* are both genuinely
slaves, put to a range of tasks, while an *amah* is closer to a housekeeper,
whose work is domestic, a different institution altogether. That is why the
female has two words and the male one. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg notes that
Bilhah and Zilpah are still called *shifchah* after Yaakov married them
(Bereishis 32:23, 33:2, 33:6), out of humility: raised to be full wives, they
went on as before and did not assert their new standing.

### eved (עבד)
One word for the Jewish bondsman and the non-Jewish slave alike. The Tosafists
(*Moshav Zekeinim* to Shemos 21:4): a Jewish man sold as an *eved* loses
something of his standing as a Jew, which is why his master may require him to
cohabit with a non-Jewish bondwoman, ordinarily forbidden to him. Rabbi Moshe
Sherrow draws the conclusion: since neither the Jewish nor the non-Jewish male
slave is in the ordinary position of a Jew, one word covers both.

## Rendering guidance

- אמה: "maidservant" or "handmaid." Where the passage is about nursing or
  raising the master's children, "nursemaid" carries Bedersi's reading.
- שפחה: "bondwoman," or "maidservant" only where nothing turns on the contrast.
- עבד: "slave" of a non-Jew, "bondsman" of an *eved Ivri*; "servant" where the
  usage is honorific (a servant of Hashem, a king's servant).
- Trap: English "maidservant" absorbs *amah* and *shifchah* without a trace, and
  most translations let it. Shmuel I 25 is where it must not: Avigail moves
  between the two words about herself, and one English word hides the shift. So
  too Hagar, *shifchah* before Yishmael and *amah* after, Bedersi's whole proof.
- Do not write "Jewish maidservant" for *amah* and "gentile slavewoman" for
  *shifchah* in a Chumash translation; that is the Rabbinic convention read back
  into pesukim that do not observe it.
