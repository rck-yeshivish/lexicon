# Sword and knife: cherev / shelach / sayif / sakin / maacheles / chalaf

## Bottom line

*Cherev* and *shelach* are both **swords**; Malbim separates them by use, a
*shelach* for stabbing and a *cherev*, he implies, for slicing. *Sayif* is the
common Aramaic word for a sword in the Talmud. Tanach's two words for a **knife**
are *sakin* and *maacheles*; *chalaf*, the common word for a *shochet*'s knife, is
Arabic (Rashi, Rabbeinu Shimshon of Shantz) or Greek (Bartenura).

## Where the authorities disagree

**The origin of *chalaf*.**
- Rashi (to Yoma 36a and Zevachim 20a) and Rabbeinu Shimshon of Shantz (to Middos
  4:7): "knife" in Arabic. Arabic *chalaf* does mean something sharp, likely, Rabbi
  Klein suggests, from Hebrew *charif*, the r switched for an l, as is common.
- Bartenura: it comes from Greek. Rabbi Yisrael Lipschitz (1782 to 1860) notes,
  correctly in Rabbi Klein's view, that there is no such Greek usage.
- Rabbi Reuven Margolios (1889 to 1971) and Rabbi David Kamin (d. 1922) ask why
  Rashi resorts to Arabic rather than cite *machlifim* (Ezra 1:9), which he himself
  explains as knives. Ritva (to Yoma 36a) asks the same, adding *kalil yachlof*
  (Yeshayahu 2:18), the future cutting down of idols.

**The origin of *sakin*.**
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: related to *sikim*, a kind of thorn (Bamidbar 33:55),
  for the likeness of knives to thorns.
- Rabbi Klein suggests the root ס-ך, to spread or smear, as a table knife spreads
  butter; or perhaps *sakanah*, danger, the risk of a knife fight or other misuse.

## Distinctions

### cherev (חרב)
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814) relates *cherev* to
  *churban*, the destruction of something not itself damaged but cut off from an
  outside element it needs to flourish: an intact but abandoned town, or
  *charbah*, a dry land infertile not through its own fault but for want of
  water from outside. The sword, an ancient tool for reducing a population,
  brings that destruction.
- Linguists note that an Akkadian word like *cherev* means a kind of plow.

### shelach (שלח)
Malbim: a *shelach* is for stabbing, as against a *cherev*, which he implies is
for slicing; perhaps, Rabbi Klein suggests, a *shelach* was something like a
dagger. As a noun meaning "sword" it appears seven times in Tanach (Divrei HaYamim
II 23:10, 32:5; Yoel 2:8; Nechemiah 4:11, 4:17; Iyov 33:18, 36:12), and nine more
times as the name of Shem's grandson, the father of Ever (Bereishis 10 to 11;
Divrei HaYamim I 1). Radak: a verb cognate meaning "cut" appears in Bereishis 37:32.

Moshe Oussi reads *vayeshalchu es kesones hapasim* (Bereishis 37:32) as cutting
the garment with a sword, from *shelach*.

The name Metushelach is *mes*, "he died," and *shelach*. According to the Midrash
(cited by *Peirush HaRokeach*, *Sifsei Kohen* and others), his sword, engraved with
the Ineffable Name, with which he killed as many as 98,000 demons in one moment to
protect mankind, was buried with him, so that when he died his sword also "died"
(though *Imrei Noam* and *Zera Beirech* say it later came to Avraham).

### sayif (סייף)
The common Aramaic word for a sword in the Talmud. The Targumim render *cherev*
generally as *charba*, sometimes as *sayif* (Targum Yonasan to Shemos 20:22, Shoftim
20:2). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549): singular *cherev* becomes *charba*,
plural *sayif*; why that should matter is a mystery to Rabbi Klein. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim and Rabbi Avraham Vilner (1765 to 1808), a son of the Vilna Gaon, derive
*sayif* from *sof*, end, as a sword can end a life (forms of *sof* mean death in
Bereishis 18:23 and Yeshayahu 7:20). The Arabic name Saif also means "sword."

The Tashbetz (*Magen Avos*, Jerusalem 2007, p. 57): *sayif* and *cherev* mean
exactly the same thing.

### sakin (סכין)
Spelled with a sin in its one appearance in Tanach (Mishlei 23:2), with a samech
in later rabbinic writings.

### maacheles (מאכלת)
Tanach's other word for a knife, found four times (Bereishis 22:6, 22:10; Shoftim
19:29; Mishlei 30:14). Rashi (to Bereishis 22:6) relates it to א-כ-ל, eating: it
"eats" the slaughtered animal's flesh by cutting it, or lets others eat what it cut.

### chalaf (חלף)
The common word for the knife a *shochet* uses.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "sword" absorbs *cherev*, *shelach* and *sayif*. Beside *cherev*, a word for
  a stabbing weapon keeps Malbim's distinction; "dagger" is Rabbi Klein's guess.
- Trap: "knife" absorbs *sakin*, *maacheles* and *chalaf*. Rashi's explanation of
  *maacheles* at the Akeidah depends on its link to eating, which "knife" hides.
