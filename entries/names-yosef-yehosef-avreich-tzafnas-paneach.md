# Names of Yosef: Yosef / Yehosef / Tzafnas Paneach / Avreich

## Bottom line

*Yosef* is a name; Rachel gave it from *yosef*, "He
shall add" (Bereishis 30:24). *Yehosef* is the same person with a hei of
Hashem's Name added (Tehillim 81:6). *Tzafnas Paneach* is, by Rashi's reading,
**revealer of mysteries**. *Avreich* is
read as **father to the king**, **a father in wisdom though young
in years**, or **one before whom all kneel**. *Rach* may, on some
readings, mean "royal."

| יהוסף | Yehosef | Yehosef | the extra hei of the Divine Name, earned in Potiphar's house (Sotah 10b, 36b) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What avreich means** (Bereishis 41:43). Three readings, and Rashi carries all
three.

1. **Father to the king.** Targum Onkelos does not leave the word untranslated
   at all: *dein abba l'malka*, "this is the father to the king." Rashi's first
   explanation follows, parsing *avreich* as *av* plus *reich*, king. Our texts
   of Rashi say *reich* is Aramaic for king, but no such Aramaic word exists;
   it is generally agreed that a mistake crept into our versions and that Rashi
   should be read as saying *reich* means king in *Latin*. The Latin word does
   appear in the Gemara: opposing Herod's plan to refurbish the Beis HaMikdash,
   the emperor warns that on account of his questionable lineage he is "neither a
   *reicha* nor the son of a *reicha*" (Bava Basra 4a). Latin *reggio*/*rex*
   means king, from the Proto-Indo-European root *reg-*,
   making it a cognate of German *reich*, kingdom or empire, and of a whole bevy
   of English words, among them *raj*, *royal*, *regal*, *regius*, *reign*,
   *regent*, *rector*, *director* and *prerogative*; it also underlies the names
   Regina, Reginald, Reinhold, Ronald, Rhona, Richard, Heinrich, Hendrik,
   Aldrich, Rodriguez, Patrick, Derek, Fredrich, Imre and Emmerich, the last of
   which has as its Italian equivalent the name of Amerigo Vespucci, for whom
   the Americas are named.
2. **A father in wisdom, young in years.** *Bereishis Rabbah* 90:3 and *Sifrei
   Devarim* 1 split it as *av* plus *rach* (soft, young), setting Yosef's
   surpassing wisdom against his youth. This is the source of the modern use of
   *avreich* for a young man in kollel.
3. **One before whom all kneel.** Others in *Sifrei Devarim* 1 take it from
   ב-ר-כ, *berech*, a knee. The Taz in *Divrei Dovid* notes why: these
   authorities rejected splitting the name into two words at all, so they needed
   a single root. *Targum Yonasan* synthesizes the first and third readings.

**Whether Tzafnas Paneach reveals or conceals.** *Bereishis Rabbah* 90:4 gives
four readings; three take *tzafnas* from צ-פ-נ, hidden. On *paneach* there is
less agreement.
- In the Midrash, some combine פ-ע (appearing) with נ-ח (easing or placating):
  Yosef unravels hidden matters easily, or he settles people's questions by
  disclosing what was hidden.
- Rashi (to Bereishis 41:45) renders "revealer of mysteries."
- The Chasam Sofer reverses it: "concealer of mysteries," for Yosef's
  undertaking to keep secret that Pharaoh could not speak Lashon HaKodesh (Sotah
  36b).
- Later Hebrew, in *Nishmas* and in HaKallir's piyyutim, uses *paneach* as a
  verb for deciphering, which may simply follow the Midrash.

**Whether Yosef and Yehosef are one name or two.**

- Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky is reported to have first held them distinct, so that a
  father could name one son Yosef and another Yehosef; he later retracted and
  wrote that for naming siblings and for shidduchim one should be stringent and
  treat them as one name.
- Rabbi Mordechai Gross of Bnei Brak leaves it in doubt.

The two spellings also stand side by side in the Mikdash: Rambam (*Hilchos Klei
HaMikdash* 9:9) has *Yehosef* on the shoham stones of the Kohen Gadol's
shoulders while the choshen reads *Yosef* (see *Minchas Chinuch* 99).

**When Yosef became Yehosef.**

- Sotah 10b and 36b: the hei was added when the angel Gavriel came to teach him
  the seventy languages, not at the moment of the test.
- Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev infers from the piyyut *Even Choger*
  (Shacharis, first day of Rosh HaShanah) that he was Yehosef already in the
  womb. He reconciles them with Niddah 30b: the unborn child is taught the whole
  Torah by an angel, and all seventy languages are contained in Torah, so the
  name fit him then; birth made him forget, and Gavriel restored him to Yehosef.

**Whether rach can mean "king."** *Rach* means soft, tender, or young, but
*reich* in the sense of "king" may appear in several other places as well:
- *Sefer Russiana* and, independently, the Pittsburgher Rebbe (*Emunas Avraham*)
  on Leah's eyes as *rakos* (Bereishis 29:17), against the many who read them
  soft or weak from weeping (see Rashi there);
- *Targum Yonasan* on David's "I am today *rach* and anointed king" (Shmuel II
  3:39), which it renders "I am today like a commoner who will grow up to be
  king," and from which Radak *implies* that Targum took *rach* for king;
- Rabbi Chaim Vital (*Pri Eitz Chaim*, *Shaar HaYichudim* ch. 19) on *harakah*,
  the pampered woman of Devarim 28:56;
- and Rashi (to Shabbos 53a) explaining the amora Shmuel's nickname Aryoch as
  royal, the *rach* of it being the Latin for king, since he was expert in civil
  law and in that role functioned in a way resembling a king, though Rashi
  elsewhere (Menachos 38b) derives Aryoch from *aryeh*, the lion to which
  Yehudah is compared (Bereishis 49:9). The name Aryoch is borne in Tanach by
  the king of Elasar, one of the four kings Avraham fought (Bereishis 14:1,
  14:9), and by a minister of Nevuchadnetzar (Daniel 2:14, 2:15, 2:24-25).

## Distinctions

### Yosef (יוסף)
- Root י-ס-פ for early lexicographers such as Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, and
  Radak: to add. Rachel exclaims *asaf* (Hashem has gathered up my disgrace) and
  names him *Yosef* (may Hashem add me another son), Bereishis 30:23-24.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim treats the yud as extraneous and takes the core as
  ס-פ, "end," which yields *sof* (an end), *sayif* (a sword, which ends a life),
  *safah* (a lip, edge, or riverbank), *saf* (a threshold, a domain's edge), and
  *asifah* (gathering, which redraws a set's boundaries). Adding, on this
  reading, is extending an end to where it had not reached.

The name *Yosef* appears more than 200 times in Tanach of him and of his
descendants, and three others there bear it: the father of Yigal the spy from
Yissachar (Bamidbar 13:7), a singer of the family of Assaf in David's day
(Divrei HaYamim I 25:9), and one of the returnees in the days of Ezra and
Nechemiah (Ezra 10:42, Nechemiah 12:14).

### Tzafnas Paneach (צפנת פענח)
*Paneach* looks quadriliteral, פ-ע-נ-ח, and is otherwise unattested in Biblical
Hebrew. Grammarians such as Ibn Ezra and Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur hold as a rule that words from quadriliteral
roots are not native to Hebrew but come in as loanwords, which suggests turning
to Egyptian. Rabbi Klein did so in his *Lashon HaKodesh: History, Holiness,
& Hebrew*, offering a collection of possible translations on the assumption of
an Egyptian origin: "lord of life," "Neth speaks life," "the
god speaks and [this man] lives," "redemption of the age," "savior of his time,"
"he who is called Ip-Ankh," and "the man who knows things." He presents
these as possibilities, not as a settled reading.

## Rendering guidance

- Names stay names. Write Yosef, Yehosef, Tzafnas Paneach, and do not translate
  them in running text; gloss once in a footnote if the derashah needs it.
- Trap: "Joseph" absorbs both Yosef and Yehosef and destroys Tehillim 81:6,
  where the whole point is the extra letter. Transliterate both, distinctly.
- *Avreich* is the trap in the other direction. In a modern sentence it means a
  kollel man; in Bereishis 41:43 it does not, and importing the modern sense is
  an anachronism. Where the pasuk is quoted, leave *avreich* and gloss it by the
  reading the passage assumes.
- Where *rach* appears with a person (Leah's eyes, David after Avner's death,
  the pampered woman of Devarim 28:56), "soft," "tender," or "delicate" is the
  safe rendering; reserve "royal" for a passage explicitly citing that reading.
