# Synonyms: the method (davar, vayomer / vayidaber, melucha / moshel, ba / atah)

## Bottom line

Rabbi Klein's founding premise: in Hebrew a word expresses the essence of the thing, which is why *davar* means both **word** and
**thing**; every element of creation is a Divine word crystallized into
material existence. That raises a problem: why would Hebrew need several words
for one concept? The answers usually follow a
few templates: the words only seem alike, or they focus on different aspects,
or (in verbs) differ in degree or intent, or stand as general term and species,
or one is borrowed from another language to make a point, in which case it may
mean just what the Hebrew word means. For Malbim *vayomer* is **he said**
(brief) and *vayidaber* **he spoke** (extended); for Ibn Ezra *melucha* is
**kingship** accepted by the people and *moshel* a **ruler** whether accepted or
not; for the *meforshim*, *ba* and *atah* both mean **came**, the second being
Aramaic and chosen for that reason.

## Distinctions

### the five resolutions
Where two Hebrew words look identical in meaning, the answer usually takes one of
these forms:
1. They only seem alike, and a slight, barely visible difference separates them.
2. They name the same thing but focus on different properties of it.
3. In verbs, the same act done to a different degree or with a different intent.
4. Taxonomy: one is the general term (hypernym), the other a species that falls
   inside it (hyponym).
5. The Torah has borrowed a word from another language to make a point, and the
   loanword may bear the same meaning as a Hebrew word.

*Afarkesta DeAnya* (vol. 3, *Yoreh De'ah* 197): the holy tongue is not like
other languages, whose many names for one thing differ hardly at all and were
coined only to enrich the vocabulary; in Hebrew every name and every word
carries a concept of its own, which one who knows the language fully would never
exchange for another.

*Sifrei* (Naso 23) says the Torah speaks in two expressions for one thing, and
gives *yayin* and *shechar*, *os* and *mofes*, *zevichah* and *shechitah*,
*kemitzah* and *haramah*, *amukah* and *shefelah*.

### vayomer (ויאמר) and vayidaber (וידבר)
The two seem to mean more or less the same. Malbim: *vayomer* is a brief,
short verbal expression; *vayidaber* is a lengthy, drawn-out monologue.

### melucha (מלוכה) and moshel (מושל)
Tehillim 22:29, quoted several times in the daily tefillos: "for to Hashem is
*melucha*, and He is *moshel* over the nations." Ibn Ezra: both words refer to
sovereignty, but *melucha* denotes a popular sovereign whose dominion his
constituents willingly accepted, while a *moshel* is a dictator who goes on
ruling whether or not his people object to him. See
[king](king-melech-moshel.html), where the Vilna Gaon works the same pair out at
length.

### ba (בא) and atah (אתה)
Devarim 33:2: "Hashem *ba* from Sinai... and He *atah* from the holy
multitudes."

- The *meforshim*: the two words mean the same, but the first is Hebrew and the
  second Aramaic, and the Torah uses the Aramaic word here to allude to Hashem's
  having first offered the Torah to the other nations before giving it to Klal
  Yisrael.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, whose explanation Malbim cites in *Yair Ohr*, does
  distinguish them: *biah* can mean both reaching one's destination and
  travelling toward it, while *atah*, which also focuses on the destination, is
  a coming with more finality, referring only to arriving and never to the way
  there.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg reads *bo el Paroh* (Shemos 10:1) as "bring
  [this message] to Pharaoh," since *l'havi*, to bring, is itself a conjugation
  of *bo*: to bring a thing from one place to another is to cause it to go. In
  the same vein Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives *navi*, a prophet, from the
  two-letter root of *bo*, because a *navi* brings Hashem's words to those who
  hear him.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and Rabbi Aharon Marcus trace *bo* and *biah* to the
  one-letter root ב, inside, which also fits the work of the *beis* as a prefix:
  one who comes to a place goes inside it and stops coming, having arrived,
  though *bo* may still be used of him while he is on the way. Rabbi Klein takes
  this as accounting for the assumption of the Tosafists and the *Zohar* that
  *bo* implies going inside.

### davar (דבר)
The premise itself. Other languages' words are a consensus among speakers that
they should carry their meanings; the meaning of Hebrew words is Divinely
inherent. One word carries "word" and "thing" because all elements of creation
are Divine words crystallized into material existence. So close study of words and their true meanings is fully warranted.

## Rendering guidance

- Fix a stable English word to each Hebrew word across a passage and hold it
  there. *Vayomer* is "said," *vayidaber* is "spoke"; if the English needs
  variety, get it from the sentence, not by swapping the pair.
- Where two synonyms sit in one pasuk, assume the second is doing work. Devarim
  33:2 and Tehillim 22:29 both look like poetic repetition and are not.
- Trap: English "speak" and "say" are interchangeable in ordinary prose, so a
  translator will pick by rhythm. That destroys Malbim's distinction wherever a
  derashah turns on which verb the Torah used.
- Trap: an Aramaic word inside a Hebrew pasuk is a signal, not a stylistic
  accident. Do not smooth *atah* into the same English as *ba* without a note.
- Where a word is the general term and its partner a species of it (resolution
  4), the English must keep the hierarchy: a general word for the hypernym, a
  narrow one for the hyponym, never two words of equal breadth.
