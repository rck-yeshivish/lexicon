# Pulling and drawing: moshe / moshech / shoev / shoef

## Bottom line

*Moshech* is the general verb: **pull, draw**, taking something from its place
toward oneself. *Moshe* is narrow and almost always in water: **draw out of the
water** (Rashbam). *Shoev* is **draw water**, in almost all biblical instances
from a well; *shoef* is its air counterpart, **inhale**, and in *sheifah*,
**yearning**. Wertheimer is the one who holds the water/air line in practice.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| משה | moshe | draw out of water | specifically from within water (Rashbam, Rabbi Chelouche) |
| משך | moshech | pull, draw | the general term; includes moshe (Rabbi Chelouche) |
| שאב | shoev | draw water | almost always from a well; water and not air (Wertheimer) |
| שאף | shoef | inhale, yearn | air and not water (Wertheimer); hence sheifah, an internalized higher yearning |

## Where the authorities disagree

**The root of moshe.** The triliteralists (Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, Radak) give
מ-ש-ה; Menachem Ibn Saruk gives the biliteral מ-ש. Rashi (Shemos 2:10) takes up
the question over *mishisihu* and rules with the triliteralists, rejecting
Menachem by name.

**Whether moshe and moshech share a root.** Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche brings
both back to one biliteral מ-ש, "moving or removing something from its place,"
so that although his definitions leave a slight semantic difference, the root is
one. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim separates them by tracing them to *separate*
biliteral roots. He theorizes about triliteral roots that open with a *mem*: in
many of them the semantic core is the two letters after the *mem*, and the *mem*
itself flips that core to its exact opposite. His examples: ח-ק, engrave, against
מחק, erase; ל-צ, scorn, against מליץ, justification or defense; ע-ד, connecting
to an end-goal, against מעד, slipping or tripping, which sets one off-goal; ר-ד,
governing, against מרד, rebellion; ש-ל, excluded from one's domain, against
מושל, included in one's dominion; and נ-ע, movement, against מונע, withholding
or stopping. On that rubric *moshe* rests on ש-ה, which he traces further to the
monoliteral ש, "equalize," whence *shaveh* (equal), *shav* (futile, equally
purposeless however one looks at it), *nosheh* (a loan, which equates the
property of lender and borrower), *shaanan* (utter tranquility, a uniform state)
and *shoah*/*shayit* (utter destruction, likewise uniform); so *moshe* is the
polar opposite, disrupting a thing's prior uniformity and equilibrium by shaking
it up. *Moshech* rests on ש-ך, "to be settled or positioned properly," whence
*shochen* (dwelling), *shachen* (neighbor), *mashkon* (collateral, an item
temporarily housed by the lender), *keshoch*/*shechachah* (quelling, anger
abating back to one's normal state), *shechem* (shoulder, a limb positioned to
connect neck and upper arm), *eshech* (testes, positioned within the sac) and
*neshech* (biting, the teeth coming together as they naturally want to); so
*moshech* interrupts that well-groundedness to pull a thing from its place.
Pappenheim does recognize a biliteral מ-ש,
"feeling/touching," but does not see it as the core etymon of *moshe*.

**Which stage of drawing shoev names.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 24:65, Devarim
28:22): the water being drawn into and filling the bucket, and Targum Onkelos
bears this out, typically rendering *shoev* as *mamlei*, a cognate of *mimaleh*,
"filling." Rabbi Yaakov Berger of Kiryat Sefer, in his unpublished *Milon Leshon
Mikra*, infers from Ramban (Bereishis 24:17) that the core act is the lowering of
the bucket into the well to bring back water.

**Whether shoev and shoef are one word.**
- Rav Hirsch connects them through the interchangeability of *beis* and *pei*.
- *Midrash Sechel Tov* (to Bereishis 24:11), the earliest source to draw the
  connection, notes the thematic likeness between internalizing a yearning
  (*shoef*) and filling a container with water (*shoev*), and rejects the
  comparison on principle: he agrees to exegeses built on interchangeable
  letters in proper names, not in ordinary content words.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer writes that strictly speaking the two could be
  viewed as somewhat interchangeable, but that in practice *shoev* is drawing
  water, at least in Biblical Hebrew, and *shoef* is drawing air.

## Distinctions

### moshe (משה)
The given name Moshe appears in Tanach exactly 770 times, on Even Shoshan's
concordance, but the verb is rare. *"Ki min hamayim mishisihu,"* "I drew him from
the water" (Shemos 2:10); and, the only other clear examples, David asking
Hashem to "pull me out (*yamsheini*) of the multitude of water" (Shmuel II 22:17,
Tehillim 18:17). Rashbam (Shemos 2:10) fixes the sense: drawing out from within
water, which leaves open the possibility that *moshech* is the more general pull.
Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche says so explicitly, making *moshech* the hypernym and
*moshe* the water-specific species. A further possible instance is Yeshayahu
63:11, where Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim* records that some explain *moshe* as
this verb, while most commentators (Targum, Rashi, Mahari Kara, Ibn Ezra and
Radak there) take it as the proper noun, the verse reading "the bygone days of
Moshe and his nation." Rav Hirsch, also following the triliteralists, pairs
מ-ש-ה with מ-צ-ה, squeezing, through the interchangeability of *shin* and
*tzadi*: both apply pressure and extract from liquid, the one pulling something
from within the water, the other drawing liquid or juice from a solid such as
fruit.

Shadal (to Shemos 2:10) offers another theory altogether, suggesting that the
name Moshe and even the words *mishisihu* and *yamsheini* are not of Hebrew
origin at all but were borrowed into Hebrew from Egyptian, in contrast to
*meshech*, which seems to be a native Hebrew word.

### moshech (משך), shoev (שאב) and shoef (שאף)
*Moshech* has about forty appearances: pull something toward oneself, extended
to stretching, dragging, and leading; at Shoftim 5:14 it is used for writing,
*mosh'chim b'shevet sofer*, since ink flowing from the pen is akin to being pulled from it.
*Shoev*, root ש-א-ב, some twenty appearances, in almost all instances drawing
water from a well, an act done by lowering a bucket into the subterranean
wellspring, letting it fill, and raising it back toward oneself. Later Hebrew
widened it: in Mishnaic Hebrew the capillary action of a wick drawing up oil is
*shoev* (*Shabbos* 2:4); in Talmudic Hebrew a magnet is an *even shoeves*, a
drawing stone (Sotah 47a, Sanhedrin 107b, Avodah Zarah 44a); and in Modern
Hebrew a vacuum cleaner is a *shoev avak*, that which draws in dirt. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim traces it to ש-ב, "return," so that *shoev* stops the flow of water in
a wellspring by capturing it in a vessel. *Shoef*, root ש-א-ף, typically refers
to inhaling (Yirmiyahu 2:24, 14:6; Yeshayahu 42:14; Tehillim 119:131), a way of
pulling air into the lungs, and the same root gave way to *sheifah*, a higher
yearning or desire that one has internalized (Koheles 1:5; Iyov 7:2, 36:20).

### why Moshe and not Mashui
Many commentators have asked why he is called Moshe, "he who pulls," rather than
*Mashui*, "he who was pulled out." Was he not the one who *was* pulled from the
water?

Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes He'Aruch*) raises the question implicitly,
asserting that Moshe should have been called Mashui and that the one who did the
saving, Paroh's daughter, should have been the *Moshe*; he does not explain why
the name came out as it did. *Peirush HaTur* (to Shemos 2:10) gives the prosaic
answer in the name of Rabbi Yosef Kimchi: Paroh's daughter was simply not fluent
enough in Hebrew grammar to tell *poel* from *pual*, so she gave him a
grammatically inappropriate name; Rabbi Kimchi adds that there was an element of
Divine providence in it too, the name hinting at his future role in pulling the
Jewish People out of their exile in Mitzrayim.

Others read the active form as pointing at Moshe himself.
- Rabbi Moshe Alshich (to Shemos 2:10) suggests that in naming him Moshe she
  meant to express the magnetic charm that compelled her to leave the palace and
  bathe in the river (see Ibn Ezra there), as though *he* pulled *her* toward
  it.
- Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Ehrenreich similarly suggests the name reflects Moshe's
  power to draw the people after him, a magnetic personality, or his pulling
  Yisrael out of the impurities of Mitzrayim.
- *Peirush HaRokeach* (to Shemos 2:10): he was destined to pull the Jewish
  People out of the water at the Splitting of the Sea.
- The Tosafists (*Moshav Zekanim* to Shemos 2:10): she prophesied his drawing
  water from the rock in the wilderness.
- *Midrash HaGadol* (to Shemos 2:10): Moshe pulled himself out of the water, in
  that his own future merits caused her to save him.

Rabbi Menachem Mendel Kasher in *Torah Shleimah* cites an unpublished medieval
manuscript suggesting that Hashem arranged for her to name him Moshe because
משה is השם spelled backwards.

Two more go in a slightly different direction. Chizkuni
(to Shemos 2:10) implies that in giving the name she was praying that Moshe grow
up to be the one who liberates the Jewish People from bondage, as she had rescued
him from the water. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 2:10) advances a similar idea and makes it pedagogical: she called
him Moshe rather than Mashui so as to raise him ever conscious that his own life
had depended on the saving act of the one who drew him out, so that he in turn
would act to save others who are oppressed and extract them from their situations.

## Rendering guidance

- Keep "draw out of the water" for *moshe*; bare "drew" loses the name's point.
- *Moshech* takes "pull" when a person or object is moved, "draw" when the sense
  is toward oneself, "prolong" when it is time or sound.
- Where *shoev* and *shoef* stand near each other, render "draw" and "inhale";
  never let "draw in" cover both.
- Trap: a passage with both *moshe* and *moshech* reads as repetition in English
  unless the water is marked in the one and left out of the other.
- On why the name is Moshe (active) and not Mashui (passive), see the section
  above. Render "Moshe" and let a note carry the readings; Rabbi Klein does not
  pick among them, closing only that the name "encompasses various
  interpretations, reflecting his destiny to lead, save, and liberate."
