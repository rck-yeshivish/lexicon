# Sweet: matok / areiv / na'im

## Bottom line

*Matok* is **sweet** to the tongue; for Rabbi Pappenheim, the objective taste
opposed to bitter. *Areiv* is **pleasing**: for Rabbi Pappenheim, sweetness as
this particular person finds it; in Tanach it attaches to a voice, on one view
to a scent, and to food only once. *Na'im* is **pleasant**: visual appeal for
the Vilna Gaon, and for Rabbi Pappenheim not a physical property at all but the
transcendent pleasure of enjoying the thing. Where the Hebrew moves from
*matok* to *areiv* it has moved, on Rabbi Pappenheim's reading, from what
everyone tastes to what one person happens to love.

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

| נעים | na'im | pleasant | visual appeal (Vilna Gaon) or the soul's pleasure in a thing (Pappenheim) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What na'im names.**

- The Vilna Gaon: *na'im* and *noam* are visual appeal, as *matok* is gustatory
  appeal.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau: *na'im* names no physical property at all
  but the transcendent pleasure one derives from enjoying something sweet, so
  that *matok* and *areiv* refer to the physical and *na'im* to the sublime.

Rabbi Klein sets the two side by side and does not choose between them.

**Where areiv comes from.**

- According to etymologists, the origin of *areiv* is unknown, though some
  linguists suggest it first meant "to be well-mixed," related to *arev*, mixed:
  a glass of wine properly mixed with the right proportion of water is pleasant
  and especially appealing, and on their view that is the basis of *areiv* as
  "sweet."
- Rabbi Klein, for his part, argues from the whole ע-ר-ב family, which in his
  view always denotes a form of connection (see Distinctions).

The Vilna Gaon does not directly address *areiv*.

## Distinctions

### matok (מתוק)
Root מ-ת-ק. At Marah Hashem had Moshe cast a tree into the bitter (*mar*) waters
and made them *matok*. How does *matok* differ from *areiv*?

- The Vilna Gaon does not directly address that question, but distinguishes
  *matok*, gustatory appeal, from *na'im*/*noam*, visual appeal.
- In Tanach, words related to *matok* or *metek* commonly refer to culinary
  sweetness. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *matok* is the objective measure of taste,
  the kind everyone recognizes as sweet, found in honey, figs and dates, and the
  polar opposite of bitter. From there it was borrowed for anything one might
  argue is good, desirable, or worthwhile. Pappenheim adds a reading of the
  waters of Marah (Shemos 15:25): they are called *matok* not because they
  became objectively sweet but by contrast, because the bitterness they had was
  gone.

### areiv (ערב)
Root ע-ר-ב. Pappenheim: against *matok*, *areiv* is a *subjective* sweetness, an
acquired taste and a matter of personal choice. One with an affinity for bitter
food might say "bitter is *areiv* to me," but not "bitter is *matok* to me."

Rabbi Klein surveys the usage: *areiv* never appears in a
gastronomical context in Tanach except Mishlei 20:17; it is used of a voice
(Shir HaShirim 2:14, "your voice is *areiv*"), and one opinion in Berachos 43a has the berachah over balsam oil as
*borei shemen areiv*, which suggests it also applies to scent. So it seems that
*matok* refers specifically to a sweet taste, while *areiv* can also refer to a
sweet voice and a sweet scent.

Rabbi Klein offers a further reading of his own: every group of words built on
ע-ר-ב denotes a joining of things that were apart. An *arev* is a guarantor,
tying lender to borrower; *erev* joins day to night; *taarovet* and *irbuv* are
mixtures; the *eirev* is the woof running through the warp; an *eruv* makes many
domains one; the plague of *arov* was an assortment of beasts; the *erev rav*
were a mixed multitude. So too *areiv*: sweetness creates a connection between
the object and the one who beholds it, and the more pleasing a thing is, the
more one wants to come closer to it, so sweetness, as the impetus for that
link, is related to the words for binding. He frames this as parallel to Rabbi
Pappenheim's *na'im*.

### na'im (נעים)
Root נ-ע-ם.

- Vilna Gaon: visual appeal.
- Pappenheim: not a physical property but the transcendent pleasure of enjoying
  something sweet, so that *matok* and *areiv* speak of the physical and *na'im*
  of the sublime. He notes that the last two letters of the root spell *im*,
  "with," since the soul tends to bond with what it finds pleasant; so
  *na'im*/*noam* is the spiritual pleasantness that binds disparate entities and
  joins them together.

## Rendering guidance

- מתוק: "sweet," and keep it for the tongue. Where the sense is borrowed (for
  anything one might argue is good), "sweet" still carries; the word itself is
  a borrowing.
- ערב: "pleasing," or "pleasant to him" where the subject matters. Reserve
  "sweet" for *matok*; if *areiv* has to be "sweet" in a liturgical line
  (*ve'haarev na*, "make the words of Your Torah sweet in our mouths"), keep
  the personal sense visible in the surrounding words.
- נעים: "pleasant." Not "sweet," even where the parallelism tempts it.
- Trap: English "sweet" will absorb *matok* and *areiv* without complaint. Rabbi
  Klein suggests, with a "perhaps," why the two prayers use *areiv* rather
  than *matok*: we ask that the Torah be *areiv* in our mouths because we want
  it to appeal to us personally, and this might also be why we ask that our
  prayers be *areiv* before Hashem like korbanos, since, as opposed to
  paganism, a korban has no intrinsic value but only the value of Hashem's
  command, so it should appeal to Him by His personal favor. Render both with
  "sweet" and that suggestion disappears.
- When two of them stand in one phrase, translate for the axis, not the
  temperature (on Rabbi Pappenheim's scheme): *matok* is what it is, *areiv* is
  what it is to you, *na'im* is the pleasure of having it.
