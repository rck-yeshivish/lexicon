# Boats: oniah / sefinah / rafsodah (and the smaller craft)

## Bottom line

*Oniah* is the general Biblical word, **ship**; *sefinah* is the ordinary
Mishnaic word for the same thing, **ship** or **boat**, and appears only once in
Tanach. *Rafsodah* and *dovrah* are both a **raft** of tied beams; *avarah* a
**ferry**; *dugit* a small **fishing skiff**; *tzi* a **large ship**, usually a
warship. In a pasuk *sefinah* is a rarity, in a Mishnah the default word.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What *oniyos eiveh* means (Iyov 9:26).** Rashi: *eivah* is the name of a strong
river, seemingly implying boats for waters with strong currents. Ibn Ezra: a
place name, seemingly implying boats sturdy enough to make the journey to Eiveh;
alternatively, that *eivah* is a fruit or other commodity, so these are the boats
used to carry it. Ralbag: especially fast boats. Dr. Raphael Patai and Dr. Chaim
Tawil argue instead that *eiveh* is related to the Akkadian *abu*/*apu*, reeds,
so that these are boats made of reeds, typically insulated with tar and pitch.
Nothing settles it; render by what the argument needs, footnote the rest.

**Where *sefinah* comes from.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives four derivations
pulling in different directions: from *saf*, a liquid receptacle it resembles in
shape (Shemos 12:22; Shmuel II 17:28); from *safah*, "edge," since when not in use
it is housed at a port along the sea's edge; from its being less seaworthy than
an *oniah*, so that it must be kept closer to shore and is unsuitable for
deep-sea work; or from *tzafun*/*safun*, "hidden" or "embedded," since goods were
packed away and carried on it (Devarim 33:19 with Abarbanel there). Only the
third has a translation consequence, and Pappenheim himself only suggests it; the
Bottom line keeps *sefinah* and *oniah* equivalent. Rabbi Klein adds that he
seems to recall commentators explaining *safun* of the hull being immersed, and
so hidden, in the sea while only the upper part shows, but reports that he could
not locate the source.

**Whether a *dugit* is sold with the ship it is tied to.** Sumchos (*Tosefta*
Bava Basra 4:1): yes, since it was attached to the *sefinah* and typically towed
by the mother ship out at sea. Others seem to disagree, holding that the sale of
the larger ship cannot be assumed to include the smaller boat (Yerushalmi Bava
Basra 5:1; *Tosefta* Succah 3:2), and those sources call the small boat *iskupah*
instead. *Iskupah*, the column notes, is from the Greek *skaphe*, literally a
bowl, hence a bowl-shaped boat, and is the ancestor of the Germanic *scif*, from
which the English "ship," "skiff" and "skipper" come.

## Distinctions

### oniah (אניה)
First in Yaakov's berachah to Zevulun, who will settle the coast and live from
sea trade (Bereishis 49:13). Dr. Raphael Patai calls it the most basic, general
term for boats in Biblical Hebrew, and it takes modifiers for type: *oni
Tarshish* or *oniyos Tarshish*, ships able to travel as far as Tarshish, possibly
Tarsus or Tunis (Melachim I 10:22, 22:49; Yeshayahu 2:16; Tehillim 48:8);
*oniyos socher*, merchant ships equipped for long journeys (Mishlei 31:14); *oni
shayit* (Yeshayahu 33:21), either rowboats, since *shotim* are sticks or
paddle-rods (Radak), or a general term for boats that "swim," *shat*, on the
surface (Rashi). Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral א-נ, "where," since a ship goes where the
water pushes it, so a watcher asks where it is bound; Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis
16:8) says only that the *oniah* brings a person to a "where."

### sefinah (ספינה)
Once in Tanach, of Yonah's ship (Yonah 1:5), and thereafter the ordinary word,
across the Mishnah (Berachos 4:1, Shabbos 9:2, Bava Basra 5:1, Keilim 15:1,
Ohalos 8:1, and many more). It takes modifiers as *oniah* does: *sefinah
Alexandris*, the deep-sea ships out of Alexandria (Rambam to Keilim 15:1);
*sefinas haYarden*, the ferry across the Yarden (Shabbos 83b); *sefinos shel
piraton*, pirate ships (Shemos Rabbah 17:5). Why Yonah's vessel is called both
words in one perek is an open question: Rabbi Mordechai Koster put it to Rabbi
Klein, who reports that he failed to find a good answer.

### rafsodah (רפסודה) and dovrah (דוברה)
Rashi and Radak: both are seagoing rafts, beams lashed together (*dovros*,
Melachim I 5:23; *rafsodos*, Divrei HaYamim II 2:15). Even-Shoshan's dictionary
cites some scholars tying *rafsodah* to the Akkadian *rakasu*, tying or knotting.
In later Hebrew *rafsodah* is a footstool or ottoman (*Midrash Tanchuma*,
Bereishis 5), and in modern Hebrew it has been redefined as the wooden pallet
used for transporting goods. Rabbi Klein wonders aloud, without deciding,
whether the English "rhapsody" is related, its etymon being the ancient Greek
*rhapsoidein*, to weave or sew songs, which is not far from beams tied together;
the English "raft," he adds, does not seem to be related, being apparently from
the Old Danish *rafft*, a stick or beam.

### the smaller and Aramaic craft
*Avarah*, the ferry (Shmuel II 19:19), from *l'avor*, appears in the Gemara as
*ma'abara* or *mavra* (Shabbos 32a, Bava Kama 116a). *Siros dugah* are fishing
boats (Rashi and Radak to Amos 4:2), the Mishnaic equivalent being *dugit* (Bava
Basra 73a); Rabbi Dr. Daniel Sperber derives *dugit* from *dag*, fish, and
identifies it with the fisherman's boat the Greeks called a *lembus*. *Tzi* and
*tzim* are the large ships (Rashi to Bamidbar 24:24, Yeshayahu 23:13, and
*Sanhedrin* 106a), which Malbim adds are typically warships, built big and
strong. In Aramaic the Targumim render *oniah* sometimes *ilfa* (Yonah 1:3;
Tehillim 48:8, 104:26) and sometimes *sefinah*/*sefinta* (Bereishis 49:13;
Devarim 28:68; Melachim I 9:26), and Rashi glosses *ilfa* as *sefinah* in one
place (Avodah Zarah 10b) and as *oniah* in another (Eruvin 53b); Ilfa is also the
name of a sage (*Kesuvos* 69b). *Arva*, a small boat or yacht in Aramaic, is
spelled sometimes with an *ayin* and sometimes with an *aleph*, which Rabbi
Yaakov ben Yehudah Chazzan of London (*Eitz Chaim*) gives as an example of
Rashi's remark (to Shabbos 103b) that people sometimes write the one letter for
the other, the two sounds being close. *Borni* is a further Talmudic word for a
boat (*Rosh HaShanah* 23a; *Bava Metzia* 80b), and Rashi (to Shabbos 19b, 156b)
writes in the name of the Geonim that Aramaic *zuzi* means boat, possibly related
to *la'zuz*, to move.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: אניה "ship," ספינה "ship" in a Mishnah and "boat" where a smaller
  vessel is meant. Do not manufacture a size difference the sources do not fix.
- Trap: English "boat" covers *oniah*, *sefinah*, *dugit*, and *arva* at once.
  Where a sugya turns on two together, as in whether the *dugit* goes with the
  sale of the *sefinah*, keep "ship" for the large and "skiff" for the small.
- צי is "warship" only where Malbim is being followed; otherwise "great ship."
- Aramaic in a Gemara: אילפא is "ship," ערבא "small boat"; do not import the
  Hebrew distinctions into them.
