# Bread: lechem / kikar / challah / pat (rifta, nahamah)

## Bottom line

*Lechem* is the wide word, **bread** in the sense of the staple food, over three
hundred times in Tanach. *Kikar* seems to mean a circle; it is the **loaf**,
named for its round shape, but in Tanach it usually means a talent of metal or a
valley ringed by mountains, not bread. *Challah* in Tanach is a **meal-cake**,
almost always one brought as a korban, and in the Mishnah generally the
**tithe** separated from dough, not the loaf we call by that name today. *Pat* is a **morsel**, a broken piece, which became a plain synonym for
bread in Rabbinic usage. *Rifta* and *nahamah* are the Aramaic words for bread.

## Distinctions

### lechem (לחם)
Root ל-ח-ם.

- That *lechem* means bread may not be its literal sense at all but only a
  colloquialism: Rabbi Dr. Guy Matalon explains that *lechem* denotes the staple
  food of a society, which takes on different meanings by society. Among Klal
  Yisrael bread is the staple, so *lechem* is bread; in Arabic culture the
  staple is meat, so *lahm* is meat. On that reading, Rabbi Klein adds,
  historians cannot settle the original meaning of Beis Lechem (Bethlehem),
  which could be read "House of Bread" or "House of Meat."
- The same root gives *lochem*, to wage war, and *milchamah*; wars were often
  fought over the food supply, and the root also carries soldering or pressing
  things together, which fits both bread, whose parts are fused in baking, and
  combatants closing with each other on a battlefield.
- The Netziv (*Haamek Davar* to Bamidbar 28:2) relates *lechem* to *halchamah*,
  soldering, in the sense that food cements the connection between body and soul
  by keeping a person alive, and explains that korbanos are called *lechem*
  because they likewise cement the bond between Klal Yisrael and Hashem; he does
  not connect it to *milchamah*.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, discussing the biliteral ל-ח, whose core sense he
  takes as moisture, lists *lechem*, bread or meat, among its derivatives, as
  what sustains and rejuvenates a person's inner moisture.

At Bamidbar 21:5 the Manna is called *lechem haklokel*, the insubstantial bread.

### kikar (ככר)
The word seems literally to mean a circle. In Tanach it most commonly means a
talent of gold or silver, and secondarily a valley ringed by mountains; loaves
being commonly round in shape, it came also to mean a loaf, in the phrase *kikar
lechem* (Shemos 29:23, Shoftim 8:5, Shmuel I 10:3, Yirmiyahu 37:21, Mishlei
6:26, Divrei HaYamim I 16:3). The Mishnah later shortens the phrase to *kikar*
alone, and there *kikar* for bread is frequent, where in Tanach it is not.

### challah (חלה)
A baked cake of flour; Jastrow renders it specifically a rolled or rounded
meal-cake. *Challah*, like *kikar*, implies a round shape (see Ibn Ezra to
Vayikra 2:4). Nearly every occurrence in Tanach is a meal-cake offered
as a korban; the one exception is Bamidbar 15:20, the mitzvah to separate a
special tithe for the kohen, taken from every dough destined for a meal-cake,
and in the Mishnah, above all in *maseches Challah*, the word generally means
that tithe. The everyday sense, any loaf of
bread, is vernacular and later.

### pat (פת) and pita (פיתא)
*Pat* in Hebrew, *pita* in Aramaic. Radak (to Shmuel I 2:36): a broken piece, a
morsel. The imperative *pasos* at Vayikra 2:6 means to break into pieces. Of
fourteen occurrences in Tanach, eleven are the phrase *pas lechem*, a piece of
bread. In practice *pas* simply became another synonym for bread, which is how
Mishnah and Gemara commonly use it; Targum even renders *kikar* as *pas*
sometimes (Onkelos to Shemos 29:23).

### rifta (ריפתא) and nahamah (נהמא)
Both Aramaic words for bread in the Gemara; *nahamah* also runs through the
Zohar. Some linguists claim that *nahamah* is probably
borrowed from the Persian *nan*, "bread." *Rifta* is apparently related to
the Arabic *raghif*, a flat loaf; linguists claim that *rifta* was
originally written with a tzadi, tying its root to *retzef*/*ritzpah*, a floor,
which is likewise flat, the tzadi in Aramaic having morphed into an ayin that
was then dropped. In Tanach *rifos* are pounded or softened grain (Shmuel II
17:19, Mishlei 27:22). Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) explains Talmudic
*rifta* as a meal of two identical loaves; Rabbi Klein adds that these probably
had some other food in the middle, an early form of what we call a sandwich,
a food supposedly invented by John Montagu, the fourth Earl of Sandwich.

## Rendering guidance

- לחם in a pasuk: "bread." Where the sense is clearly provisions generally
  rather than a baked loaf, "food" or "sustenance" is right, but do not use it
  for a pasuk that is talking about actual bread.
- ככר לחם: "a loaf of bread." A bare ככר in Tanach is a talent (weight) or a
  plain; in the Mishnah it is a loaf. Check which corpus you are in before
  reaching for "loaf."
- חלה: in Chumash and in the korbanos, "meal-cake." In the Mishnah, "*challah*,"
  the separated portion, and say so in a gloss. Never write "challah bread" for
  either; the modern Shabbos loaf is not what the sources mean.
- Trap: English "loaf" will take both *kikar* and *challah*, and English "bread"
  will take *lechem*, *pas*, *kikar* and *challah* together. Where two of them
  stand in one phrase (*kikar lechem*, *pas lechem*), keep the head noun
  distinct: "a loaf of bread," "a piece of bread," never "some bread" for both.
- פת: "morsel" or "piece of bread" where the breaking is in view; plain "bread"
  in ordinary Rabbinic usage (*pas shacharis*, *pas akum*), where the sense of
  breaking has worn off.
- Aramaic ריפתא/נהמא in a sugya: "bread." Do not carry the *rifos* sense of
  pounded grain into them.
