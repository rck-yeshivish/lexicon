# Merchant: socher / rochel / cnaani (and tagar)

## Bottom line

For Malbim (to Yechezkel 17:4, 27:3) the three are a graded set: *socher* is
the **wholesaler**, *cnaani* the **trader** in the middle, *rochel*
the **peddler** who buys from the *socher* and goes door to door. Standing alone,
*socher* is simply **merchant**, and *tagar*, the Aramaic word, is also
**merchant** (Rashi, Eruvin 55a, makes him the local seller against the
travelling *socher*). *Cnaani*, in a string of pesukim (Bereishis 38:2,
Yeshayahu 23:8, Mishlei 31:24, Zechariah 14:21 as most commentators read it, and
with Rashi at Yechezkel 16:29, Hoshea 12:8, Iyov 40:30), means "trader," not
"Canaanite."

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether *socher* and *rochel* differ at all.**
- Rashi (to Erchin 23b) defines *rochel* by *socher*, showing that he saw the
  two as more or less synonymous.
- Malbim (to Yechezkel 17:4, 27:3) builds a three-tier trade: super-wholesalers
  who sail abroad in large ships to import, the *socher* who buys from them in
  bulk and supplies the *rochel*, and the *rochel* who buys from the *socher*
  and goes place to place, with the *cnaani* a middleman between the last two,
  selling on a smaller scale than the *socher* but a bigger man than the
  *rochel*; a similar understanding is implied by Rabbi Moshe
  Tedeschi-Ashkenazi, who writes that the *rochel* does not travel a *socher*'s
  distances.
- Rabbi Klein enters his own dissent: had he been asked, he would have put the
  *cnaani* at the top, as the super-wholesaler who supplies the *socher*, since
  the Canaanites were known for overseas trading ventures, which fits Malbim's
  description of those mega-suppliers.

**What a *rochel* sells.** Rashi, consistently and in many places (to Vayikra
19:16; Melachim I 10:15; Shabbos 90a, 91b; Yevamos 24b, 63b; Kiddushin 82a;
Bava Kamma 82a; Bava Basra 22a; Sanhedrin 100b), makes him a seller of
perfumes and fragrances for women, though in some contexts the word seems to
mean a merchant of any sort of goods (see pseudo-Rashi to Nazir 21a).

**Where *cnaani* as "merchant" comes from.**
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (*HaRechasim LaVikah* to Vayikra 16:21):
  the word may originally have been a gentilic for the nation, and widened, once
  the Canaanites were famous as traders, to anyone in the trade.
- Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit (*Leshon Chachamim*), and in a similar vein
  earlier Rabbi Yosef Nechemias (to Mishlei 31:24), take it from כ-נ-ע,
  submissiveness, as in *hachna'ah*, because a merchant typically speaks to his
  prospective customers in a subdued, servile way.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche suggests *cnaani* means merchant for the same
  reason *socher* does: its core root is the two-letter נ-ע, movement, as in
  *tenuah*, and it names the merchant's habit of being constantly on the road.

## Distinctions

### socher (סוחר)
Root ס-ח-ר, about sixteen times in Tanach, with verbs for trading and nouns for
merchandise; the Midianite *socharim* who passed by Yosef's pit and, the pasuk
implies, bought him and sold him to the Yishmaelim (Bereishis 37:28). The root
also carries "around": *secharchar* (Tehillim 38:11), round-going movement, and
modern Hebrew *secharchoret*, dizziness; Targum's usual rendering of *saviv* is
*s'chor*; and Chazal have the warning to a nazir who nears a vineyard, *lech
lech, sechor sechor*, "go, go, around, around, do not approach the vineyard"
(*Shabbos* 13a and parallels).
- Ibn Saruk and Ibn Janach seem to take the "merchant" and "around" senses as
  two unrelated meanings in one root.
- Radak bridges them, since a merchant typically travels around.
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (1821-1898) suggests that perhaps *socher*
  derives instead from *sahar*, "moon," the ches and hei interchanging, since
  just as the full moon is seen as a complete circle, so the *socher* goes
  around in a circuit selling his wares.

### rochel (רוכל)
Seventeen times, mostly in Yechezkel, with a feminine *rocheles*. The adjacent
*rachil* from the same root gives *rechilus*, talebearing, famously at Vayikra
19:16 and in five other passages (Yirmiyahu 6:28, 9:3; Yechezkel 22:9; Mishlei
11:13, 20:19), because merchants who travelled from place to place were often
taken for tale-mongers, carrying news and rumor from one town to the next.
Rashi (to Vayikra 19:16) reads the *kaf* as interchangeable with *gimmel*,
linking *rochel* to *regel*, "foot," and *meragel*, "spy"; Rabbi Avraham
Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*) explains that a merchant has in some ways to
function like a spy, going place to place to scout out the best merchandise to
buy and sell. Rabbi Klein adds his own suggestion, that perhaps a *rochel* would
typically travel by foot to hawk his goods, which bears similarities to the
"around" in *socher*. Rashbam (to Bava Basra 29b) sharpens the definition
further: the *rochel* goes around the various towns and comes back to his home
city only after a while.

### cnaani (כנעני)
Pesachim 50a holds that Yehudah's father-in-law could not have been a literal
Canaanite: if Avraham was so particular that Yitzchak not marry a Canaanite
woman (Bereishis 24:3, 24:37), and Yaakov's parents were as meticulous about
Yaakov (27:46, 28:1, 28:6), it is highly implausible that Yehudah would go
against the norm. Chazal therefore felt forced to read *ish cnaani* (38:2) as
his occupation, a merchant; Ramban (to 38:2) clarifies that even so he
incidentally lived in the land of Canaan, and see Radak there.

The reading is less farfetched than it sounds, since *cnaani* means "merchant"
in a number of pesukim. Yeshayahu 23:8 calls Tyre "the magnificent, whose
merchants (*socher*) are princes, whose *cnaanim* are the esteemed of the
earth," which Rashi and Radak read as merchants, *Metzudas Zion* adding that
*socher* and *cnaan* are synonyms set side by side for poetic effect. Tyre is associated with the Canaanite sub-group known as
Phoenician/Punic, a community largely made up of traders who won a reputation
in the ancient world for their success in overseas commerce. Echoing that,
Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743-1826) writes in *HaRechasim
LaVikah* (to Vayikra 16:21) that while *cnaani* may originally have been a
gentilic for the Canaanite people, once they gained fame as traders the word
widened to anyone in that trade. An inscription attributed
to Pharaoh Rameses III seemingly uses *cnaani* in this latter sense.

Zechariah 14:21 foretells a Messianic era, long after the Canaanites had ceased
to exist as a nation, in which there will be no *cnaani* in the Beis HaMikdash.
The commentators explain the prediction in various ways, most of them taking it
to mean that there will no longer be any need for merchants to offer their
goods, and Rashi (there) cites that explanation; alternatively Rashi reads
*cnaani* as a contraction of *ein kan ani*, "there is no poor man here," a
reference to the prosperity the Jewish People will enjoy then.

The *eishes chayil* makes linens and sells them, "and a sash she gives to the
*cnaani*" (Mishlei 31:24), where the commentators unanimously take *cnaani* as
a merchant, Rashi using the Aramaic *tagar*, Ibn Ezra and Ralbag the Hebrew
*socher*.

Against all this precedent, Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (1920-2016) notes a
major difficulty: Shua's daughter herself is called a *cnaanis* (Divrei HaYamim
I 2:3), which should mean "Canaanitess" literally, and it makes no sense for
the pasuk to call the daughter by an appellation that fits her father's
occupation, which suggests that *cnaani* said of the father is a nationality
after all. Unless, Rabbi Klein suggests, Shua's daughter was perhaps a trader
herself: Targum Rav Yosef to Divrei HaYamim there renders *cnaanis* as
*pragmatita*, a Greek term for a female merchant, which supports Chazal's
reading of the father's *cnaani* as well.

The Zohar (Lech Lecha 80a) says the *yetzer hara* is called *cnaani* because it
serves as a sort of merchant, supplying its customers with (evil) sustenance
like a sleazy salesman who hawks poor merchandise and convinces his buyers they
cannot live without it. In line with this, though without citing it, Meiri (to
Mishlei 31:24) explains the Woman of Valor's selling a sash to the *cnaani* as
her setting boundaries around the *yetzer hara* to rein it in. *Tanchuma*
(Masei 9) explains that the Holy Land is called *Eretz Cnaan* because it is the
land of merchandise; Rabbi Shlomo Fischer (1932-2021) clarifies that this is
not any physical commodity or natural resource but Torah, which is more
precious than any article of trade (Mishlei 3:14). This conjures the idea that
the land is especially suited to Torah study. Either way, *cnaan* there is being expounded
as merchants and merchandise, not genealogical Canaanites.

### tagar (תגר)
The word Chazal use when they gloss *cnaani*: Targum Onkelos and Targum
pseudo-Yonasan to Bereishis 38:2, *Bereishis Rabbah* 85:4, Pesachim 50a, Rashi
to Bereishis 38:2, and others; Targum also uses it for *socher* (Melachim I
10:28; Yeshayahu 23:8) and for *rochel* (Melachim I 10:15).

Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur (1469-1549), in his lexicon of Rabbinic Hebrew *Sefer
HaTishbi*, gives ת-ג-ר two separate entries, *tagar* ("merchant") and *teigar*
("dispute," "controversy," "fight"), and the fact that he divided them shows he
took the two as unrelated. Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894) writes in *Aruch
HaShaleim* to the same effect: *tagar* derives from the triliteral Aramaic root
ת-ג-ר, while *teigar* derives from the biblical Hebrew root ג-ר-(ה), to
instigate or incite.

On that basis Rabbi Klein argues his own point, that although *tagar* is
semantically synonymous with *socher*, the two cannot strictly be called
synonyms, since they come from two different languages. But Eruvin 55a says
Torah is not found among *socharim* or *tagarim*, who are always looking
elsewhere for the goods, and that wording implies the two do not quite mean the
same thing. Rashi (there) accordingly reads a real difference: the *socher*
goes around the various towns, the *tagar* is a local seller who stays in his
own town, so that on Rashi the difference is semantic and not only
etymological.

### pragmatia and melay
*Pragmatia*/*prakmatia* in Rabbinic Hebrew are Greek loanwords for
"merchandise," etymologically related to the English *pragmatic* and
*practical*; Rashi (to Moed Katan 10b) defines *pragmatia* as *sechorah*,
which brings the cluster full circle. *Melay*, "merchandise," "stock," seems to
be a cognate of *maleh*, "full," yet Rashi defines it as *sechorah* (Pesachim
53b), as *pragmatia* (Shabbos 56a), or as both (Sotah 47b).

## Rendering guidance

- One word alone: סוחר "merchant," רוכל "peddler," תגר "merchant." Where two or
  three stand together, grade them: "wholesaler," "trader," "peddler."
- Trap: English "merchant" absorbs all four. Yeshayahu 23:8 is the test, since
  *socher* and *cnaani* stand in one sentence; render "merchants" and "traders,"
  not "merchants" twice.
- כנעני in Bereishis 38:2, Mishlei 31:24, Yeshayahu 23:8, Yechezkel 16:29, Hoshea
  12:8, and Iyov 40:30 is "merchant." Writing "Canaanite" at Bereishis 38:2
  contradicts Pesachim 50a.
- רכילות stays "talebearing," not "peddling." Where a source pairs *socher* with
  *tagar* (Eruvin 55a), keep "travelling merchants" against "local dealers."
