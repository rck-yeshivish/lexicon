# Beast and boor: be'ir / behemah / chayah / ba'ar / bur

## Bottom line

*Behemah* is the ordinary word for a **domesticated animal**; *chayah* a **wild
animal**; *be'ir* a rare word for a **beast**, and for Rav Hirsch the most
subdued of the three, doing nothing but what instinct drives it to. The same root
gives *ba'ar*, the **brute** or **boor**, and on one view the Mishnaic *bur*, the
**uncultured man**. A pasuk that pairs *ba'ar* with *behemah* (Tehillim 73:22) may allude to the connection between beast and boor.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| בעיר | be'ir | beast, livestock | wholly subdued, instinct-driven; cognate with ba'ar (Rav Hirsch) |
| בהמה | behemah | domesticated animal | the standard term; a platform for man's elevation (Rav Hirsch) |
| חיה | chayah | wild animal | independent life, not subservient to man (Rav Hirsch) |
| בער | ba'ar | brute, boor | sub-human for want of intelligence, like a be'ir (Edel, Malbim) |
| בור | bur | uncultured man | uncultivated field (Rashi to Avos 2:5) or empty pit (Weiser) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**The root of behemah.**
- Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer HaShorashim*): a triliteral ב-ה-ם, a
  single beast or animals in general.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: the beis is a prefix and the root is ה-מ-ה,
  "noise, confusion," since animals make confused sounds while human speech
  carries meaning.
- *Ohalei Yehuda* offers a merger of בה with המה, and alternatively *bah mah*,
  "what is in her?", the creature's insignificance beside man (compare Shemos
  16:7-8).

**Where be'ir sits in the root ב-ע-ר.** Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak all take
*be'ir* from the triliteral ב-ע-ר, a root that carries removing, grazing,
consuming, destroying, burning, kindling, and foolishness.
- Ibn Janach files *be'ir* under grazing, since animals are typified by eating.
- Ibn Saruk files it under *ba'ar*, "fool."
- Rashi (to Shemos 22:4) seems to weigh in for grazing, though his exact intent
  remains unclear (see Rabbi Yaakov Yechiel Weinberg, *Seridei Aish* vol. 4
  *Chakiras HaMekoros* §7:2).
- Against them, Mitchell First (*Words for the Wise*, Kodesh Press 2022, p. 149)
  reasons that *be'ir* is unrelated to any of the other senses of ב-ע-ר, and
  notes that an Arabic cognate of that root means "camel" while in some
  dialects, South Arabic among them, it is a general word for animals much as
  biblical *be'ir* is; on that basis Rabbi Shet bar Yefet (*Chemas HaChemdah* to
  Shemos 22:4) argues that *be'ir* is in fact Arabic and not Hebrew.

Others read the bet of *be'ir* as no part of the root.

- Rabbi Shlomo Yehuda Rapoport, a son-in-law of the *Ketzos HaChoshen* who is
  typically counted a maskil, argues that *be'ir* should be read "in the city,"
  the bet being the prefix and *ir* the city, which he takes to reflect that
  *be'ir* means specifically the domesticated animals one would find in an urban
  setting, as against *behemah*, which can take in wild animals too (Tehillim
  8:8).
- Similarly, Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche takes the core as ע-ר, "bare": the fool
  is bereft of the adornment of wisdom and so left bare, and the grazing animal
  bereaves the field of its produce and leaves it bare.

**Which way the metaphor runs.**

- Edel and the Malbim: a *ba'ar* is so called because his want of intelligence
  makes him like a *be'ir*.
- *Ohalei Yehuda* reverses it: the animal behaves like a *ba'ar*.

Either way, the connection may be alluded to in Tehillim 73:22.

**What bur means.**
- Some explain it as a cognate of the other Mishnaic *bur*, an uncultivated,
  desolate field (Peah 2:1; Targum Onkelos to Bereishis 47:19; so Rashi, the
  Tashbetz, the Bartenura and others to Avos 2:5).
- Rabbi Dr. Asher Weiser: from *bor*, a pit, the man whose mind is empty, and he
  links *be'er*, a wellspring, which holds only so much water, and *biur*,
  explanation, the antidote to the *ba'ar*'s ignorance, to that family.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*): a form of *ba'ar* (Targum to
  Mishlei 12:1, 30:2).

All three agree on the sense and differ on the image.

**Whether the English boor belongs here at all.** Rabbi Klein had
assumed the English word came from the Hebrew *bur*, and when asked, the
translator and literary critic Hillel Halkin, writing as Philologos, replied
that the two are unrelated: Rabbinic Hebrew *bur*, he holds, is closer to the
Aramaic *bar*, outside (as in *baraisa*), and has nothing to do with either
biblical *ba'ar* or English *boor*. Halkin adds that the two words reach a
similar meaning from opposite ends, *bur* out of rural values, where the
ignorant mind is unfarmed land, and *boor* out of urban ones, where no one is as
ignorant as a farmer. Etymologists give two main accounts of the English word:
some derive it from the French *bouvier*, a herdsman, and so from the Latin
*bovarius*, bovine, from *bos*/*bov* (the Celtic tribe called the Boii takes its
name from Proto-Indo-European cognates of the same word for cattle, and lives on
in the names Bohemia and Bavaria, as the ox's plowing lives on in
*boustrophedon*, a script that runs one line left to right and the next right to
left); others tie it to the Old English *gebur*, a dweller, peasant or farmer,
cognate with the English *neighbor* and the German *bauer*.

## Distinctions

### be'ir (בעיר)
Only six occurrences in the sense of "animal" (Bereishis 45:17; Shemos 22:4;
Bamidbar 20:4, 20:8, 20:11; Tehillim 78:48), against nearly two hundred for
*behemah*. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:24, 45:17; Shemos 22:4; Vayikra 1:2;
Tehillim 73:21):
a creature totally subdued, more so than the merely domesticated *behemah*,
trapped in a cage and doing only what instinct pushes it to do to survive, and so
cognate with *ba'ar*, the boorish man likewise driven by nothing but his
animal instincts. These nuances are lost in Targumic Aramaic, which typically renders both
*behemah*
and *be'ir* as *be'ira*. Some commentators read the name Bilam ben Beor here
too, as an allusion to the Gemara's account of Bilam and his donkey: Bilam
parses as a metathesized form of *boel* and Beor as a form of *be'ir* (*Meor
Ha'Afeilah* to Bamidbar 22:2; *Yad Ramah* to Sanhedrin 105a).

### behemah (בהמה) and chayah (חיה)
- Rav Hirsch: *behemah* is related to *bamah*, an elevation or platform, since
  the animal subservient to man carries his menial work and so lifts him toward
  loftier things; alternatively animals are the lower rung of life, figuratively
  trodden upon by their servility to man, and he compares ב-ה-ם to פ-ע-ם,
  "stepping," by the interchange of bet with peh and hei with ayin. *Chayah* he
  takes from *chai*, life: the wild animal is an independent life-form, or
  alternatively animal life is a higher order than *tzomeach*.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus derives *behemah* from the root ב-ה-ה, an onomatopoeic
  representation of a common animal sound ("baaaa"), and links it to the ancient
  Latin and Old Irish *bo(s)*, related to the English *bovine*.

For the fuller treatment of this pair, including Ramban's division by diet, see
[animal](animal-behemah-chayah.html).

### ba'ar (בער) and bur (בור)
*Ba'ar* is Biblical, *bur* Mishnaic. Avos 2:5: a *bur* cannot be a fearer of
Heaven. Dovid HaMelech puts the pair in one pasuk: "I am a *ba'ar* and I do not
know, I was like an animal (*behemos*) with You" (Tehillim 73:22), which may allude to
the connection between the beast and the boor.

Pri Megadim (*Igeres* 6, p. 340 in the Friedman *Shulchan Aruch*, vol. 1): a
*bur* is a farmer.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "beast" swallows *be'ir* and *behemah* both, and "brute"
  swallows *ba'ar* and *bur*. Fix *behemah* as "animal," and reserve "beast" for
  *be'ir* where the two stand together.
- Tehillim 73:22, where *ba'ar* and *behemah* sit in one pasuk, wants two
  different English words: "brute" for the man, "animal" for the comparison.
- Shemos 22:4, the damages pasuk, is *be'ir*: "his animal" reads naturally, but
  where the sugya turns on the word, say "his beast" and gloss.
- Do not render *bur* "boor"; that English word carries a different insult.
