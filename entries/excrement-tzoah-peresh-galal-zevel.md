# Excrement: tzoah / peresh / galal / zevel (ree, chara)

## Bottom line

*Tzoah* is the general word and the human one: in Tanach and in the Mishnah it
never means animal droppings. The others are each specific. *Peresh* is what is
still inside the slaughtered animal and was never voided, and in Tanach it
belongs to the sacrifices. *Zevel* is animal manure, and unlike *peresh* it has
already left the body. *Galal* is named for its round shape, the pellet.
*Ree* is what the body could not take in because too much was eaten. *Chara*
is the coarse word, and the Mesorah will not let it be read aloud, substituting
*tzoah* in its place.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What the root of tzoah is.** The grammarians are unanimous that it is a
cognate of *yetziah*, going out, and divide only on how far back the root
reduces: Radak to the triliteral י-צ-א, Menachem Ibn Saruk to the biliteral
צ-א, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim to the single letter צ.

**What peresh names.** The early lexicographers, Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach
and Radak, trace it to פ-ר-ש, which also means spreading, separating and
elucidating, so that *peresh* is droppings strewn about by an animal, and this
holds even though the word in fact names the matter before it leaves the body.
Rabbi Pappenheim takes the same root the other way: what is separated off, the
byproducts of digestion parted from the nutriment and not absorbed.

**Where techorim comes from.** Four readings, and the difficulty driving them
is that *techorim* is the clean word the Mesorah substitutes, yet it looks to
share the root of *chara*, which is the word too offensive to say.

1. Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak: the triliteral ט-ח-ר, which simply
   means hemorrhoids and nothing else.
2. Rabbi Yitzchak of Zeldin (*Shoresh Yesha*): a portmanteau of the biliterals
   ט-ח, a trajectory or path, and ח-ר, a hole.
3. Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916): kin to *achor*, behind, for where the
   affliction sits.
4. Rabbi Yaakov Yehuda Zilberberg (1914 to 2003), who offers his etymology
   because of that very difficulty: ט-ר-ח, *tircha*, a burden or bother, by
   metathesis, hemorrhoids being a bother to a man.

Rabbi Klein adds an observation of his own: Rabbi Shabsai Donnolo (913 to 982)
uses *techor* of the rectum, as a body part, and if that is the word's primary
sense then it is plain why *techor* should be less offensive than *afolim*; he
notes that he has not seen an earlier source using it that neutrally.

## Distinctions

### tzoah (צואה)

The general word, and the human one. The Torah has it where the camp must keep
its latrines outside, "and you shall cover your *tzoah*" (Devarim 23:14), and it
is the commonest word in the Mishnah (*Berachos* 3:5, *Shabbos* 16:7, *Avodah
Zarah* 4:5, *Keilim* 10:2, *Mikvaos* 9:2 and 9:4, *Machshirin* 5:6). In Tanach
and in the Mishnah alike it is human feces and never animal droppings.

Rabbi Pappenheim draws the consequence of the etymology: since *tzoah* is
whatever waste leaves the body, it covers vomit too (Yeshayahu 28:8).

### peresh (פרש)

In Tanach it belongs to the sacrifices, every time (Shemos 29:14; Vayikra 4:11,
8:17, 16:27; Bamidbar 19:5; Malachi 2:3). Rabbi Pappenheim and Rabbi Shmuel
Strashun of Vilna (1794 to 1872) make it the fecal matter found within a
slaughtered animal's intestines that was never excreted, and Rabbi Strashun sets
it against *zevel*, which is what has already been voided.

### galal (גלל)

Five times in Tanach as dung (Melachim I 14:10; Yechezkel 4:12, 4:15;
Tzefaniah 1:17; Iyov 20:7). Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970) traces it to the
biliteral ג-ל, whose core sense Rabbi Pappenheim gives as roundness: *galgal*, a
wheel; *gulgoles*, a skull; *gal*, a heap of stones set in a circle;
*megillah*, a scroll, which is rolled. So *galal* is named for the round,
cylindrical form of the pellet.

By a later borrowing it came to mean anything left outside that is a public
nuisance, whether trash (Rashi to *Bava Kamma* 29b, *Bava Basra* 28a) or stones
(Rashi to *Bava Kamma* 92b, *Sanhedrin* 109b). Rashi (to Devarim 29:16) ties
*gilulim*, idols, to *galal*, because idols are putrid and disgusting as
excrement is.

### zevel (זבל)

Modern Hebrew has made it trash, garbage, rubbish, but that is a neologism. The
word is absent from Tanach altogether and appears in many places in the Mishnah,
where it usually means animal manure. Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi, a grammarian
of the thirteenth century, derives the noun from the verb of fertilizing a
field, which is what fixes it on animal excrement.

### ree (ראי)

Iyov 33:21, Nachum 3:6, Tzefaniah 3:1. Though written with a reish and an
aleph, Rabbi Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ר-ו, quenching or satisfying,
and makes it the excrement that results from eating past what the body can
absorb, so that the surplus passes through.

It appears four times in the Mishnah, once spelled reish-aleph-yud
(*Machshirin* 6:7) and three times reish-ayin-yud (*Shabbos* 20:4, *Makkos*
3:14, *Keilim* 17:2), the Biblical aleph having become an ayin in Mishnaic
Hebrew (see Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi to Vayikra 1:16 and Rabbi Avraham Bakrat's
*Sefer Zikaron* there). Rashi (to Vayikra 1:16) connects *mur'ah*, a bird's
crop, to *ree*. On the vowels, Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur has the Mishnaic form as
*rayee*, while the Kaufmann manuscript usually has *ree*, and in *Makkos*
*reeee*.

### chara (חרא)

The coarse word, and so uncommon that it survives in Tanach only as what is
written and not as what is read. *Megillah* 25b lists the places where the
Mesorah's reading departs from its writing because the written word is too
offensive to say. Four of them bear here.

- Devarim 28:27, the curse of untreatable hemorrhoids: *afolim* written,
  *techorim* read.
- Melachim II 6:25, the famine in Shomron in which dove dung grew dear:
  *charyonim*, the *chara* of doves, written, *divyonim*, what flows from doves,
  read.
- Melachim II 18:27 and Yeshayahu 36:12, where Ravshakeh taunts Yehudah that
  they sit on the wall to eat their own excrement: *chara* written, *tzoah*
  read.
- Melachim II 10:27, where Yehu pulled down the temple of Baal and made it a
  latrine: a cognate of *chara* written, a cognate of *tzoah* read.

In the last three the vulgar word is read as the clean one.

On the root, Menachem Ibn Saruk and Rabbi Pappenheim both give the biliteral
ח-ר. Menachem says one of its senses is a hole, as in *chor*, pointing to where
the excrement leaves the body. Rabbi Pappenheim goes further back and makes the
core sense "other than the one in front of us," which yields *acher*, another;
*machar*, tomorrow, a day other than today; *achar*, after, a time other than
now; and *achor*, the back, the side other than the one facing us. The sense of
a hole he then derives from *achor*, since the hole in question is on a man's
backside.

### techorim (טחורים) and afolim (עפלים)

The pair the Mesorah swaps at Devarim 28:27, *afolim* written and *techorim*
read. For *techorim* see the four derivations above. For *afolim*, Rabbi Shes
bar Yefes in *Chemas HaChemdah* (to Bamidbar 14:44) relates the root ע-פ-ל to
א-פ-ל, darkness, on the interchange of ayin and aleph, so that the lesions are
named for the dark part of the body they afflict.

## Rendering guidance

"Excrement" belongs to *tzoah*, which is the general word and the only one that
is specifically human; using it for the others loses what each of them adds.
*Peresh* wants a rendering that keeps it inside the animal, so "dung" alone is
too loose where the contrast with *zevel* is live, and something like
"intestinal contents" or "the dung in its innards" carries Rabbi Strashun's
distinction. *Zevel* is "manure," *galal* is "dung" and can take "pellet" where
the shape is the point, and *ree* has no one English word: where its sense
matters it needs a phrase.

*Chara* is the trap. Because Tanach never has it read, a translation that puts a
coarse English word in the text reverses what the Mesorah does; the received
reading is *tzoah*, and a note is the place for the written form. The same holds
at Devarim 28:27, where the read word is *techorim*.

For the etymologies, Rabbi Klein observes that English *excrement* happens to
work the same way the Hebrew words do: *ex-* is "out," as *tzoah* is, and
*crement* is from the Latin *cernere*, to sift or separate, as *peresh* is.
