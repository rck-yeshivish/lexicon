# Speech: dibbur / amirah

## Bottom line

*Dibbur* is speech as an act (**speak, address, discourse**): forceful, bold,
and complete whether or not anyone receives it. *Amirah* is speech as
communication (**say, tell**): gentle, aimed at a hearer who takes the
meaning in. Rashi's "*dibbur* is harsh, *amirah* is soft" is limited from
several directions (Mecklenburg, the Gemara on *yadber*, Maharal, *Moshav
Zekanim*). Rav Hirsch reads the pair as act of speech against act of
communication. But the direction is not settled: Malbim reverses the force of
the two, and the Vilna Gaon to Bamidbar 22:5 seems to make *dibbur* the more
communicative of them. *Aseres HaDibros* is "Ten Sayings" or "Decalogue"; Rabbi
Klein's line is that this "would be better" than "Ten Commandments," said with
all due respect to Cecil B. DeMille and Charlton Heston. He renders *Asarah
Maamaros* "Ten Sayings" too.

## Distinctions

### dibbur (דיבור)
Root ד-ב-ר.
- Rashi (to Shemos 6:2 and 32:7): *dibbur* is harsh speech, learned from "the
  man, the master of the land, spoke (*dibber*) with us harshly" (Bereishis
  42:30), and set against *amirah* as supplicatory speech that seeks the
  listener's favor (to Bamidbar 12:1).
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865) limits this: not every *dibbur*
  in the Torah has to mean harsh speech, and *vayedaber* to Dinah's heart
  (Bereishis 34:3) was intense but not necessarily harsh, since otherwise she
  presumably would not have acceded; and Rashi himself (to Vayikra 10:19) reads
  *dibbur* as boldness, not necessarily harshness.
- *Yadber* (Tehillim 47:4), a rare form, means a kind of leadership, and one
  might be tempted to hear strong, forceful terms in it, but the Gemara
  (*Makkos* 11a) separates the two: *dibbur* connotes harshness and *yadber*
  actually connotes softness.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk lists *dibbur* and *yadber*, "leading" or "exterminating," among the distinct
  meanings of ד-ב-ר, and Radak bridges the two: animals are led by the speech of
  the one in charge, who commands them where to go.
- Maharal (*Nesivos Olam*): in the context of Torah study *dibbur* is always
  pleasant, and only in other contexts is it harsh.
- *Moshav Zekanim* (to Shemos 6:2) confines the harsh sense to the Chumash,
  against the rest of Tanach.
- *Sifsei Chachamim* on Devarim 2:16 deals with the implication of Rashi's
  comment there that *dibbur* is a term of endearment.
- Zohar (*Idra Rabbah*, the commentary to Parashas Naso, 132b): *dibbur*
  requires raising the voice to make a forceful announcement; *amirah* does not.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814, *Yerios Shlomo*): *dibbur*
  is the act of using the vocal cords, whether or not it produces sound with a
  particular meaning.
- Rav Shimshon Raphael Hirsch (1808 to 1888) follows this basic approach and
  expands it: *dibbur* is the simple, physiological act of expressing an idea in
  words, and because it is the act itself it applies even with nobody around to
  hear or understand; it is a conglomeration of phonemes meant to express an
  idea.
- Malbim, at odds with this: *amirah* is absolute while *dibbur* is a suggestion
  or proposition, not necessarily absolute.
- *Dibah*, slander or malicious speech, does not seem to be related to *dibbur*:
  the biliteralists trace it to ד-ב and the triliteralists to ד-ב-ב (*Yalkut
  Shorashim*, Rav Hirsch). Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis
  37:2) relates it instead to ז-ב, *zav*, flowing, dalet and zayin interchanging
  as between Hebrew and Aramaic: speech is likened to liquid falling or dripping
  down, Hashem's word to rain and snow (Yeshayahu 55:10), Moshe's words to dew
  (Devarim 32:2; see Yechezkel 21:2, Iyov 29:22), so *dibah* is malice that
  flows and drips from the speaker's mouth.
- Rabbi Yitzchak bar Yakar (on the Yom Kippur prayer *umah nesaper lefanecha
  shochen shechakim*): *sippur* is the kind of saying that implies the most
  knowledge on the speaker's part.
- HaKsav VeHaKabbalah (to Devarim 4:13): *dibbur* is the general term for
  speech, which takes in all the others.

### amirah (אמירה)
Root א-מ-ר.
- Rashi (to Shemos 19:3): "so shall you *say* (*tomar*) to the House of Yaakov,"
  the women, gently, since Moshe was to broach the idea of receiving the Torah
  with them in a gentle manner.
- Rabbi Pappenheim: *amirah* is speaking in order to convey a certain message
  that must have a particular meaning; he also derives it from the two-letter
  root מ-ר, "switching" or "exchanging," which fits, since *amirah* is the
  exchange of ideas and in polite dialogue the parties constantly switch between
  being vocal and being quiet. Rabbi Wolf Heidenheim (1757 to 1832), in his
  comments to *Yerios Shlomo*, notes that Ibn Ezra and Rabbi Yoel Ibn Shuaib
  (both to Tehillim 19:4) offer very similar approaches.
- Rav Hirsch: *amirah* is not simply verbalizing a thought but an act of
  communication, said *to* somebody who then translates the sounds he hears back
  into the ideas they express.
- Mecklenburg expands the מ-ר sense: *amirah* is speech that creates a reality,
  switching the situation into something different.
- Malbim, second approach: *amirah* is for short statements, *dibbur* for the
  longer discourse that elaborates on and explains them.
- Rabbi Shmuel Jaffa-Ashkenazi of Istanbul (1525 to 1595) similarly calls
  *amirah* the "headings" or "headlines" of a topic without the details,
  supporting it from Yeshayahu 17:6, which foretells that Sancheriv will not
  conquer Yerushalayim just as a harvester cannot reach the olives on the
  uppermost branch (*rosh amir*): the *amir* is the branch, not all of its
  contents.
- The Vilna Gaon (1720 to 1797, to Bamidbar 22:5, second version) *seems* to run
  the other way and understand *dibbur* as implying more of a form of
  communication than *amirah* does.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim (to Devarim 32:1): the heavens were created with *dibbur*
  (*bidvar Hashem shamayim naasu*, Tehillim 33:6) and the earth with *amirah*
  (*ki lasheleg yomar hevei aretz*, Iyov 37:6), since Hashem created the earth
  from snow (see *Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer* ch. 3).
- Kedushas Levi (to Bereishis 21:1): *amirah* is more intimate and quiet,
  *dibbur* out in the open.

### maamar (מאמר) against dibrah (דיברה)
Why *Asarah Maamaros* for creation (*Avos* 5:1) and *Aseres HaDibros* at Sinai?

- From the Zohar's line that *dibbur* requires raising the voice, Rabbi Klein
  argues: the whole point of raising the voice is to make a forceful impression
  on a listener, so at creation, with no listeners yet, Hashem did not need to
  raise His voice and those sayings are *maamaros*, while at Sinai there was an
  assembled audience and those are *dibros*.
- Rav Hirsch's application is his own: *maamar* requires an active listener on
  the receiving end to hear the utterance and translate it into reality, and at
  creation each utterance took effect at once, while *dibbur* exists
  independently of the listener, the Decalogue being Hashem's revelation of His
  absolute will, which continues to exist whether or not anybody follows it.
- *Sefer HaChochmah* (ascribed to the late twelfth century Ashkenazic scholar
  Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms): *amirah* denotes setting up a framework and
  context within which a *dibbur* can be said, so on this understanding the
  *Asarah Maamaros* set up a reality within which the *Aseres HaDibros* can have
  relevance.
- Rabbeinu Bachaya (to Shemos 13:1): *dibbur* alludes to the Written Torah,
  *amirah* to the Oral Torah, an idea many have cited and expanded, including
  the Vilna Gaon, Rabbi Pinchas Horowitz of Frankfurt (1731 to 1805) and Rabbi
  Naftali Tzvi Yehudah Berlin, the Netziv (1816 to 1893). Interestingly, *Amora*
  (derived from *amirah*) is the Talmudic sage who expounds the scriptures and
  laws, the hero of the Oral Torah. The Vilna Gaon reads *Haazinu* by it:
  "Listen, heavens, and I will speak (*va'adabeirah*)," the Written Torah
  revealed from above, "and let the earth hear the sayings (*imrei*) of my
  mouth" (Devarim 32:1), the Oral Torah brought out by the earth's inhabitants
  below.
- Zohar (Bereishis 239b): in *Vayedaber Hashem el Moshe leymor*, the *leymor*
  reveals the hidden elements not included in the *vayedaber*, so *amirah* is an
  expansion upon *dibbur*. Rabbi Klein runs this into a parallel: as the Written
  Torah is a fixed canonized text and the Oral Torah an expansion on it, so the
  Zohar (Shemos 161a) teaches that Hashem looked into the Torah and created the
  world, making the Torah the fixed canon and the world the expansion; so the
  speech expressing the Torah is *dibbur*, and the words creating the world
  *amirah*.

Malbim's two approaches feed the same question.

- On the first (*amirah* absolute, *dibbur* a proposition), it seems to Rabbi
  Klein that the words that made the world, and so the rules of nature, must
  have been absolute and forceful, since only Hashem can break them, while the
  Decalogue connotes a more malleable reality, some prohibitions being
  legitimately suspendable in certain circumstances. Rabbi Moshe Feinstein (1895
  to 1986) uses the exact opposite reasoning to explain why *tzitzis*, not
  absolutely obligatory but essentially optional, opens with *vayomer* rather
  than the usual *vayedaber* (Bamidbar 15:37).
- On Malbim's second approach, with Jaffa-Ashkenazi, Rabbi Klein argues that
  verbosity is a rhetorical device for making sure an audience understands, so
  at creation, with no intended audience, Hashem could be as brief as He wished,
  just the headlines, hence *maamaros*, whereas at Sinai He sacrificed brevity
  for clarity, hence *dibros*.
- Following Mecklenburg's "switching" logic, Rabbi Klein adds that the sayings
  that made the world fit an *amirah* verb because they were the ultimate change
  in reality, from nothing to something.

Rabbi Klein's own answer is psychological: when a person feels the weight of
living up to expectations, those expectations generally feel as though they
were said in harsh, demanding terms even if objectively they were not, so the
Decalogue, which lays out Hashem's expectations of us, is *Dibros*; whereas the
*Asarah Maamaros* that created the world are Hashem's gift, and a gift is taken
as an expression of love, a *maamar*. (In Talmudic usage *maamar* denotes a form
of marriage instituted by the Rabbis as a prelude to consummating *yibbum*.)

### he'emarta / he'emircha (האמרת / האמירך) and chativah (חטיבה)
Devarim 26:17-18.
- Rashi: forms of separation and division; Israel set Hashem apart from the
  false gods, and Hashem set Israel apart from the nations.
- Rashi's alternative: forms of glory and pride, citing *yisamru* (Tehillim
  94:4).
- Rav Saadia Gaon (882 to 942) adds two more. First, expressions of "being on
  top," just as the *amir* (Yeshayahu 17:6) is the most important branch of a
  tree because it is on top: Hashem put the Jews on top and the Jews put Hashem
  on top. This is also related to the Arabic *Emir*, "military commander" or
  "tribal chief," who sits atop the hierarchical society he presides over (see
  Bartenura to *Shekalim* 5:3). Dunash ibn Labrat (925 to 990), Rav Saadia's
  interlocutor, preferred this first approach, which in some ways resembles
  Rashi's glory and pride reading; Ibn Ezra (to Devarim 26:17) likewise explains
  the words as referring to greatness.
- Rav Saadia's second reading takes them plainly as what Hashem *said* to Israel
  ("I am Hashem your God") and what Israel *said* about Him ("Hashem our God,
  Hashem is one"). Mecklenburg reads the doublet *emor* and *ve'amarta* of
  Vayikra 21:1 similarly: the first denotes raising the *kohanim's* status,
  making them great again, the second simply that these laws should be "said."
- Many chassidishe commentators (Rabbi Pinchas Horowitz of Frankfurt, 1731 to
  1805, and his brother Rabbi Shmuel Shmelka Horowitz of Nikolsburg, 1726 to
  1778; Rabbi Kalonymus Kalman Epstein of Krakow, 1753 to 1823; Rabbi Yaakov
  Yosef of Ostrog, 1738 to 1791; Rabbi Shimon Maryles of Jaroslaw, 1758 to 1849;
  and many others) read *amirah* as an expression of love, so that the passages
  mean Hashem showed His love for the Jewish People and they in turn showed
  their love for Him. What is the philological basis? One might perhaps suggest
  the Latin *amor*, "love," the basis of English *amorous* and *enamored*, which
  sounds like a homonym of *amirah*; but Rabbi Klein calls it quite implausible
  that these chassidishe masters based their teaching on a Latin homonym, and
  offers a much sounder basis. The Targumim render these words *chativah*;
  *chativah* in biblical Hebrew is "splitting" or "chopping," which might be the
  basis of Rashi's first explanation as "separation," but in Aramaic it can also
  mean to fall in love or to woo, perhaps because two lovebirds feel like one
  person split into two. Raavan, Rabbi Eliezer ben Nosson of Mainz (1090 to
  1170), explaining the word in the piyut some recite on the second night of
  Pesach, says it is an expression of love and references these very Targumim;
  and the line in *Akdamus* about Israel making Him a *chativah* at sunrise and
  sunset is explained by Rabbi Yonah Isaac Neiman of Makow as the twice-daily
  declaration of love in Krias Shema (*Berachos* 6a). So the link between
  *amirah* and love is already in the Rishonim and even alluded to in the
  Targumim.

## Rendering guidance

- In the recurring *Vayedaber Hashem el Moshe leymor*, keep both verbs
  distinct: "Hashem spoke to Moshe, saying." "Said... to say" flattens the pair
  that the Zohar's derashah rests on.
- Trap: English "said" will absorb both words, and "spoke" will absorb *dibbur*
  and *nefesh*-level verbs like *siach*. Where a passage contrasts the two
  (Devarim 32:1; Shemos 19:3; the *maamaros*/*dibros* pairing), force the
  contrast: "speak" for *dibbur*, "say" for *amirah*, and let the surrounding
  clause carry the tone rather than smuggling "sternly" into the verb.
- Do not render *dibbur* as "commanded." Where the Torah wants a command it has
  *tzivah*. Rabbi Moshe Feinstein builds on *vayomer* in the *tzitzis* parashah
  (Bamidbar 15:37) precisely because the verb there is not the usual
  *vayedaber*; he uses the exact opposite of the reasoning Rabbi Klein draws
  from Malbim.
- *Aseres HaDibros*: "Ten Sayings" or "Decalogue," which Rabbi Klein says
  "would be better" than the popular "Ten Commandments." Any derashah on
  *dibros* against *maamaros* is lost with "Commandments."
- Where the pasuk pairs *emor* with *ve'amarta* (Vayikra 21:1), render them
  differently rather than repeating "say" twice: on Mecklenburg's reading the
  first raises the *kohanim's* status and the second is the plain telling.
