# Disgrace: bizayon / kalon / zilzul / gnai

## Bottom line

Four words for putting something down. *Kalon* treats a person as a lightweight,
worth less than he is: **dishonor, disgrace**. *Bizayon*, **contempt,
disparagement**, is stronger: where *kalon* lowers a man's value, *bizayon*
cancels it and treats him as worth nothing at all (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim).
*Zilzul* is cheapening, selling low what should be dear: **making light of,
cheapening**. *Gnai* is post-biblical, and Rambam defines it simply as the
opposite of praise: **disgrace, discredit**.

## Distinctions

### bizayon (בזיון)
Root ב-ז-ה, from the two-letter core ב-ז, which Rabbi Pappenheim defines as
holding something to be utterly worthless. He states plainly that *bizayon* is a
stronger disparagement than *kalon*: the object is treated as valueless, where *kalon* only undervalues it. The verb *vayivez* is rare, twice in Tanach, and both
times marks a man writing something off entirely: Eisav after selling the
birthright (Bereishis 25:34), which Rashi reads as his mocking and repudiating
the service of Hashem, and Haman, for whom striking at Mordechai alone was
beneath him (Esther 3:6). Malachi turns the same root on Kohanim who despise
(*bozei*) the [*avodah*](work-melachah-avodah.html#%D7%A2%D7%91%D7%95%D7%93%D7%94) with blemished offerings (Malachi 1:6-8), and Hashem
turns it back on Eisav through Ovadiah, *bazui atah me'od* (Ovadiah 1:2). Rabbi
Pappenheim groups [*bizah*](spoils-shalal-bizah-malkoach.html#%D7%91%D7%96%D7%94), plunder, under the same core: property taken without
paying for it is cheap in the taker's eyes. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche, late
Chief Rabbi of Netanya, holds that *bizayon* can be understood as a cognate of
[*bushah*](shame-bushah-klimah-cherpah.html#%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A9%D7%94) on the interchange of *zayin* and *shin*.

### kalon (קלון)
Root ק-ל-ן, from the biliteral ק-ל, which Rabbi Pappenheim defines as the
opposite of heavy: physically light (Shmuel II 2:18; Yeshayahu 19:1), lightening
a load (Bereishis 8:11; Shemos 18:22; Melachim I 12:4), and in the language of
Chazal the lenient side of a *kal vachomer*. To besmirch a man is to declare him
a lightweight, not worth the honor due him. The verbal cognates carry that
sense: Hagar making light of Sarah once she conceived (Bereishis 16:4), the
court officer who degrades a criminal by exceeding the count of lashes (Devarim
25:3), and the child who slights his parents (Devarim 27:16). Rabbi Pappenheim
draws two further words off the same core: [*kelalah*](curse-arur-kelalah-allah-kavah.html#%D7%A7%D7%9C%D7%9C%D7%94), a curse, which is at
bottom the dismissal of a man as unimportant and undeserving of the good Hashem
has for him, and *kilkul*, spoilage, a thing that has fallen so far in value
that almost nothing of it is left.

### zilzul (זלזול)
Not in Tanach as such, but the root ז-ל is. Rabbi Pappenheim gives its core as
the inability of parts to stay joined: *zulas*, "apart from," what is not
attached to the set; *nozel*, flowing liquid, whose parts will not hold together;
*zal/zolel*, unbridled monetary spending (Yeshayahu 46:6), as though a man
cannot stay attached to assets that just flow away from him. The English
"liquidate," selling things cheaper than they are worth, is similar. Since one
who sells low or spends loosely shows the goods meant little to him, the same
root yields lowered worth (Eichah 1:8), *zalzal*, the unimportant shoots pruned
off a vine so they do not harm the more valuable fruit (Yeshayahu 18:5), and in
the language of Chazal *zol*, cheap, since a product supplied in abundance drops
in price and in people's regard. *Zilzul* is accordingly disparagement by
cheapening a thing's value. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur connects *zilzul* to this
root explicitly and notes that it is sometimes used by the Targumim to render
cognates of *kalon* in Aramaic (Iyov 40:4; Mishlei 12:9).

### gnai / genus (גנאי, גנות)
Also absent from Tanach; it lives in the Targumim and in Mishnaic Hebrew. Rambam
(Commentary to the Mishnah, Shabbos 6:4 and Yoma 3:11) defines it as the
opposite of praise, and the Mishnayos he is commenting on show the range. In
*Shabbos* 6:4 it is the point of a machlokes: Rabbi Eliezer holds that carrying
a weapon is an adornment for a man, while the Sages hold that it is a *gnai*.
*Yoma* 3:11 applies the term to the House of Garmu, who refused to teach how to
bake the *lechem hapanim*, the House of Avtinas, who refused to teach how to
prepare the *ketores*, and others who likewise withheld the skills that would
have enhanced the *avodah*. *Pesachim* 10:4 teaches that in the Haggadah one
begins with *genus* and concludes with praise, that is, begins the telling from
the point at which the nation was in utter disgrace, its pre-Avrahamic pagan
origins or its slavery in Mitzrayim. The adjective is *megunah*.

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur, in *Meturgeman*, files *gnai* under a four-letter root
  ג-נ-א-י of its own.
- Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein traces it instead to an Aramaic root ג-נ-י meaning to
  be within shade, and further connects *gnai*/*genus* to the Arabic *janyya*,
  "crime." Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that perhaps the shade sense might be
  related to the English "to cast a shadow" over something. That Aramaic root
  evidently goes back to Rabbi Pappenheim's biliteral ג-ן, protection, which
  yields *gan*, a guarded and protected plot, *niggun*, a melody whose variety
  of sounds mimics the variety of flora in a garden, and *magen*, a shield.
- On the pairing of *gnai* with *gan* the Maggid of Kozhnitz (Rabbi Yisroel
  Hopstein) built a Hassidic insight, not an etymological one: a garden needs
  large amounts of manure to thrive, and the more of such "disgraceful" matter
  one adds, the better it grows; so too a man, the more he humbles himself
  before Hashem and understands his own disgracefulness, the more he can grow
  and reap the fruits of his efforts.

## Rendering guidance

- *Kalon* in a pasuk: "disgrace" or "dishonor." *Bizayon*: "contempt" where a
  person is the object, "disparagement" where a value or a mitzvah is.
- *Zilzul*: "making light of" as a verbal phrase, "cheapening" where the value of
  the thing is at issue. Avoid "disrespect," which is thin and modern.
- *Gnai*: "discredit" or "disgrace"; in Pesachim 10:4, "we begin with the
  disgrace and end with the praise."
- Trap: English "disgrace" will swallow *kalon*, *bizayon*, and *gnai* without a
  seam. Where a passage grades them, or where a *mussar* argument turns on
  *bizayon* being worse than *kalon*, spend the words: "held light" for *kalon*,
  "held worthless" for *bizayon*.
- Second trap: *zilzul* and *kalon* look like one word in Aramaic, since the
  Targum sometimes renders cognates of *kalon* with *zilzul*. Do not cite a
  Targum as proof that the two Hebrew words are identical.
- *Kelalah* under this root: a curse is a species of *kalon*, so where a source
  reads the two together, "make light of" preserves the link that "curse" hides.
