# Spying: latur / leragel (tarim, meraglim)

## Bottom line

The two verbs are not interchangeable. *Latur* is to **scout out** with an eye
for what is good; *leragel* is to **spy out** with an eye for what is weak
(Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg). Bamidbar calls Moshe's twelve *tarim*,
**scouts**, throughout. Tanach never calls them *meraglim*; that traditional
name is drawn from *leragel*, the verb Devarim uses, alongside *veyachperu*,
when it looks back on the episode.

## Distinctions

### latur (תור) and tarim (תרים)
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865): *latur* is the search for the
good, the positive attributes of what is being scouted. Rabbi Klein explains the
mission on those lines: Hashem had already promised the Land, and they were
destined to conquer it no matter what, so Moshe's errand was not for
conventional military reasons; it was meant to strengthen the nation's
conviction by having the men see the good of the Land and report it. In the end
they betrayed the mission and gathered the Land's perceived disadvantages
instead. Because they were commissioned to look for the good, Bamidbar always
describes them and their actions as *tarim*.

Rabbi Levi Yitzchok of Berditchev (*Kedushas Levi*, beginning of Shelach) reads
*tur* as Torah: the *tarim* were sent to teach Torah.

- Conventional linguists trace "tourist" to Old French *tourner*, "to turn".
- Dr. Isaac Elchanan Mozeson, an unconventional linguist, finds its origin in
  Hebrew *tor*, and Rabbi Klein finds the likeness striking, *tarim* being, so
  to speak, "tourists" who explore a land for positive information.
- David Curwin notes a conceptual link between *tor*, "scout," and *tor*,
  "dove": doves first appear in Tanach when Noach sends one from the Teivah to
  explore the land, expected to report something positive.
- Shadal (to Bamidbar 13:2): *tur* and *toar* are related to turning, since to
  draw an outline one must turn around its corners; so *vayasuru* is going about
  looking everywhere, as also in *lo sasuru*. *Meragel* is from *regel*, going
  about on foot, and is related to *rochel* and *rachil*.

### leragel (רגל) and meraglim (מרגלים)
The search for weakness and vulnerability (Mecklenburg). Devarim, which Moshe
spoke about forty years later, describes the men's actions with other words:
*veyachperu lanu es haaretz* (Devarim 1:22) and *vayeraglu osah* (Devarim 1:24).

- The first is the verb of digging, the deliberate mining of damning
  information, and Rabbi Mecklenburg points out that *chafirah*, digging, is
  related to *cherpah*, disgrace: here, digging for negative information about
  the Land.
- Rav Hirsch understands *chafirah* as digging so deep that one reaches the
  depths of the earth and can bring up the spring waters embedded there. Beyond
  that concrete sense the word also means to scout, spy or investigate: as
  digging deep retrieves the waters at the planet's nadir, spying or
  investigating retrieves information otherwise hidden from view.
- Rabbi Klein adds that perhaps *leragel* derives from *regel*, the foot, the
  body's lowest part, and so fits a search for a place's lowest features.
- Rav Hirsch (to Vayikra 19:16): the *meragel* is named for the foot because
  each step he takes counts in itself, all serving his aim of knowing the land
  and what goes on in it, as against plain *halichah*, walking, which is only a
  means of getting from one place to another.
- Shadal (to Shemos 15:11) once held that *regel*, spying, comes from *ra gilah*
  and *tur* from *asa ra'ah*, but retracted both, having found compound roots
  less common in Hebrew than he had thought.

The noun *meraglim* never describes Moshe's men in Tanach; it is drawn from the
Devarim verb. It does appear when Yosef, as viceroy, accuses his brothers of
being spies, seven times and in a decidedly negative context (Bereishis 42), and
of the two unnamed men Yehoshua sent to scout the vulnerabilities of Yericho
(Yehoshua 2:1); these and the other instances denote people sent to expose an
enemy's weaknesses.

- The popular Midrashic understanding is that Yehoshua's two men were Kalev and
  Pinchas.
- Pseudo-Philo identifies them as Kenaz and Seenamias, the sons of Kalev;
  another Midrash says they were Peretz and Zerach, Yehudah's twin sons.

## Rendering guidance

- Bamidbar 13-14: "scout out the Land," "the scouts." Reserve "spies" for where
  the Hebrew has a cognate of *ragel*.
- Devarim 1:22-24: the digging verb, then "and they spied it out." The shift in
  verbs carries Devarim's hindsight and must survive into English. At 1:22 the
  verb stands in the people's request ("that they may dig out the land for us").
- Bereishis 42 and Yehoshua 2:1: "spies" is right. In Bereishis 42 the context
  is decidedly negative; in Yehoshua 2:1 the men are sent to find the city's
  vulnerabilities.
- Trap: English "spy" absorbs both roots, and the traditional name *meraglim*
  for Moshe's men pulls a translator toward it even where the Hebrew says
  *tarim*. Where a passage quotes both the Bamidbar and the Devarim account,
  keep "scouts" for the first and "spies" for the second, and let the difference
  do the work.
