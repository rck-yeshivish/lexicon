# Witness: eid / sahad (and kadosh)

## Bottom line

*Eid* is **witness** and *eidus* **testimony**; *sahad* is the Aramaic for the
same, so render it **witness** too. They are not true synonyms, since they belong
to two languages (Yerushalmi Sotah 7:2; Shabbos 115b), which is the whole point
of Lavan naming the mound *Yegar Sahadusa* and Yaakov *Gal-Eid* (Bereishis
31:47). *Eid* also means **warn**, and that sense must not be flattened into
"testify." One who dies al kiddush Hashem is a *kadosh*, never a "witness."

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| עד | eid | witness | Hebrew; the one who knows, and who warns (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim) |
| עדות | eidus | testimony | what he gives; also the mitzvos that testify, and the Torah that warns |
| סהד | sahad | witness | Aramaic and Syriac; the one who relays (Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer) |
| קדוש | kadosh | martyr, holy one | the Jewish word for one killed for Hashem's sake, from "holy," not "witness" |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What separates *eid* from *sahad* in Iyov 16:19**, where they stand in parallel
("my witness is in Heaven, my *sahad* on high"). Malbim: *eid* attests to Iyov's
conduct with other people, *sahad* to his piety before Hashem. Rabbi Shlomo
Aharon Wertheimer: *eid* names how the witness came to know (from *vaad*, a
meeting), *sahad* how he conveys it (kin to *heid*, a thunderclap or echo,
Yechezkel 7:7; Amos 4:13). Both read a poetic doubling; the Bottom line renders
both "witness" and leaves the distinction to a note, since the Talmudim
(Yerushalmi Sotah 7:2; Shabbos 115b) treat *sahaduta* as simply the Aramaic
word.

**Where *eid* comes from.** Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, and Radak give the
biliteral ע-ד without saying how its offshoots hang together. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*, *Cheshek Shlomo*) supplies the thread: the root
means joining one thing to another, as in *ad*, "until," hence *moed* (a
meeting), *eidah* (a congregation), *adi* (an adornment joined to what it
beautifies); testimony joins a deed to the one who did it. Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*) derives *eid* from *adi* directly: testimony stands
out from ordinary speech as ornament stands out. Rabbi Avraham
Kobernick (*Hipuch Osiyos*) reads ע-ד as ד-ע, "knowledge," reversed, making the
*eid* the one in the know. Shadal (to Bereishis 31:50) goes outside Hebrew, to a
Syriac root ע-ה-ד, remember, mention, warn, making warning the primary role.
Follow Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, who covers the warning and monument senses at
once.

**Whether *sahad* is related to *sa'ad*.** Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 20:3) and
Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*) say yes, *hey* and *ayin*
interchanging: a witness supports one side of the case. Neither changes the
English.

## Distinctions

### eid (עד) and eidus (עדות)
Beyond the courtroom, *eid* is used of a monument that keeps a message before
people, which is why the mound Yaakov and Lavan raised is *Gal-Eid*. A class of
mitzvos is called *eidos* because they testify to what befell Klal Yisrael
(Devarim 4:45, 6:17; Tehillim 93:5). The second sense is warning: because a
witness's job includes warning the would-be sinner, *eid* took on "warn" outside
any court setting (Bereishis 43:3; Shemos 19:21, 21:29). From that, the Torah
itself is *eidus* (Shemos 31:18; Bamidbar 18:2), most of its injunctions being
warnings, and an ox that has gored three times is a *muad* (Bava Kama 1:4, 2:4)
because its owner has been warned.

### sahad (סהד)
Twice in Tanach: *Yegar Sahadusa*, Lavan's Aramaic name for the mound (Bereishis
31:47), and Iyov's *sahadi* in the heights (Iyov 16:19). It does not appear in
the Mishnah, but *sahadei*, spelled with a *samech*, is the ordinary Talmudic
Aramaic for witnesses, as in *anan sahadi*, "we ourselves testify." The initial
letter is unstable: a *sin* in both pesukim, a *samech* in the Gemara.

### kadosh (קדוש)
The Syriac cognate of *sahad*, and the Arabic *shahid* borrowed from it, both
mean "witness" and both came to mean martyr, as the Greek-derived English
*martyr* does. Judaism does not name its martyrs that way. One killed al kiddush
Hashem is a *kadosh*, from *kedushah*, whose core is separation: Hashem is
*Kadosh* because He is wholly other. A Jew who gives his life declares that even
life stands below Hashem's will, which is a declaration of Hashem's *kedushah*,
not a proof of the Torah's truth, which needs no such proof.

## Rendering guidance

- Both עד and סהד render "witness"; עדות is "testimony." Do not invent an English
  contrast between them, since the difference is which language is being spoken.
- Bereishis 31:47 is where the contrast is the whole point: transliterate both
  names, *Yegar Sahadusa* and *Gal-Eid*, and gloss ("both mean 'mound of
  witness,' the one Aramaic, the other Hebrew").
- Trap: English "witness" will absorb the warning sense of עד as well. Shemos
  21:29 (*v'hu'ad bivalav*) and Bereishis 43:3 (*ha'ed he'id banu*) are "warned,"
  not "testified"; a translation that keeps "testify" there loses the halachah.
- עדות of the Aron is "the Testimony" by convention (*Aron ha'Eidus*); the
  mitzvah class is "*eidos*" or "testimonial mitzvos."
- Never render *kadosh* in a martyrdom context as "martyr" without keeping the
  Hebrew or the sense "holy one"; the whole distinction from *shahid* rides on it.
