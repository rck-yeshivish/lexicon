# Witness: eid / sahad (and kadosh)

## Bottom line

*Eid* is **witness** and *eidus* **testimony**; *sahad* is the Aramaic for the
same, **witness**. The column opens on the mound Lavan named *Yegar Sahadusa*
and Yaakov *Gal-Eid* (Bereishis 31:47), and holds that, strictly speaking, the
two are not quite synonyms: their meanings line up, but they come from two
languages, as both Talmudim recognise in calling *sahaduta* Aramaic (Yerushalmi
Sotah 7:2, Sanhedrin 10:1; Shabbos 115b; Megillah 9a). Malbim and Rabbi Shlomo
Aharon Wertheimer nonetheless posit a difference: for Malbim, *eid* attests to
conduct toward people and *sahad* to piety before Hashem; for Rabbi Wertheimer,
*eid* names how the witness knows and *sahad* how he relays it. *Eid* also means
**warn** (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim; Shadal makes warning its primary role). One
who dies al kiddush Hashem is called a *kadosh*, "holy," where the Syriac,
Arabic and English words for a martyr all mean "witness"; the column reads that
difference as a statement about how Judaism views martyrdom.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What separates eid from sahad.** In Iyov 16:19 they stand in parallel ("my
witness is in Heaven, my *sahad* on high"), Iyov poetically using *sahad* as the
counterpart of *eid*. Malbim (there) posits a difference: *eid* attests to
Iyov's upright conduct with other people, *sahad* to his piety before Hashem.
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935), generally: *eid* relates to
*vaad*, a meeting, the witness being one who happened to be present and so
"met" the information, which names how he knows; *sahad* names his role in
relaying what he saw, and he surmises it is related to *heid*, a thunderclap or
echo (Yechezkel 7:7; Amos 4:13), a sound others hear. The column's own point,
made before these, is that the meanings of the two words line up, and that they
are not strictly synonyms only because they come from two languages.

**Where eid comes from.** Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970), Ibn Janach (990 to
1050) and Radak (1160 to 1235) give the biliteral ע-ד, but none offers a way to
connect its many offshoots. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814; *Yerios
Shlomo*, *Cheshek Shlomo*) makes the core "connecting one thing to another," as
in *ad*, "until," in time, place or condition; hence *moed*, a meeting;
*eidah*, a congregation; *adi*, an adornment joined to what it beautifies; and
*eden*, enjoyment, which joins the one who enjoys to the thing enjoyed.
Testimony connects an event to the one responsible for it. Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Parchon (twelfth century; *Machberes HeAruch*) derives *eid* from *adi*: as
ornament marks a thing as special, testimony stands out from other speech in
relaying important information. Rabbi Avraham Kobernick (*Hipuch Osiyos*)
reads ע-ד as ד-ע, "knowledge," reversed, making the *eid* the one in the know,
much as Rabbi Wertheimer's *vaad* does. Rabbi Wertheimer's explanation echoes
Shadal (Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto, 1800 to 1865; to Bereishis 31:50 and
Devarim 32:21, and *Bikkurei Ittim* 1829, pp. 194-196), who derives *eid* from a
triliteral ע-ה-ד attested in Syriac but not in Tanach, meaning to remember,
mention, warn, which he takes as the *eid*'s primary role, warning a sinner off a
misdeed. Going a step further, he makes the core of that root the two letters
ה-ד, to which Hebrew added an initial ayin and Aramaic an initial sin or samech,
so that *eid* and *sahad* are etymologically related.

**Whether sahad is related to sa'ad.** Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 20:3) says yes:
*sahad*, with sin or samech, is related to ס-ע-ד, "support," by the interchange
of hey and ayin, since a witness's testimony supports one side of the case.
Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras, an eighteenth-century grammarian and dayan,
connects them similarly in *Ohalei Yehuda*, and also suggests *sahad* is a
blend of *zeh*, "this" (sin or samech for zayin), and *eid*, which like Shadal
relates the two words. In the edition of *Machberes Menachem* published by
Herschell Filipowsky (1816 to 1872), Menachem defines *eid* as *sa'ad*, which
fits Rav Hirsch; in the unpublished edition prepared by Professor Ángel
Sáenz-Badillo (1940 to 2013), he defines it as *sahad*, which, the column notes,
is less novel.

## Distinctions

### eid (עד) and eidus (עדות)
Rabbi Pappenheim extends the idea from human witnesses to physical monuments
and objects that keep a message before people, to which they "testify." A class
of mitzvos is called *eidos* because they testify to events that befell Klal
Yisrael (Devarim 4:45, 6:17; Tehillim 93:5, 119:2, 119:31). The second sense is
warning: since one of a witness's main roles in court is to warn the would-be
sinner of the consequences, *eid* took on "warn" outside any court setting
(Bereishis 43:3; Shemos 19:21, 21:29). So the Torah itself is *eidus* (Shemos
26:33, 31:18; Bamidbar 18:2), most of its injunctions being prohibitions that
warn, and an ox that has gored three times is a *muad* (Mishnah Bava Kamma 1:4,
2:4, 2:6, 3:8, 4:2, 4:5, 4:9) because its owner has already been warned that it
is dangerous (Shemos 21:29).

### sahad (סהד)
The root seems to be ש-ה-ד. Twice in Tanach: *Yegar Sahadusa*, Lavan's name for
the mound (Bereishis 31:47), and Iyov's *sahadi* on high (Iyov 16:19). It does
not appear in the Mishnah, but *sahadei*, spelled with a samech, is the ordinary
Talmudic Aramaic for witnesses, as in *anan sahadi*, "we testify." A *kesubah*
from Damascus found in the Cairo Genizah, dated 933, uses *eid* and both
spellings of *sahad*, with sin and with samech, interchangeably in the one
document. Rabbi Yosef Kerman clarifies that the Aramaic word really begins with
a sin, as in the two pesukim, though its cognates are often written with a
samech, as in the Talmud.

### kadosh (קדוש)
A cognate of *sahad* is the Syriac Christian word for a martyr, which Islam
took over as the Arabic *shahid*; both literally mean "witness," as does the
Ancient Greek *martur* behind English *martyr*. (Arabic usually turns a Hebrew
shin into its samech, but here it turns the sin of *sahad* into a shin.) The
Jewish term for one who dies for his faith is *kadosh*, plural *kedoshim*,
"holy." Rabbi Klein reads the difference as telling. Christianity and Islam, he
writes, are far more concerned with proving the truth of their beliefs, and so
see a co-religionist's death as testimony to that truth; Judaism does not accept
that willingness to die proves anything, since many people die for false ideas,
and holds its truths to be self-evident, needing no such proof. Its focus is on
what the death shows: that for this person Hashem and His word rise above
everything, even his own life. The core of *kedushah* is separation, and Hashem
is the ultimate *Kadosh* because He is different in kind from everything else;
one killed *al kiddush Hashem* makes the ultimate declaration, but not proof, of
Hashem's *kedushah*, and so is himself called *kadosh*. The column adds that
terminology alone cannot capture these traditions fully, but calls the
difference an important heuristic.

## Rendering guidance

- Both עד and סהד render "witness"; עדות is "testimony." Unless a commentary is
  drawing Malbim's or Rabbi Wertheimer's distinction, do not create an English
  contrast between them.
- Bereishis 31:47 is where the two languages meet: transliterate both names,
  *Yegar Sahadusa* and *Gal-Eid*, and gloss ("both mean 'mound of witness,' the
  one Aramaic, the other Hebrew").
- Trap: English "witness" will absorb the warning sense of עד as well. Shemos
  21:29 (*v'hu'ad bivalav*) and Bereishis 43:3 (*ha'ed he'id banu*) are
  "warned," not "testified"; "testify" at Shemos 21:29 loses the halachah of the
  *muad*.
- עדות of the Aron is "the Testimony" by convention (*Aron ha'Eidus*); the
  mitzvah class is "*eidos*" or "testimonial mitzvos."
- Never render *kadosh* in a martyrdom context as "martyr" without keeping the
  Hebrew or the sense "holy one"; the whole distinction from *shahid* rides on it.
