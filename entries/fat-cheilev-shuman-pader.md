# Fat: cheilev / shuman / pader / tarba

## Bottom line

*Cheilev* is **fat**, and in halachic contexts specifically the **forbidden
fat**: the hard visceral globs offered on the mizbeach and barred from eating.
*Shuman* is post-biblical and names the **permitted fat**, the soft fat attached
to the meat. *Pader* is contested: most of the grammarians read it as simply
**fat**, a synonym of *cheilev*; Ramban narrows it to the **thin fatty
membrane** dividing the innards; and a group of grammarians drops the fat sense
altogether for the **organ cluster** of trachea, lungs, heart, and liver.
*Tarba* is the Aramaic standing in for *cheilev*. *Cheilev* is also sometimes
borrowed for **the choicest part** of a non-animal thing, where it does not
refer to animal fat at all.

## Distinctions

### cheilev (חלב)
About ninety occurrences in Tanach, the largest share of them in the opening chapters of
Vayikra. It is fat in the plain sense, of animals and of people, as when Ehud
ben Gera's blade disappeared into Eglon's belly (Shoftim 3:22; see also Tehillim
73:7). Rashi (Bereishis 45:18; Tehillim 147:14): the word is sometimes borrowed for
whatever is choicest and richest, even where no animal fat is involved. So
Pharaoh's promise that Yosef's brothers would eat the *cheilev* of the land
(Bereishis 45:18), and the Levi'im separating *terumas maaser* from the
*cheilev* of the produce (Bamidbar 18:29-32). One of David's warriors was named
Cheilev (Shmuel II 23:29), though elsewhere his name is given as Cheiled (Divrei
HaYamim I 11:30) or Cheldai (Divrei HaYamim I 27:15).

*Cheilev* also covers parts of a korban that are not technically fat but share its
law. The tail of a sheep holds no visceral fat, yet the Torah speaks of its fat
(Vayikra 3:9) because it goes on the mizbeach with the *cheilev* proper (Ramban
and Rabbeinu Bachya there; Rambam, *Hilchos Maachalos Assuros* 7:5). Likewise
*chelvei hatamid* in the version of *Shekalim* 8:8 cited at Sukkah 54b: since
the *tamid* is an olah, every limb is burned, and Rashi explains that they are
all called *cheilev* because they share *cheilev*'s law, burned on the mizbeach
and forbidden to eat (see Tosafos there and the standard text of the Mishnah).

The fats are called *eimurim* because they are the parts of the animal *amurim*,
said in the Torah, to be burnt on the Mizbeach (Tosafos Yom Tov, after the
Rambam's introduction to *Seder Kodashim*: "these are called *eimurim*, meaning
the things He commanded to burn").

*Sefer Aruch* (s.v. מר): the fats are called *eimurim* because they are lords,
*morim*, over all the limbs, and they go up on the Mizbeach as the portion of
the Lord of the world.

The root ches-lamed-beis also gives *chalav*, milk, which appears close to fifty
times in Tanach by Even Shoshan's concordance.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*): *cheilev* is from *chalav* because
  it accrues in hard globs that are typically white, like milk.
- Rabbi Klein adds an alternative of his own: perhaps *cheilev* relates to
  *chalav* because unskimmed milk itself contains fatty acids, milkfats.
- Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 45:18) joins *cheilev*, *chalav*, and *chalaf*, removed
  from its place or reused for a different purpose: milk is the nourishment
  separated out of the mother's body to sustain another, and *cheilev* is the
  energy the body stores aside for future use rather than present need.
- Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bamidbar 18:17): Assyrian
  *tahlubti*, a covering or garment, is a cognate of *cheilev*, the fatty
  membrane that covers certain organs.

### shuman (שומן)
Not in Tanach at all; it enters with the Mishnah (*Kerisus* 4:1).

- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer ties it to the biblical root shin-mem-nun,
  oily, familiar from *shemen*.
- Rashi (Chullin 45b): *cheilev* is the general Hebrew word covering both
  forbidden and permitted fats, and *shuman* was coined by Chazal for the
  permitted fats specifically, so that the two could be told apart. Mammals do
  in fact carry two kinds of fat: visceral fats, usually large independent
  pieces of blubber, denser and harder from fascia compression, and
  intermuscular fats, attached to the meat and softer.
- Ramban and Rabbeinu Bachya (Vayikra 3:9) expand on this: *cheilev* is the
  visceral fat, the globs not attached to muscle, which the Torah forbids, while
  *shuman* is the intermuscular fat, the strips one meets in a kosher cut, which
  is permitted.
- Pappenheim: *shuman* is from *shemen* because it is the more pourable,
  oil-like fat.

### tarba (תרבא)
Targumic Aramaic for *cheilev* (Onkelos to Shemos 23:18, 29:13; Vayikra 3:3,
7:24, 8:26), with the exception of Hevel's korban, where Onkelos renders it with
a form of *shuman* / *shemen* (Bereishis 4:4; so too Targum to Tehillim 73:7).

- Rashi often defines the Gemara's *tarba* as *cheilev* (Shabbos 133b, 136b;
  Avodah Zarah 28b; Chullin 93a; Bechoros 30a), though at Bava Metzia 83b he
  defines it as *shuman*.
- Chizkuni (Vayikra 3:3) and *Tirgem Avraham*: *tarba* is from the Aramaic
  *rav*, big or growing, since the more a person or animal eats the more fat
  accrues. That Aramaic term is in turn probably related to the Hebrew
  reish-beis-(hei) of multiplying.

### pader (פדר)
A word that seemingly means "fat," vocalized *pader* or perhaps *peder*. Three
occurrences, all in Vayikra and all in korbanos lists beside limbs such as the
head (Vayikra 1:8, 1:12, 8:20). Onkelos always renders it *tarba*, which
points to identity with *cheilev*, and Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*), Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*), Rashi (Tamid 31a), Ibn Ezra and Rashbam
(Vayikra 1:8) all simply define *pader* as *cheilev*. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino
(*Ohel Moed*) makes the argument explicit from Onkelos rendering both words the
same way. Chullin 27b explains the pairing with the head: since the place of
slaughter would ooze blood unbecomingly on the mizbeach, it was covered with the
*pader*; the Mainz commentary attributed to Rabbeinu Gershom takes this as the
internal fats, again equating *pader* with *cheilev*.

- Against this, Ramban (Vayikra 1:8): *pader* is not a general word for fat but
  one specific fat, the thin membrane separating the internal organs from one
  another, and the word comes by metathesis from peh-reish-dalet, to separate.
- Rav Hirsch (Vayikra 1:8; Shemos 13:2) builds a family on peh-dalet-reish with
  peh-tes-reish (*peter*, opening) and peh-tav-reish (*pisaron*, the
  interpretation of a dream), the three linked by the interchangeability of tav,
  tes and dalet, and all of them revealing, removing, or separating: *peter
  rechem* (Shemos 13:2, 13:12, 13:13, 13:15, 34:19; Bamidbar 3:12, 18:15;
  Yechezkel 20:26) is the firstborn opening the womb and so revealing the
  mother's potential motherhood actualized; an interpreter separates a dream's
  true meaning from the rest; and *pader* is fat removed from the body for the
  korban, lying over the entrails like a headdress. He also suggests linking
  peh-dalet-reish to beis-tav-reish, cutting or disconnecting, peh and beis
  being interchangeable as dalet and tav are.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche takes the core as peh-dalet, the final reish a
  radical, equivalent to beis-dalet, alone or apart, peh and beis being
  interchangeable.
- Rabbi Shaul Goldman speculates that peh-dalet-reish may actually be related to
  peh-zayin-reish, to spread, diffuse or disperse, dalet and zayin often being
  taken as interchangeable in the Semitic languages and especially in the shift
  from Hebrew to Aramaic; and he notes that Onkelos does not in fact render
  *pader* by *tarba* word for word but by *prisusa d'tarba*, a spread of fat,
  which would mean that *pader* implies fat that spreads over and covers, not a
  generic word for fat perfectly synonymous with *cheilev*.

Other grammarians drop the fat sense entirely: Rabbi Saadia Gaon (Vayikra 1:8), Ibn
Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*), and Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur (*Nimukim* to Radak's *Sefer HaShorashim*) take *pader* as the
attached structure of trachea, lungs, heart, and liver.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "fat" will absorb *cheilev*, *shuman*, and *pader*. In a korbanos or
  kashrus passage, render *cheilev* "the forbidden fat" (or leave it
  transliterated and gloss it once), *shuman* "permitted fat," and *pader* by
  the reading you are following, "fatty membrane" or "suet."
- *Cheilev* in a non-animal context is "the choicest" or "the richest," never
  "the fat": *v'achaltem es cheilev ha'aretz* is "you shall eat the fat of the
  land" only as an English idiom, and where the idiom does not carry, write "the
  best of the land."
- Where a sugya sets *cheilev* against *shuman*, the contrast is the whole
  point, so do not translate both "fat" and rely on the reader.
- *Chelvei hatamid* is not "the fats of the tamid" in the literal sense; on
  Rashi's reading it means the portions burned on the mizbeach. Translate the
  law, not the anatomy, and note the variant texts.
- *Pader* is contested. Most of the grammarians make it a plain synonym of
  *cheilev*, so if the passage does not depend on it, "fat" or "suet" follows
  the majority; if it does depend on it, name the view (Ramban's membrane, Rabbi
  Goldman's covering spread of fat, or Rabbi Saadia Gaon's organ cluster) rather
  than smoothing the machlokes away.
