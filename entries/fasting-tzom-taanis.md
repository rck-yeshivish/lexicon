# Fasting: tzom / taanis (inui)

## Bottom line

*Tzom* is the Biblical word for a **fast**, usually abstaining from food; *taanis* is
the Mishnaic word for a **fast day**, built on *inui*, **affliction**, and so
wider than food alone. The words are not free variants, and two approaches to
what separates them are given below; on the first, *Tzom Gedaliah* and
*Taanis Esther* carry their words for a reason. *Inui* is affliction, of which fasting is one form.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| צום | tzom | fast | Biblical; from "gathering" per Radak in his father's name, which a derashah reads as a fast the whole nation joins |
| תענית | taanis | fast day | from inui, affliction; the Mishnaic term, and already in Ezra 9:5 |
| עינוי | inui | affliction | the Torah's word for Yom Kippur; abstaining from food is only part of it |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What separates tzom from taanis.**

1. **Level of obligation.** Rabbi Akiva Shlomo Deutsch-Dayan of Geneva first
   raises and then rejects the simple answer, that *tzom* is Biblical Hebrew
   and *taanis* Rabbinic, because *taanis* itself appears at Ezra 9:5 (he
   grants that Ezra sometimes writes closer to Rabbinic Hebrew, and still
   rejects it). Instead: *tzom* is a fast required by law, *taanis* one
   declared *ad hoc* or kept as a custom without being strictly obligatory.
   Hence *Tzom Gedaliah*, which Yirmiyahu prescribed by prophetic fiat, against
   *Taanis Esther*, which was instituted in post-Talmudic times and of which the
   Rema writes that it is not obligatory and one may be lenient when necessary
   (*Orach Chaim* 686:2). The fasts declared in the Purim story itself are
   called *tzomos* because at that time they were obligatory.
2. **Mode of fasting.** Rabbi Avraham Etiel Gurwitz, from Rambam's own usage:
   *tzom* is the undertaking to refrain from food and drink, which is why
   Rambam uses it in *Hilchos Nedarim* for a personal fast; *taanis* is the
   acceptance of a day as halachically distinct, whose distinction rules out
   eating and drinking, which is why Rambam uses it in *Hilchos Taaniyos*.

The two approaches stand side by side; Rabbi Gurwitz "takes a different
approach," and nothing decides between them.

**Where tzom comes from.** *Tzom* and its variations appear close to fifty times
in Tanach and usually refer to abstaining from eating. It is probably related to
*tzamei*, thirsty, since one who fasts generally ends up thirsty. Nonetheless,
Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*, and to Melachim I 21:9)
reports in his father's name that *tzom* literally means gathering, and cites
in support the Mishnah's *tzomes hagidim* (*Chullin* 4:7), the point in an
animal's leg where the sinews converge; Modern Hebrew's *tzomes*, a junction,
preserves the sense. The gathering reading is the one that carries a
*derashah*, below.

## Distinctions

### tzom (צום)
Zecharyah 8:19, foretelling a time when the four fast days will be turned into
holidays, days of joy and gladness, names them by their months: the fast of the
fourth (17
Tammuz, when the walls were breached), the fifth (9 Av, the destruction and the
exile), the seventh (3 Tishrei, when Gedaliah ben Achikam was assassinated),
and the tenth (10 Teves, when the siege began). All four mark stages of the
churban of the first Beis HaMikdash.

### taanis (תענית)
Built on *inui*, so it names the affliction rather than the abstention. The
Torah uses verb forms of *inui* for what is required on Yom Kippur (Vayikra
16:29 to 31, 23:27 to 32; Bamidbar 29:7), and refraining from food and drink is
one of the things included in it. Strictly, then, *taanis* means any self-affliction, fasting included. *Taanis Esther*, kept the day before Purim, and
*Taanis Bechoros*, kept by many firstborn before Pesach, both carry this word.

### the gathering reading in Yeshayahu
Rabbi Yechiel Heilpern cites Rabbi Moshe Di'Segovia Benveniste on the Yom
Kippur haftarah. When the people protest "Why have we fasted (*tzamnu*) and You
did not see, afflicted our souls (*ininu*) and You do not know" (Yeshayahu
58:3), their choice of *tzom*, on Radak's sense of gathering, is itself the
claim: we fasted as one. The Gemara says that a fast which does not take in
everyone, the sinners of Yisrael included, is no fast (Kerisus 6b). Yeshayahu's
answer is that the unity was a front; they made a show of loving one another so
that favors would come back to them. And their *inui* was no better: they
claimed to be dulling the body to sharpen the soul, while in truth they declared fast
days for their own convenience, needing time free of their regular schedules to
harass those who owed them money, "on the day of your fast you pursue your
desires, and press all those who are indebted to you." The fast Hashem does want
is the one that "breaks open the shackles of wickedness, unties the bonds of
injustice, sends free the oppressed, and cuts off all iniquities" (58:3 to 6).

## Rendering guidance

- *Tzom Gedaliah* and *Taanis Esther* keep their Hebrew names; do not
  standardize both to "the Fast of". If they must be Englished, "the Fast of
  Gedaliah" and "the Fast of Esther", with a note that the Hebrew words differ.
- Trap: English "fast" absorbs *tzom*, *taanis*, and *inui* alike. Reserve
  "fast" for *tzom*, "fast day" for *taanis*, and "affliction" for *inui*. In
  Yeshayahu 58:3 both appear in one pasuk and the parallelism depends on the
  difference.
- On Yom Kippur, the Torah never says *tzom*; it says *v'inisem es
  nafshoseichem*, "you shall afflict your souls". Writing "you shall fast"
  narrows a *mitzvah* that includes more than food.
