# First person: anochi / ani

## Bottom line

Both mean **I**. *Ani* is the plain first person; *anochi* is the emphatic, **I
myself**, and it is the weightier and rarer word (*ani* outnumbers it more than
two to one in Tanach). Where the two are contrasted, the workable rule
for rendering is Malbim's and Rabbi Pappenheim's: with *anochi* the news is in
the subject (I, and no one else, did it), with *ani* the news is in the
predicate (what I did, as against something else I might have done). But that is
not the only reading in the field: Rav Hirsch takes *anochi*'s force to be
nearness rather than exclusion, and Rabbi Edel takes the pair to name body and
soul. A translator who renders both "I"
loses every derashah built on the difference, beginning with *Anochi Hashem
Elokecha*.

## Distinctions

### ani (אני)
The ordinary first-person pronoun, and the one that survived into everyday
speech; *anochi* by contrast sounds archaic today. Maharal: *ani* is simply
first person, without stress. Malbim and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *ani* is used
where the fresh information sits in the predicate, so *ani omed* means "I am
standing," that is, standing and not sitting. Rav Hirsch: *ani* marks the
speaker off as distinct from the one he is addressing. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel:
when a person says *ani* he speaks for his soul. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
shows from a range of sources that although *ani* is normally a common pronoun,
when Hashem uses it, it can function as a Name of His.

### anochi (אנכי)
The emphatic form. Maharal: reflexive first person, one who is stressing
himself, "I myself." Malbim and Rabbi Pappenheim: the novelty is in the
subject, so *anochi omed* means "I, and not another, am standing." Applied to
the opening of the Aseres HaDibros, this is why the Dibrah begins *Anochi*
(Shemos 20:2): He and no other is the God who took us out of Mitzrayim.

Rav Hirsch reads the emphasis differently, and warmly: *anochi* carries the
speaker's closeness to the one he addresses, in his standing as benefactor. On
that reading *Anochi* opens the Dibros to establish Hashem's nearness to Klal
Yisrael as the One who is their God and who brought them out. This somewhat
echoes the Midrashic tradition that ties *anochi* in the Dibros to the Egyptian
word *anoch*, meaning love and endearment.

Rabbeinu Bachya says *anochi* carries deeper, kabbalistic weight than *ani*,
without explaining further. One might read that as *anochi* for the soul and
*ani* for the body, but the evidence points the other way: Ibn Ezra relates
*anochi* to *anach*, a plumb line (Amos 7:7-8), and Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel
builds on this that *anochi* is the bob at the line's end, which anchors the
straightedge, as the body anchors the soul in this world and holds it inside
physical bounds. So for Rabbi Edel the two words name two elements of a
person's existence, *ani* speaking for the soul and *anochi* for the body.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *ani* is "I," *anochi* is "I myself" or "It is I who." Reserve the
  heavier English for places where a source is actually reading the emphasis.
- Trap: English has one word for both. Where a *ma'amar Chazal* or a mussar
  passage turns on *Anochi* versus *Ani*, transliterate and gloss rather than
  translating twice as "I" and hoping the reader hears it.
- *Anochi Hashem Elokecha* (Shemos 20:2): keep the Hebrew, and where an English
  rendering is needed pick the one your source demands. On Malbim and Rabbi
  Pappenheim: "I, and none other, am Hashem your God." On Rav Hirsch: the
  emphasis is not exclusion but nearness, so "I am Hashem your God" with the
  intimacy carried in a note.
- Do not render *anochi* as "myself" standing alone; it is a subject pronoun,
  not a reflexive object.
- *Anach* in Amos is "plumb line," never "I"; the link belongs to a note.
