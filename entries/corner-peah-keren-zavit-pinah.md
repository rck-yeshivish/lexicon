# Corner: peah / keren / zavit / pinah / miktzoa / katzeh

## Bottom line

*Peah* is the broad **corner** or **side** of an area, and in most pesukim
simply a **direction** (Onkelos: *ruach*). *Keren* is a **horn**, and a corner
only where something juts out, chiefly the corners of the Altar. On Rabbi
Pappenheim's distinction, as the column reads him, *zavit* is the
inside of a corner and *pinah* is the outside of it, and by extension a
**prominence** or **cornerstone**, so *pinah* often wants "cornerstone" or
"leading man," not "corner." *Miktzoa* is the sharp **angle** where two sides
meet at a point; *katzeh* is the **edge/end**, a right-angled corner. The trap:
Onkelos renders *peah*, *pinah*, *miktzoa*, *katzeh* and *paam* all as *zavit*,
which flattens the Hebrew; a translation into English must not follow him and
print "corner" five times.

## Distinctions

### peah (פאה)
The commonest biblical word for corner, roughly eighty-five occurrences, but in
most of them it names a cardinal direction (Shemos 27:9; Bamidbar 35:5), usually
alongside a word for north, south, east or west, where Onkelos gives
*rucha*/*ruach*; only in the four instances where it means "corner" proper (the
*peah* of the head and of the beard, Vayikra 19:27, and the *peah* of the field,
Vayikra 19:9, 23:22) does he leave it untranslated. For the four rings on the
four *peah* of the Shulchan (Shemos 25:26, 37:13) Onkelos renders *zavit*, the
word that will matter later. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) gives two etymologies: in *Yerios Shlomo* from
PEH-HEY, "mouth," a corner being where one enters and leaves an area as the
mouth is where food enters and words leave; in *Cheshek Shlomo* from PEH-ALEPH,
"wide corner." The Moabite noblemen called *paas* (Bamidbar 24:17; Yirmiyahu
48:45) are those set apart at the corner of their society and the cornerstones
on which it rests. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865): *peah* need not
be in a corner at all, only cut off and set apart, which is why the *peah* of a
field may be left in the middle (Mishnah *Peah* 3:1).

### keren (קרן)
Primarily an animal's **horn**, and in Tanach the horn-like protrusions at the
four corners of the Altar (Shemos 38:2; Vayikra 8:15; Yechezkel 43:20); the
Mishnah widens it to any corner while keeping the biblical altar sense
(*Kilayim* 6:7, *Yoma* 5:5, *Zevachim* 5:3, 6:2, 6:5, 11:3, *Tamid* 2:5, 4:1,
7:3, *Middos* 3:2-3, 4:3, 4:5, *Keilim* 17:9-10). Malbim: the core sense is the
horn that bulges from the head, borrowed for anything projecting outward, so a
ray of sunlight is a *keren* and a corner is a *keren* because it juts from an
otherwise smooth surface. Rabbi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos
27:2, Vayikra 4:7) makes a similar point, and Radak even insinuates a connection
between the Hebrew *keren* and the Latin *cornu*, from which the English
"corner" derives by way of French. Pappenheim instead traces it to KUF-REISH, "strong impact,"
which yields *korah*, *tikrah* and *kir*, weight-bearing points of impact; a
corner is where two sides collide.

### zavit (זוית)
Twice in Tanach: the beauty of Jewish daughters likened to the hewn corners of a
palace (Tehillim 144:12), and Yisrael drinking "like the corners of the Altar"
(Zechariah 9:15). What that image means is disputed. Rashi: their victories will
leave them overflowing with the good things of the spoil, as the corner of the
Altar overflows with wine. Alternatively, that in defeating their enemies they
will figuratively drink their blood, spilled as abundantly as the wine that
overflows the Altar's corners (Radak and Mahari Kara there). Onkelos uses
*zavit* as his standard rendering for every other corner word. Pappenheim links
*zavit* to *ziv* (radiance), and offers two ways of making the connection. The
first, as Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation explains him,
seems to be that turning a corner is a change of direction, and a shiny thing
likewise makes one change direction by drawing the eye to it. In a separate
discussion he seems to give a slightly different account: light bouncing off a
shiny object toward the eye travels at an angle, and so produces a corner. He
also claims that Iyar was originally called Ziv (Melachim I 6:1) because in that
month the sun somehow turns a corner, which the column suggests, with a question
mark, may mean its ushering in the new season of spring.

The last of Haman's ten sons was named Vayzasa (Esther 9:9), an anagram of
*zavit*, and Rabbi Benzion Chaim Lubetzky (d. 1945) argues that in so naming him
Haman meant to show off his own importance, as though his sons were the
cornerstones of the Persian Empire.

The Gemara's set phrase for a corner is *keren zavit* (*Berachos* 53b, where one
may use a torch's light without seeing the fire if the two of them stand on
opposite sides of a *keren zavit*; *Kiddushin* 66a, where Torah was so neglected
that it was relegated to a mere *keren zavit* for whoever wished to take it). Dr.
Avi Hurvitz, a veteran linguist and expert philologist, notes that the phrase is
a semantic redundancy, since *keren* already means corner and so does *zavit*.

### pinah (פינה)
Twice in the Chumash, both for the corners of the Altar (Shemos 27:2, 38:2),
where Onkelos gives *zavit*, and twenty-eight more times in Tanach. If the
column has understood him correctly, Pappenheim distinguishes the two thus:
*zavit* is the inner face of a corner, *pinah* the outer. Stand inside the room
and it is a *zavit*; stand in the street and the same corner is a *pinah*. Hence
a *pinah* must be built stronger than the rest, being exposed, and hence leaders
are *pinos* (Shoftim 20:2; Shmuel I 14:38), men required to be of better quality
than the rest. He derives it from PEH-NUN, "front/surface," the root of *panim*
and of *panah*, to turn and face: at a *pinah* one must turn to keep following
the perimeter; *peninim* (precious stones) he reads the same way, a gem cut so
its faces and their corners show. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (1920 to 2016),
late Chief Rabbi of Netanya, traces both *peah* and *pinah* to the single letter
PEH, "end/edge" (with an added alef in *peah* and an added nun in *pinah*),
*peah* naming more broadly the edge of a given area and *pinah* the edge where
two of its sides meet; and the
Rashba (responsa vol. 4 §278) presumes *pinah* and *zavit* are plain synonyms.
Mordechai Zer-Kavod (*Daas Mikra* to Nechemiah 3:24) makes the primary sense
"raised place," from *rosh pinah* (Tehillim 118:22) and *pinos u'gevohos*
(Tzefaniah 1:16) and *migdalim hapinos* (Divrei HaYamim II 26:15); the *even
pinah* stands against the *even mosad* in Yirmiyahu 51:26, foundation stone
underground and corner stone all the way on top, so that a *pinah* is something
prominently placed.

### miktzoa (מקצוע)
Root ק-צ-ע, which yields a verb (cutting, removing, peeling) and three nouns:
*maktzua*, the tool for doing it (Yeshayahu 44:13; *Shabbos* 123b), *miktzoa*
itself, and *ketziah*, a spice or incense. The column's guess at what links the
first three is that in cutting or scraping one holds the blade perpendicular to
the surface, forming an angle; and perhaps *ketziah* too comes from the verb,
its plant having had to be cut. *Miktzoa* as corner appears in the Mishnah as
well (*Tamid* 1:4, 3:3, *Middos* 2:5), and Onkelos renders it, like the rest,
*zavit* (Shemos 26:23; see also Rashi to Nechemiah 3:19). Pappenheim: where
*pinah* covers any angle, right, acute or obtuse, *miktzoa* is specifically the
acute angle (he concedes it can sometimes be a right angle), a single sharp point
where two sides converge rather than an area spread out; hence he reads the
Mishnah's praise of monetary law as a *miktzoa baTorah* (*Bava Basra* 10:8) as
the discipline that sharpens the mind and opens it to higher intellectuality,
and he suggests too that the *ketziah* spice is so called because its plant has
many sharp angles, thorns perhaps. Rabbi Yishmael ben Chachamon, who lived in
Egypt in Rambam's time, notes in his commentary (*Bava Basra* 175b) that the
Mishnah's *miktzoa* means "corner" just as it does in Tanach, which as the
column observes does not much clarify the usage; Rabbi Yitzchak Zev Yadler (1843
to 1917) reads it as the cornerstone of Torah study, monetary law being rooted
in logical structures, a keystone and wellspring from which all other chiddushim
in learning may be drawn. In Modern Hebrew *miktzoa* means a profession or
occupation, which seems altogether unrelated to the original sense.

### katzeh (קצה)
Root ק-צ-ה, "edge/end," and Onkelos renders this one *zavit* too (Shemos 38:5).
Pappenheim: the corner that forms a **right angle**, and
like *peah* a spacious area at the corner rather than *miktzoa*'s single point.
He separates *katzeh* from *peah* by orientation, *katzeh* being a corner above,
below, in front or behind, and *peah* one at the side, a distinction the column
says frankly it is not sure it understands. He groups here *miketz*/*keitz*,
*katz* (being disgusted, since the disgusted man feels the thing will bring
about the end of his life), *ketzitzah*, *kayitz*, *yekitzah*, *kotz* and
*katzin* (officer), which stands to *katzeh* as *paas* stands to *peah*; the
full family is set out in `waking-yekitzah-oorah.md`.

*Paam* is a further biblical word for corner, which Onkelos likewise renders
*zavit*; the column sends the reader elsewhere for it.

Two rarities complete the set, both too infrequent to carry weight in a
rendering: *atik* (Yechezkel 41:15, 42:3), which Ibn Saruk gives a quadriliteral
root of its own, א-ת-י-ק, Ibn Janach and Radak trace to נ-ת-ק (disengaging,
uprooting), and Pappenheim to TAV-KUF ("static," a panel holding a wall in place
and not letting it move); and *demeshek* (Amos 3:12), a *hapax* that Radak takes
from the context as the corner of a bed, while Rashi explains it as though it
meant Damesek, the city, even though *demeshek* is vocalized with a shin where
the place name has a sin.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *peah* "corner" when it is a corner and "side/direction"
  when the pasuk pairs it with north, south, east or west; *keren* "horn" unless
  the context is plainly a corner; *zavit* "corner" (inner); *pinah* "corner"
  in a building, "cornerstone" or "leading man" when it is figurative;
  *miktzoa* "angle"; *katzeh* "edge" or "end."
- Trap: English "corner" will happily absorb *peah*, *zavit*, *pinah*, *miktzoa*
  and *katzeh* at once. Where the passage turns on the difference (Onkelos
  levelling four Hebrew words to *zavit*, or Pappenheim's angle grades), name
  the geometry: "the sharp angle," "the outer corner," "the right-angled edge."
- *keren zavit* is the Gemara's set phrase for a corner (*Berachos* 53b;
  *Kiddushin* 66a). Render it as one thing, "a corner," not "the horn of the
  corner." Rabbi Chaim Ozer Grodzenski (reported by Rabbi Chaim Pinchas
  Scheinberg at a Degel HaTorah rally) noted the redundancy over a century ago
  and read the phrase as "a corner of a corner," Torah consigned not even to a
  whole corner; the article, having reported it, says that an explanation given
  in a polemic context should probably not be taken as actual linguistic
  analysis. Treat it as a derashah for a note, not a lexical claim.
- Mishnaic *peah* is a halachic term of art. Leave it as *peah* and gloss it;
  "the corner of the field" is misleading, since the *peah* may sit in the
  middle (Mecklenburg on Mishnah *Peah* 3:1). The Mishnah's *rosh tor*
  (*Kilayim* 2:7, 3:3, *Keilim* 18:2), literally "ox head" in Aramaic, is
  likewise not a generic word for corner: Shadal (to Shemos 25:31) explains that
  it is a corner whose angle is intersected by another line, a shape said to
  look like an ox's head.
