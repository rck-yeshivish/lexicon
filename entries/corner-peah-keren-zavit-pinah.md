# Corner: peah / keren / zavit / pinah / miktzoa / katzeh

## Bottom line

*Peah* is the broad **corner** or **side** of an area, and in most pesukim
simply a **direction** (Onkelos: *ruach*). *Keren* is a **horn**, and a corner
as something that juts out (Malbim), in Tanach the corners of the Altar. On Rabbi
Pappenheim's distinction, if Rabbi Klein has understood him correctly, *zavit* is
the inside of a corner and *pinah* is the outside of it, whence leaders are
*pinos*; for Mordechai Zer-Kavod the primary sense of *pinah* in Tanach is not
actually "corner" but a raised place, a **prominence** (hence the
**cornerstone**). For Pappenheim, *miktzoa* is the sharp **angle** where two
sides meet at a point, and *katzeh*, from a root meaning **edge/end**, a
right-angled corner. Onkelos renders *pinah*, *miktzoa* and *katzeh*
as *zavit*, and *peah* too at the corners of the Shulchan.

## Distinctions

### peah (פאה)
The commonest biblical word for corner, roughly eighty-five occurrences, but in
most of them it names a cardinal direction (Shemos 27:9; Bamidbar 35:5), usually
alongside a word for north, south, east or west, where Onkelos generally gives
*rucha*/*ruach*; in the four instances where it means "corner" as opposed to
"direction" (the *peah* of the head and of the beard, Vayikra 19:27, and the
*peah* of the field, Vayikra 19:9, 23:22) he leaves it untranslated. For the
four rings on the four *peah* of the Shulchan (Shemos 25:26, 37:13) Onkelos
renders *zavit*, the word that will matter later.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) gives two etymologies: in *Yerios
  Shlomo* from PEH-HEI, "mouth," a corner being where one enters and leaves an
  area as the mouth is where food enters and words leave; in *Cheshek Shlomo*
  from PEH-ALEPH, "wide corner." For him the Moabite noblemen called *paas*
  (Bamidbar 24:17; Yirmiyahu 48:45) are those set apart at the corner of their
  society and the cornerstones on which it rests.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865): *peah* need not be physically in
  a corner but can be anything cut off and set apart, which is why the *peah* of
  a field may even be left in the middle (Mishnah *Peah* 1:3).

### keren (קרן)
Primarily an animal's **horn**, and in Tanach the horn-like protrusions at the
four corners of the Altar (Shemos 38:2; Vayikra 8:15; Yechezkel 43:20); the
Mishnah widens it to any corner while keeping the biblical altar sense
(*Kilayim* 6:7, *Yoma* 5:5, *Zevachim* 5:3, 6:2, 6:5, 11:3, *Tamid* 2:5, 4:1,
7:3, *Middos* 3:2-3, 4:3, 4:5, *Keilim* 17:9-10).

- Malbim: the core sense is the horn that bulges from the head, borrowed for
  anything projecting outward, so a ray of sunlight is a *keren* and a corner is
  a *keren* because it juts from an otherwise smooth surface. Rabbi Mecklenburg
  (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 27:2, Vayikra 4:7) makes a similar point, and
  Radak even insinuates a connection between the Hebrew *keren* and the Latin
  *cornu*, from which the English "corner" derives by way of French.
- Pappenheim instead traces it to KUF-REISH, "strong impact," which yields
  *korah*, *tikrah* and *kir*, weight-bearing points of impact; a corner is
  where two sides collide.

### zavit (זוית)
Twice in Tanach: the beauty of Jewish daughters likened to the hewn corners of a
palace (Tehillim 144:12), and Yisrael drinking "like the corners of the Altar"
(Zecharyah 9:15). What that image means is disputed.

- Rashi: their victories will leave them overflowing with the good things of the
  spoil, as the corner of the Altar overflows with wine.
- Alternatively, that in defeating their enemies they will figuratively drink
  their blood, spilled as abundantly as the wine that overflows the Altar's
  corners (Radak and Mahari Kara there).

Onkelos uses *zavit* to render several of the other corner words. Pappenheim
links *zavit* to *ziv* (radiance), and offers two ways of making the connection.

- The first, as Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation explains
  him, seems to be that turning a corner is a change of direction, and a shiny
  thing likewise makes one change direction by drawing the eye to it.
- In a separate discussion Pappenheim seems to give a slightly different
  account: light bouncing off a shiny object toward the eye travels at an angle,
  and so produces a corner.

He also claims that Iyar was originally called Ziv (Melachim I 6:1) because in
that month the sun somehow turns a corner, which Rabbi Klein tentatively
suggests may mean its ushering in the new season of spring.

The last of Haman's ten sons was named Vayzasa (Esther 9:9), an anagram of
*zavit*, and Rabbi Benzion Chaim Lubetzky (d. 1945) argues that in so naming him
Haman meant to show off his own importance, as though his sons were the
cornerstones of the Persian Empire.

The Gemara's set phrase for a corner is *keren zavit* (*Berachos* 53b, where one
can use a torch's light without seeing the fire if the two of them stand on
opposite sides of a *keren zavit*; *Kiddushin* 66a, where Torah was so neglected
that it was relegated to a mere *keren zavit* for whoever wished to take it).

- Dr. Avi Hurvitz, a veteran linguist and expert philologist, notes that the
  phrase is a semantic redundancy, since *keren* already means corner and so
  does *zavit*.
- Rabbi Chaim Pinchas Scheinberg (1910 to 2012) related at a Degel HaTorah rally
  that over a century ago Rabbi Chaim Ozer Grodzenski (1863 to 1940) had already
  noted the redundancy and explained the phrase as "a corner of a corner": in
  the time *Kiddushin* describes, Torah was not given even a whole corner, only
  a small corner within a corner, so that the doubled word is for emphasis.

Rabbi Klein holds that an explanation said in a polemic context should probably
not be taken as actual linguistic analysis.

### pinah (פינה)
Twice in the Chumash, both for the corners of the Altar (Shemos 27:2, 38:2),
where Onkelos gives *zavit*, and twenty-eight more times in Tanach.
- If Rabbi Klein has understood him correctly, Pappenheim distinguishes the two
  thus: *zavit* is the inner face of a corner, *pinah* the outer. Stand inside
  the room and it is a *zavit*; stand in the street and the same corner is a
  *pinah*. Hence a *pinah* must be built stronger than the rest, being exposed,
  and hence leaders are *pinos* (Shoftim 20:2; Shmuel I 14:38), men required to
  be of better quality than the rest. He derives it from PEH-NUN,
  "front/surface," the root of *panim* and of *panah*, to turn and face: at a
  *pinah* one must turn to keep following the perimeter; *peninim* (precious
  stones) he suggests reading the same way, a gem polished so its faces and
  their corners show. The Rashba (responsa vol. 4 §278) presumes that *pinah*
  and *zavit* are synonyms.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (1920 to 2016), late Chief Rabbi of Netanya,
  traces both *peah* and *pinah* to the single letter PEH, "end/edge" (with an
  added aleph in *peah* and an added nun in *pinah*), *peah* naming more broadly
  the edge of a given area and *pinah* the edge where two of its sides meet.
- Mordechai Zer-Kavod (*Daas Mikra* to Nechemiah 3:24) makes the primary sense
  "raised place," from *rosh pinah* (Tehillim 118:22), *hapinos hagevohos*
  (Tzefaniah 1:16) and *al hamigdalim v'al hapinos* (Divrei HaYamim II 26:15); the *even
  pinah* stands against the *even mosad* in Yirmiyahu 51:26, foundation stone
  underground and corner stone all the way on top, so that a *pinah* is
  something prominently placed.

### miktzoa (מקצוע)
Root ק-צ-ע, which yields a verb (cutting, removing, peeling) and three nouns:
*maktzua*, the tool for doing it (Yeshayahu 44:13; *Shabbos* 123b), *miktzoa*
itself, and *ketziah*, a spice or incense. Rabbi Klein's guess at what links the
first three is that in cutting or scraping one holds the blade perpendicular to
the surface, forming an angle; and perhaps *ketziah* too comes from the verb,
its plant having had to be cut. *Miktzoa* as corner appears in the Mishnah as
well (*Tamid* 1:4, 3:3, *Middos* 2:5), and Onkelos renders it, like the rest,
*zavit* (Shemos 26:23; see also Rashi to Nechemiah 3:19).
- Pappenheim: where *pinah* covers any angle, right, acute or obtuse, *miktzoa*
  is specifically the acute angle (he concedes it can sometimes be a right
  angle), a single sharp point where two sides converge rather than an area
  spread out; hence he reads the Mishnah's praise of monetary law as a *miktzoa
  baTorah* (*Bava Basra* 10:8) as the discipline that sharpens the mind and
  opens it to higher intellectuality, and he suggests too that the *ketziah*
  spice is so called because its plant has many sharp angles (thorns, Rabbi
  Klein wonders).
- Rabbi Yishmael ben Chachamon, who lived in Egypt in Rambam's time, notes in
  his commentary (*Bava Basra* 175b) that the Mishnah's *miktzoa* means "corner"
  just as it does in Tanach, which, Rabbi Klein observes, does not help clarify
  the usage.
- Rabbi Yitzchak Zev Yadler (1843 to 1917) reads it as the cornerstone of Torah
  study, monetary law being rooted in logical structures, a keystone and
  wellspring from which all other chiddushim in learning may be drawn.

In Modern Hebrew *miktzoa* means a profession or occupation, which seems
altogether unrelated to the original sense.

### katzeh (קצה)
Root ק-צ-ה, "edge/end," and Onkelos renders this one *zavit* too (Shemos 38:5).
Pappenheim: the corner that forms a **right angle**, and
like *peah* a spacious area at the corner rather than *miktzoa*'s single point.
He separates *katzeh* from *peah* by orientation, *katzeh* being a corner above,
below, in front or behind, and *peah* one at the side, a distinction Rabbi Klein
says frankly he is not sure he understands. He relates *katzin* (officer) to
*katzeh* as he relates *paas* to *peah*. Other words from the root include
*miketz*/*keitz*, *katz* (being disgusted, since the disgusted man feels the
thing will bring about the end of his life), *ketzitzah*, *kayitz*, *yekitzah*
and *kotz*; the full family is set out in [waking](waking-yekitzah-oorah.html).

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 47:2): *katzin* comes from
*katzeh*, because the *katzin* is the visible face of an armed force, and so
*pa'asei Moav*. He suggests that *keren* as something important (for example
Eichah 2:3) comes from the same idea.

*Paam* is a further biblical word for corner, which Onkelos likewise renders
*zavit* (Shemos 25:12); it is set out in [foot](foot-paam-regel.html).

Two biblical rarities complete the set: *atik* (Yechezkel 41:15, 42:3), which Ibn Saruk gives a quadriliteral
root of its own, א-ת-י-ק, Ibn Janach and Radak trace to נ-ת-ק (disengaging,
uprooting), and Pappenheim to TAV-KUF ("static," a panel holding a wall in place
and not letting it move); and *demeshek* (Amos 3:12), a *hapax* that Radak takes
from the context as the corner of a bed, while Rashi explains it as though it
meant Damesek, the city, even though *demeshek* is vocalized with a shin where
the place name has a sin.

The Mishnah has one more term, *rosh tor* (*Kilayim* 2:7, 3:3, *Keilim* 18:2),
literally "head of an ox" in Aramaic. Shadal (to Shemos 25:31) explains that it
is not a generic word for corner but a corner whose angle intersects with
another line, a shape said to look like the head of an ox; it also resembles the
letter aleph, since *aleph* literally means "ox."

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *peah* "corner" when it is a corner and "side/direction"
  when the pasuk pairs it with north, south, east or west; *keren* "horn" unless
  the context is plainly a corner; *zavit* "corner" (inner); *pinah* "corner"
  in a building, "cornerstone" or "leading man" when it is figurative;
  *miktzoa* "angle"; *katzeh* "edge" or "end."
- Trap: English "corner" will happily absorb *peah*, *zavit*, *pinah*, *miktzoa*
  and *katzeh* at once. Where the passage turns on the difference (Onkelos
  levelling four Hebrew words to *zavit*, or Pappenheim's angle grades), name
  the geometry: "the sharp angle," "the outer corner," "the right-angled edge."
- *keren zavit* is the Gemara's set phrase for a corner (*Berachos* 53b;
  *Kiddushin* 66a). Render it as one thing, "a corner," not "the horn of the
  corner." Rabbi Chaim Ozer Grodzenski (reported by Rabbi Chaim Pinchas
  Scheinberg at a Degel HaTorah rally) noted the redundancy over a century ago
  and read the phrase as "a corner of a corner," Torah consigned not even to a
  whole corner; Rabbi Klein, having reported it, says that an explanation given
  in a polemic context should probably not be taken as actual linguistic
  analysis. Treat it as a derashah for a note, not a lexical claim.
- Mishnaic *peah* is a halachic term of art. Leave it as *peah* and gloss it;
  "the corner of the field" is misleading, since the *peah* may sit in the
  middle (Mecklenburg on Mishnah *Peah* 1:3). The Mishnah's *rosh tor*
  (*Kilayim* 2:7, 3:3, *Keilim* 18:2), literally "ox head" in Aramaic, is
  likewise not a generic word for corner: Shadal (to Shemos 25:31) explains that
  it is a corner whose angle is intersected by another line, a shape said to
  look like an ox's head.
