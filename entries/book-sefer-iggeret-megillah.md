# Book: sefer / iggeret / megillah (gilayon, shtar, machzor, kuntres)

## Bottom line

*Sefer* is the general **book** or written document, and in early medieval usage
specifically the **scroll**. *Megillah* is the **scroll** meant to be rolled
up, and unqualified it means Megillas Esther. *Iggeret* is a **letter** or
**epistle**, on several accounts a short-lived document sent elsewhere. *Gilayon* is a single
**sheet**, later a margin and then a **gloss**; *shtar* a **deed** meant to
last; *machzor* originally the **codex**, later the yom tov siddur; *kuntres* a
**pamphlet**. Esther is called all three of *sefer*, *megillah* and *iggeret*,
and each name carries a halachah.

## Distinctions

### sefer (ספר)
The generic term, used of the twenty-four books (though some are called a
*megillah*), of a Sefer Torah, and of legal documents (the Mishnah, *Yevamos*
15:3, calls a kesubah a *sefer kesubah*). Moshe's plea after the eigel:
erase me from Your *sefer* that You wrote (Shemos 32:32),
where the word is a book or written document plainly.

- Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk: *sefer* is from *sfar*, a border, since as a
  border guards a country by making one clear it to enter, the Torah guards Klal
  Yisrael in their closeness to Hashem.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro takes the whole ס-פ-ר family as unifying disparate parts:
  *sippur* joins details into one narrative, *sfar* binds a territory into one
  polity, *mispar* and *sofer* bind numbers into one ordered system, and a
  *sefer* binds all it contains into one entity, and this holds of all books, sifrei kodesh among them.
- Professor Raymond P. Dougherty of Yale wrote to explain how *sefer* grows out
  of the core sense "to count": counting is a mental act, memory is unreliable,
  so one falls back on a written tally, and from there *sofer* comes to mean
  scribe and *sefer* a record, a letter, a book.

Avodah Zarah 24b notes that the Persian for *sefer* is *dvir*, which is how it
explains the city Dvir being renamed Kiryas Sefer (Shoftim 1:11).

### megillah (מגילה)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ג-ל, round, with *gal* (a
heap), *golel* (to roll), *galgal* (wheel), *gulgoles* (skull), and *magal*
(sickle): a *megillah* is sheets of parchment sewn together and rolled up. He
puts *gilayon* here too, a single sheet written on and rolled, whence the blank
margins of a document and then the glosses written in them.

Rabbi Shmuel Strashun notices that when Bava Basra 14b lists the books of
Tanach, it says *Sefer Tehillim* and *Megillas Esther* and uses neither word
for any of the other twenty-two. He postulates that *sefer* of Tehillim marks its
affinity to the Chumash, which is likewise divided into five and whose verse
count is comparable (see Kiddushin 30a); and *megillah* of Esther as marking
the requirement that the Esther read on Purim be written on its own scroll and
not one containing other books (Radak to Yirmiyahu 36:1; Megillah 19a).
Unqualified *megillah* in rabbinic literature means Esther, and the berachah is
*al mikra megillah*. Rabbi Shmuel Strashun therefore finds *Maseches Sofrim*
14:3 difficult, since it has that berachah recited over any of the five
megillos; the Vilna Gaon (*Biur HaGra* to *Orach Chaim* 490) nonetheless adopts
that practice, still followed in many communities in Eretz Yisrael.

The claim that a *sefer* rolls on two wooden pins and a *megillah* on one is
untraced: Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein reports that he understood the difference that way from
childhood, that he later looked for a source and came up empty-handed, and that he would welcome one.

### iggeret (אגרת)
Esther calls itself an *iggeret* (Esther 9:26-29), and Shmuel (Megillah 7a)
holds it "was said to be read, not said to be written." *Iggeres* and its
variations appear only in the later, post-Exilic books, Esther, Ezra and
Nechemiah, and Divrei HaYamim II. That distribution fits the view of Professor
Avi Hurvitz: he takes *iggeret* and *sefer* as synonyms, both meaning "letter," and holds that
the choice between them reflects nothing but the stage of the language, *sefer*
the older variant and *iggeret* the later one.

Rabbi Dr. Jose Faur proffers a different account, from Divrei HaYamim II 30:6,
where Chizkiyahu sends runners with *igros* calling for teshuvah: an *iggeret* is
a government decision publicized aloud by a royal herald reciting from a written
memorandum, with no copies released to the public, so that one could say of it "said to be
read, not said to be written," whereas a *sefer* is an officially published work
from which copies may be made. He notes that a herald would typically unfold the whole
scroll before reading, and that an *iggeret* would not be read with the taamim;
from which he concludes that reading Esther today, with the taamim but with the
scroll first unfolded entire, is a synthesis or compromise of *iggeret* and
*sefer*.

Ritva distinguishes *shtar* from *iggeret* by durability: a *shtar* is written
to last, ostensibly so as to serve as evidence of what it records, while an
*iggeret* is not. Rabbi Yosef Dov Soloveitchik applies the same sort of axis to
*sefer* against *iggeret* (see Yirmiyahu 32:14), though without quoting Ritva
directly, and uses it to explain Megillah 19a, where Rav
holds that although Esther is called an *iggeret*, which would allow sewing its
sheets with linen thread, since it is also called a *sefer* it must be sewn
with sinews, the more lasting material. (Against this, Sotah 13a uses *iggeret*
for a document attesting a sale, which is closer to a *shtar*.)

In Mishnaic Hebrew *iggeret* typically means a writ issued by a beis din: a get
(*Gittin* 6:5, and *Gittin* 9:3 gives *sefer teiruchin*, *iggeres shevukin* and
*get pitturin* as synonyms), an *iggeres mered* issued against a wife who refuses
her wifely duties (Yevamos 64a), an
*iggeres bikores* announcing intent to sell property (*Kesubos* 11:5). It also
covers halachic letters sent from Eretz Yisrael to Bavel by Ravin (Kesubos 49b,
Bava Metzia 114a, Niddah 68a; see also Shabbos 115a, Bava Basra 41b,
Sanhedrin 29a), and Shabbos 19a speaks of sending an *iggeret* by post.

On etymology the authorities split.
- Rabbeinu Tam (in Tosafos to Kesubos 100b): an *iggeret* is a document issued
  by one beis din to be sent to another. In line with that, Rabbi Moshe Chaim
  Leiter of Kiryat Sefer suggests it may derive from ג-ר, temporary residence,
  since a document written to be sent elsewhere has only a short-lived first
  home.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim offers a similar understanding, a document moved about
  a province so as to publicize its contents thoroughly, which is closer still
  to Rabbi Faur.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer derives it instead from *agur*, a collection
  stored long term, so an *iggeret* is a document meant to be preserved, which
  on the surface, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says, seems to contradict Rabbi Faur
  and Rabbi Soloveitchik.

Malbim's summary is the compact one: an *iggeret* is a document
between two parties; a *sefer* tells (*mesaper*) about a subject; a *megillah*
is written to be rolled (*golel*); a *gilayon* is written to be revealed
(*gilui*) and not rolled or folded; *ksav* is anything written.

### machzor (מחזור) and kuntres (קונטרס)
In early medieval sources a Tanach codex is a *machzor* and *sefer* means the
scroll. The name is from ח-ז-ר, to return, since one can return to a codex's
beginning at once, unlike a scroll that must be rolled from one end to the other.
The other medieval word for a codex was *mitzchaf*, apparently borrowed from the
Arabic *mishaf*; it fell out of use, and *machzor* came to mean the yom tov
siddur, now recalling the returning cycle of the year's chagim.

A *kuntres*, often rendered "notebook," is in current speech a short treatise
not weighty enough to stand as its own *sefer*; the Tosafists typically call
Rashi's Gemara commentary the *kuntres*, and though Rashi does not use the word
of his own work he uses it of his rebbi's commentary (to Gittin 82a).

- Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia, Rabbi Ernest Klein and Rabbi Daniel Sperber derive it
  from the Greek for commentaries, κομμεντάριον, Rabbi Sperber explaining the
  term from the
  records of the public register that documented official government acts.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) derives it instead from the Latin
  *quinternus*, a quire of five sheets of paper.

## Rendering guidance

- Esther: keep each name where the source uses it. "The Megillah" for the
  Purim reading, "the Book of Esther" where the pasuk-count or canon is at
  issue, and "letter" only where the pasuk itself says *iggeret* (Esther
  9:26-29) or where the sugya turns on it (Megillah 19a).
- Trap: English "book" absorbs *sefer* and *megillah*, and "letter" absorbs
  *iggeret*, *shtar*, and *get*. In a halachic passage leave *shtar*, *get*,
  and *kuntres* transliterated; only *iggeret* is safely "letter."
- *Sefer* in a citation of a title (*Sefer HaShorashim*, *Sefer Tehillim*)
  stays transliterated as part of the name; *sefer* as a common noun stays "sefer," not "book," where the reference is to a work of
  Torah.
- *Gilayon* is "sheet" in Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's sense, "margin" in the
  later sense, and "gloss" where it names a commentary printed there. Pick from
  the context; the three are a chain, not synonyms.
- *Machzor* needs a gloss the first time it appears in a medieval context,
  since a modern reader hears the yom tov siddur and the source means a codex.
