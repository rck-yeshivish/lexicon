# Hearing: shema / haazinu

## Bottom line

*Haazinu* is from *ozen*, an ear: **give ear**, the plain physical act of
listening. *Shema* is **hear** in the fuller sense, taking in what is said and
understanding it (Malbim), or hearkening in answer to a call (Rokeach). On
distance, the Sifrei has *haazinu* for what is near and *shema* for what is far;
Chizkuni, Abarbanel and Rabbi Yitzchak Arama hold the reverse. The trap: English
"hear" and "listen" are used loosely, but Devarim 32:1 and Yeshayahu 1:2 assign
the two Hebrew verbs to heaven and earth in opposite order, and any translation
that does not keep them apart destroys the question the meforshim are answering.

## Distinctions

### haazinu (האזינו)
From *ozen*, ear. On this reading the verb names the ear's own work, catching
sound and passing it on, with nothing said about whether the hearer grasped it.
Rokeach: *haazinu* is simply listening of any kind, the unmarked verb.
Elsewhere he offers a second distinction: *haazinu* is listening and inferring
something only said implicitly, against *shema* for what was stated explicitly.
The Sifrei (to parashas Haazinu): *haazinu* is listening from close range.

### shema (שמע)
Malbim: *shema* is not merely the physical act; it carries a measure of
intellectual or emotional grasp of what is heard. Rokeach: *shema* is hearkening
in response to another's call, that is, hearing that answers. The column notes
that these two explanations, Malbim's and Rokeach's first, fail to account for
the change in phraseology between Moshe's opening and Yeshayahu's, which is the
question the essay set out with; Rokeach's second distinction is brought after
that observation. The Sifrei: *shema* is listening from a distance, which
demands that a man be especially attentive to the sound, concentrate properly,
hear what should be heard, and focus on its meaning.

### the reversal in Devarim and Yeshayahu
Moshe Rabbeinu opens with *haazinu hashamayim* and *v'sishma haaretz* (Devarim
32:1); Yeshayahu opens with *shimu shamayim* and *v'haazini eretz* (Yeshayahu
1:2), exactly reversed. The Sifrei resolves it by proximity: each navi used the
near-verb for what he was near to. Moshe was nearer the heavens and so said
*haazinu* to them; Yeshayahu was nearer the earth and said *haazini* to it. Why
one navi should count as nearer heaven than another, given that both were
nevi'im, is answered three ways in the meforshim: that Moshe spoke his shirah
just before his petirah, with one foot already above, while Yeshayahu spoke at
the opening of his career; that Moshe is the father of all the nevi'im, who
alone heard "mouth to mouth, in a clear vision and not in riddles" (Bamidbar
12:8), and whose nevuah became the Torah itself, where Yeshayahu's upheld it;
and that Moshe was used to ascending, having gone up the mountain repeatedly and
for long stretches.

Chizkuni, Abarbanel and Rabbi Yitzchak Arama reject the Sifrei's assignment and
define the two verbs the other way round, explaining the difference between the
two nevi'im accordingly. Some of the Baalei HaTosafos take the two words as
interchangeable, used for the poetry of the verse; most meforshim will not allow
true synonyms in lashon hakodesh and therefore must distinguish them.

## Rendering guidance

- Devarim 32:1: "Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; and let the earth hear
  the words of my mouth." Yeshayahu 1:2: "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O
  earth." Keep "give ear" for *haazinu* and "hear" for *shema* in both, so the
  reversal is visible in English. This is the whole datum.
- שמע ישראל (Devarim 6:4): "Hear, O Yisrael." Not "listen"; by Malbim the word
  already carries understanding, and by Rokeach it carries answering a call,
  both of which "hear" holds and "listen" weakens.
- Trap: English "listen" is the natural word for attentive hearing and will pull
  toward *shema*, while "hear" sounds passive and will pull toward *haazinu*,
  which is the reverse of the Hebrew. Resist the instinct: *haazinu* is the ear,
  *shema* is the mind.
- Where *shema* means to obey (*shamoa tishme'u*), render "hearken" or "obey"
  and note the shift. (This bullet is ours; the article does not treat the
  obedience sense.)
- If a passage follows Chizkuni, Abarbanel or Rabbi Yitzchak Arama rather than
  the Sifrei, say which view the rendering assumes rather than choosing silently;
  the two sides assign near and far to opposite words, and each explains the
  difference between Moshe and Yeshayahu accordingly.
