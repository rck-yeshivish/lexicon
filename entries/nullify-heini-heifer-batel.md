# Nullify: heini / heifer / batel

## Bottom line

*Heini* (*yani*) works on what has not yet come about: **restrain**, **hold
back**, stop a plan from being carried out. *Heifer* works on what already
exists: **annul**, **break**, crumble a standing legal reality out of being.
That is why Bamidbar 30 uses both of a husband nullifying his wife's neder: he
*heini*s her plan and *heifer*s the vow (Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer).
*Batel* is the Rabbinic word, apparently Aramaic in origin: **void**, and by
extension **idle, futile**.

## Distinctions

### heini (הניא), yani (יניא)
Rabbi Wertheimer: *heini* removes something that may not yet have come into
being. Tehillim 33:10 is his proof: Hashem *heini* the designs of the peoples
(*machshevos amim*), and because those thoughts are merely abstract ideas
representing only a potential for something to happen, their cancellation is
*heini*.

The word's basic sense was already disputed among the early grammarians.
Menachem Ibn Saruk: to break. Dunash Ibn Labrat, following Rav Saadia Gaon: to
deny or withhold. Rashi (to Bamidbar 30:6) follows Dunash; Ibn Ezra (to Shemos
12:9, Bamidbar 14:3, Bamidbar 30:6, Tehillim 141:5) follows Menachem, though he
also seems to treat *heini* as equivalent to *heifer*. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi
Mecklenburg weighs the two at length, framing the question as whether *heini*
is the removal of the will (Menachem) or the withholding of the will's
fulfillment (Dunash). A century after Menachem and Dunash the controversy was
still raging. Rabbi Dr. Yosaif M. Dubovick, "'Oil, which shall not quit my head':
Jewish-Christian Interaction in Eleventh-century Baghdad," *Entangled
Religions* vol. 6 (2018), discusses *yani* in Tehillim 141:5 and the historical
controversy the question generated.

Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 12:10-13) links *heini* to the similarly spelled *na*,
raw, half-cooked, the common idea being interruption or stopping: cooking cut
off partway, a vow's fulfillment hindered and impeded (so too *Midrash Sechel
Tov* to Shemos 12:9). Rabbi Klein calls the association somewhat questionable
all the same: *heini* and *yani* are generally spelled with an aleph, like *na*,
but Tehillim 141:5 has *yani* without one.

### heifer (הפר), hafarah (הפרה)
Rabbi Wertheimer: *heifer* cancels something that has already come into being,
and the word is kin to *pur*, a crumb, since *hafarah* breaks a standing thing
into pieces and grinds it out of existence. So one who abrogates a mitzvah
(Bamidbar 15:31) or a covenant (Yeshayahu 44:25) is like a man tearing up a
binding contract.

On the two words in the parashah of nedarim, Rabbi Wertheimer explains that the
father or husband accomplishes two things at once. He stops the woman from doing what she undertook to do, and
he dissolves the legal standing of the vow. The Torah says *heini* of the
first, since what she meant to do has not yet come about, and *heifer* of the
second, since the vow's legal reality exists and can therefore be broken.

Malbim similarly explains that *heini* refers to forcing
something out of existence, while *heifer* refers to simply ensuring that
something will not come to fruition, without necessarily using force.

### batel (בטל)
The standard Rabbinic verb for nullification: *bitul Torah*, idleness in place
of learning; *bitul chametz*, declaring one's chametz void before Pesach;
*bitul b'rov*, a forbidden food nullified in a majority. From the futility in
*batalah* the word came to cover whatever seems purposeless. Its root appears to
be of Aramaic origin: seven occurrences in Tanach (Koheles 12:3 and six times in
Ezra 4-6), against more appearances in the Targumim. Rabbi Klein floats, as his own
suggestion, a conceptual tie to *tal*, dew, which comes at dawn and is gone.

## Rendering guidance

- In Bamidbar 30, distinguish: *heini* is "restrain" or "hold her back";
  *heifer* is "annul" or "void." "He annulled her vow and restrained her" keeps
  both alive.
- Tehillim 33:10 has both verbs, and the pairing is worth keeping visible: the
  *heini* clause is "He frustrates the designs of the peoples," where there is
  no standing instrument to annul. Do not attach "annuls" to that clause.
- *Heifer bris* (Yeshayahu 44:25 and its like): "broke the covenant," which
  keeps the *pur* image; "violated" loses it.
- *Batel* and *bitul* stay transliterated in halachic contexts (*bitul
  chametz*, *bitul b'rov*, *bitul Torah*) with a gloss at first use. In plain
  prose, "void" or "nullified."
- Trap: English "nullify" will swallow all three. Where a source is reading the
  Torah's switch between *heini* and *heifer*, keep the Hebrew visible; a
  translation that says "annul" twice has erased the derashah.
- *Batalah* in a mussar passage is "idleness," not "nullification"; the two
  senses of the root do not travel together into English.
