# Miser: kilay / tzaykan / kamtzan

## Bottom line

Three words for the same vice, one per layer of the language: *kilay* is the
Biblical **miser**, *tzaykan* the Mishnaic **skinflint**, *kamtzan* the
post-Mishnaic and Modern Hebrew **cheapskate**. Each carries its own image:
*kilay* locks his money up, *tzaykan* is narrow and hard about parting with it,
*kamtzan* measures out the smallest possible amount. The one thing not to get
wrong: *kilay* is disputed. Commentators such as Radak read it as "miser," the
Ashkenazic commentators (Rashi, Mahari Kara, Rabbi Eliezer of Beaugency) as
"trickster," and a translation of Yeshayahu 32:5-7 must choose.

## Distinctions

### kilay (כילי)
Twice in Tanach, both in one perek (Yeshayahu 32:5, 32:7). Radak (there and in
*Sefer HaShorashim*) reads it from the contrast with *shoa* and *nadiv*, the
open-handed: the *kilay* is the opposite, careful to a fault about what he gives,
laying out money or effort only by exact measure.

Roots. Radak and Ibn Janach in their respective *Sifrei HaShorashim* assign it to
כ-י-ל, of which it is the sole derivative; Radak alternatively to כ-ו-ל, a
measured quantity. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the biliteral כ-ל,
"all-encompassing," whence *kele* (jail, which detains the whole man), and reads
*kilay* as one who jails his own money and goods so nobody else benefits. His
other derivatives of כ-ל: *kol*, *kallah* (adorned with every sort of ornament),
*kalkalah* (providing for all one's needs), *makoles*, *keli* (what surrounds its
contents on all sides), *heichal*, *kalos* (finishing, coming to be or being
destroyed entirely), and *achilah*.

Ibn Ezra (to Yeshayahu 32:5) writes that on an exegetical plane we may read
*kilay* as a portmanteau of *ki yomar sheli sheli*, "when a man says, what is
mine is mine," a veiled reference to the Mishnah in Avos 5:10, which lists four
types of men: one who says what is mine is mine and what is yours is yours is
either the average sort or of the sort of Sodom; what is mine is yours and what
is yours is mine, an ignoramus; what is mine is yours and what is yours is
yours, a chassid; what is mine is mine and what is yours is mine, a rasha (see
Rabbeinu Yonah there).

*Kilay* in the sense of cheapskate is a rather obscure and archaic biblical
word, the more so as it seems not to have been used in Mishnaic Hebrew, and
even so it saw a resurgence in medieval Sephardic circles. In Ashkenazic
circles it went largely unused, for an interesting reason: the Ashkenazic
commentators read it not as a cheapskate but as a trickster, taking the
biblical *kilay* as a derivative of נ-כ-ל, deceit. So Rashi, Mahari Kara and
Rabbi Eliezer of Beaugency in their commentaries to Yeshayahu, all of them
influenced by Menachem Ibn Saruk's *Machberes*, which files *kilay* under the
biliteral כ-ל but classes it with *nochel* (Malachi 1:14), *nichleihem*
(Bamidbar 25:18) and *vayisnaklu* (Bereishis 37:18), all of treacherous
plotting; on the triliteralists' account those words come from נ-כ-ל.
Pappenheim connects נ-כ-ל back to כ-ל by explaining that treacherous plotting
is the final decision after an entire process of planning and thinking has
been completed. Rashi has a third gloss elsewhere (to Sotah 41b): a desirous
man who always wants to drink wine.

*Bereishis Rabbah* 51:1 refers to an animal called a *kilay*, and there are
several ways of identifying it. One is that it is a sort of snail, the
understanding preferred by the Maskillic writer Shalom Yaakov Abramowitz
(1836-1917), better known as Mendele Mocher Seforim, in his scientific
encyclopedia *Toldos HaTeva*, and Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894) also seems to
favor it. Some write that the *kilay* is a *Schildkröte*, the German for
turtle. Either way both creatures carry a shell about with them, and on that
basis Solomon Rabinowitz of New York (d. 1943) in his *Sefer HaMishkalim* draws
the comparison to the cheapskate: as the cheapskate retreats into himself and
is not generous with others, so the snail or turtle retreats into its shell
rather than deal with the world, locking itself in and coming out only when the
coast is clear. Rabinowitz conjectures from this that perhaps the Midrash's
name for the creature was somehow influenced by the biblical *kilay*.

### tzaykan (צייקן)
Mishnaic. Pesachim 7:8: disqualified Pesach meats that a man must burn with his
own wood are brought by the *tzaykanim* to the Mikdash instead, so that the
Mikdash's wood pays for the burning. That is the picture: the cheapskate who
spends the community's money rather than his own. Rashi (Pesachim 81b) glosses
them as ungenerous, using a cognate of *atzar*, stopping, for how they stop their
money from reaching anyone else; so too Rashi and Rabbeinu Chananel to Eruvin
49a.

Etymology is not readily obvious. Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein (no relation to the
columnist) theorizes that *tzaykan* derives from *tzuk*/*tzok*, a narrow strait
or pressure, in the sense of the miser's narrow-mindedness about which causes
deserve his money. Another possible etymology is *mutzak*, a hard metal, his
hardness relating to the *tzaykan*'s hard-headed stubbornness about parting
with what is his; Pappenheim, as is his way, traces both words back to the
biliteral צ-ק. The word is rare but not dead:
*Orchos Tzaddikim* titles its chapter on frugality *Shaar HaTzaykanus*, where the
trait is a virtue, not a vice.

### kamtzan (קמצן)
Post-Mishnaic and the living word today. It appears already in some girsaos of
the Tosefta (Sotah, in the story of the Kohen who took two portions of lechem
hapanim and still got only a bean's worth; the article prints 13:58), and
cognates appear in the Gemara mostly as verbs, where Rashi again reaches for
*atzar* (Menachos 86a, Chullin 46a; Rashbam to Bava Basra 52b).

The root is ק-מ-צ, seven times in Tanach, six of them the *kemitzah* of a
minchah (Vayikra 2:2, 5:12, 6:8, Bamidbar 5:26). Rashi (Vayikra 2:2, Kesubos 8b,
Zevachim 64b, Menachos 11a): the kohen closes his three middle fingers over his
palm to take the flour and wipes away the excess with thumb and pinky, so that
not a grain more than those three fingers hold remains. *Kemitzah* is therefore
both containment and holding the amount to its minimum, which is exactly the
*kamtzan*: he stores his wealth and keeps to the least anyone can get from him.
The seventh appearance, *likematzim* (Bereishis 41:47), describes Mitzrayim's
yield in the years of plenty; Radak (there and in *Sefer HaShorashim*): from a
minimal seeding the land gave three-finger loads of produce, again the sense of
smallest measure.

The Aramaic *kamtza* seems to belong here too. Targum uses it for *arbeh*
(Mishlei 30:27), *yelek* (Nachum 3:15) and *chagav* (Bamidbar 13:33, Yeshayahu
40:22), all grasshoppers, and this perhaps relates to *kemitzah* and *kamtzan*
in that a single grasshopper out of a whole swarm is a small quantity from
within a much larger pool. Hence the two readings of *ein hakometz masbia es
ha'ari* (Berachos 3b, Sanhedrin 16a): that a *kometz* is a pithy sustenance,
too small a quantity to provide the lion's share, or that *kometz* here means a
grasshopper and one grasshopper will not satisfy a lion's hunger (Rashi to
Sanhedrin 16a and to Ein Yaakov on Berachos 3b, and *Hagahos HaBach* there).
Kamtza and Bar Kamtza, for whose sake the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed (Gittin
55b), are proper names of Jews at the close of the second Beis HaMikdash; but
Rabbi Chaim of Friedberg, the Maharal's brother, in *Sefer HaChaim* offers a
homiletical reading, that the dictum speaks of *kamtzanus* passed from one
generation to the next, which cost the Jewish People their fear of Heaven and
left them caring only for themselves. See also Maharal's *Netzach Yisrael*
ch. 5 on the connection between *kemitzah*, Kamtza and Bar Kamtza, and
grasshoppers.

## Rendering guidance

- Yeshayahu 32:5-7: if following Radak, "miser" or "churl" against *nadiv*
  "noble" and *shoa* "man of means." If following Rashi and the Ashkenazic
  rishonim, "knave" or "schemer." Do not blend the two into "scoundrel," which
  hides the machlokes.
- *Tzaykan*: "skinflint" or "tightfisted." Keep the transliteration in a
  Mussar context, since *Shaar HaTzaykanus* names a virtue and "miserliness"
  would misrepresent the chapter's title.
- *Kamtzan*: "cheapskate" in speech, "miser" in prose.
- *Kemitzah* and *kometz* stay transliterated in any korbanos context; "handful"
  is wrong, because Rashi's whole point is that the excess is wiped away and it
  is not a handful but a measured three-finger amount.
- Trap: English "stingy" will absorb all three, and "miser" will absorb *kilay*
  and *kamtzan*. When two appear together, or when the point is which layer of
  Hebrew a word belongs to, keep the transliterations and gloss once.
- *Ein hakometz masbia es ha'ari*: render "a scant handful does not satisfy the
  lion" only if the first reading is intended; if the grasshopper reading is
  meant, *kometz* must stay transliterated with the gloss, since one English
  word cannot hold both.
