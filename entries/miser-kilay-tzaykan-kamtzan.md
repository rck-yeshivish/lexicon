# Miser: kilay / tzaykan / kamtzan

## Bottom line

Three words for the same vice, one per layer of the language: *kilay* is the
Biblical **miser**, *tzaykan* the Mishnaic **skinflint**, *kamtzan* the
post-Mishnaic and Modern Hebrew **cheapskate**. Each carries its own image:
*kilay* locks his money up, *tzaykan* is narrow and hard about parting with it,
*kamtzan* measures out the smallest possible amount. The one thing not to get
wrong: *kilay* is disputed. Sephardic rishonim read it as "miser," Ashkenazic
rishonim (Rashi, Mahari Kara, Rabbi Eliezer of Beaugency) as "trickster," and a
translation of Yeshayahu 32:5-7 must choose.

## Distinctions

### kilay (כילי)
Twice in Tanach, both in one perek (Yeshayahu 32:5, 32:7). Radak (there and in
*Sefer HaShorashim*) reads it from the contrast with *shoa* and *nadiv*, the
open-handed: the *kilay* is the opposite, careful to a fault about what he gives,
laying out money or effort only by exact measure.

Roots. Radak and Ibn Janach in their respective *Sifrei HaShorashim* assign it to
כ-י-ל, of which it is the sole derivative; Radak alternatively to כ-ו-ל, a
measured quantity. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the biliteral כ-ל,
"all-encompassing," whence *kele* (jail, which detains the whole man), and reads
*kilay* as one who jails his own money and goods so nobody else benefits. His
other derivatives of כ-ל: *kol*, *kallah* (adorned with every sort of ornament),
*kalkalah* (providing for all one's needs), *makoles*, *keli* (what surrounds its
contents on all sides), *heichal*, *kalos* (finishing, coming to be or being
destroyed entirely), and *achilah*.

Ibn Ezra (to Yeshayahu 32:5) offers a derashah: *kilay* as *ki yomar sheli
sheli*, the man who says "what is mine is mine," pointing at the Mishnah in Avos
5:10 and its middah of Sodom.

The Ashkenazic line reads the word differently. Rashi, Mahari Kara and Rabbi
Eliezer of Beaugency to Yeshayahu take *kilay* from נ-כ-ל, deceit, so the man is
a trickster and not a miser; they follow Menachem Ibn Saruk's *Machberes*, which
files *kilay* under כ-ל together with *nochel* (Malachi 1:14), *nichleihem*
(Bamidbar 25:18) and *vayisnaklu* (Bereishis 37:18). Pappenheim reconciles the
two by making treacherous planning the settled conclusion of an entire process of
thought, hence כ-ל. Rashi has a third gloss elsewhere (to Sotah 41b): a man of
appetite who always wants wine. Because of the deceit reading, *kilay* went
largely unused in Ashkenazic circles while it revived among the Sephardim.

*Bereishis Rabbah* 51:1 names an animal *kilay*, identified variously as a snail
or a turtle. Solomon Rabinowitz (*Sefer HaMishkalim*) draws the comparison to the
miser: both withdraw into a shell and come out only when it suits them.

### tzaykan (צייקן)
Mishnaic. Pesachim 7:8: disqualified Pesach meats that a man must burn with his
own wood are brought by the *tzaykanim* to the Mikdash instead, so that the
Mikdash's wood pays for the burning. That is the picture: the cheapskate who
spends the community's money rather than his own. Rashi (Pesachim 81b) glosses
them as ungenerous, using a cognate of *atzar*, stopping, for how they stop their
money from reaching anyone else; so too Rashi and Rabbeinu Chananel to Eruvin
49a.

Etymology is not obvious. Rabbi Ernest Klein: from *tzuk*/*tzok*, a narrow strait
or pressure, the miser being narrow about what deserves his money. A second
possibility is *mutzak*, hard metal, for his hardness in parting with it;
Pappenheim traces both back to the biliteral צ-ק. The word is rare but not dead:
*Orchos Tzaddikim* titles its chapter on frugality *Shaar HaTzaykanus*, where the
trait is a virtue, not a vice.

### kamtzan (קמצן)
Post-Mishnaic and the living word today. It appears already in some girsaos of
the Tosefta (Sotah, in the story of the Kohen who took two portions of lechem
hapanim and still got only a bean's worth; the article prints 13:58), and
cognates appear in the Gemara mostly as verbs, where Rashi again reaches for
*atzar* (Menachos 86a, Chullin 46a; Rashbam to Bava Basra 52b).

The root is ק-מ-צ, seven times in Tanach, six of them the *kemitzah* of a
minchah (Vayikra 2:2, 5:12, 6:8, Bamidbar 5:26). Rashi (Vayikra 2:2, Kesubos 8b,
Zevachim 64b, Menachos 11a): the kohen closes his three middle fingers over his
palm to take the flour and wipes away the excess with thumb and pinky, so that
not a grain more than those three fingers hold remains. *Kemitzah* is therefore
both containment and holding the amount to its minimum, which is exactly the
*kamtzan*: he stores his wealth and keeps to the least anyone can get from him.
The seventh appearance, *likematzim* (Bereishis 41:47), describes Mitzrayim's
yield in the years of plenty; Radak (there and in *Sefer HaShorashim*): from a
minimal seeding the land gave three-finger loads of produce, again the sense of
smallest measure.

The Aramaic *kamtza* belongs here too. Targum uses it for *arbeh* (Mishlei
30:27), *yelek* (Nachum 3:15) and *chagav* (Bamidbar 13:33, Yeshayahu 40:22), all
grasshoppers, one out of a swarm being the small quantity drawn from a large
pool. Hence the two readings of *ein hakometz masbia es ha'ari* (Berachos 3b,
Sanhedrin 16a): a scant handful cannot fill a lion, or a single grasshopper
cannot (Rashi to Sanhedrin 16a and to Ein Yaakov on Berachos 3b, and *Hagahos
HaBach* there). Rabbi Chaim of Friedberg (*Sefer HaChaim*), the Maharal's
brother, reads Kamtza and Bar Kamtza (Gittin 55b) homiletically as
*kamtzanus* passed from father to son; see also *Netzach Yisrael* ch. 5.

## Rendering guidance

- Yeshayahu 32:5-7: if following Radak, "miser" or "churl" against *nadiv*
  "noble" and *shoa* "man of means." If following Rashi and the Ashkenazic
  rishonim, "knave" or "schemer." Do not blend the two into "scoundrel," which
  hides the machlokes.
- *Tzaykan*: "skinflint" or "tightfisted." Keep the transliteration in a
  Mussar context, since *Shaar HaTzaykanus* names a virtue and "miserliness"
  would misrepresent the chapter's title.
- *Kamtzan*: "cheapskate" in speech, "miser" in prose.
- *Kemitzah* and *kometz* stay transliterated in any korbanos context; "handful"
  is wrong, because Rashi's whole point is that the excess is wiped away and it
  is not a handful but a measured three-finger amount.
- Trap: English "stingy" will absorb all three, and "miser" will absorb *kilay*
  and *kamtzan*. When two appear together, or when the point is which layer of
  Hebrew a word belongs to, keep the transliterations and gloss once.
- *Ein hakometz masbia es ha'ari*: render "a scant handful does not satisfy the
  lion" only if the first reading is intended; if the grasshopper reading is
  meant, *kometz* must stay transliterated with the gloss, since one English
  word cannot hold both.
