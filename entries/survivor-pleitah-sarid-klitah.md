# Survivor: pleitah / sarid / klitah

## Bottom line

The Targumim generally render *klitah* and *pleitah* alike, with the Aramaic
word for salvation (*shizba*/*shaziv*), which suggests that in some ways the
two are synonyms, both "saving" in general. At the core, though, *pleitah* is
rescue by **expulsion**: one is spit out of the danger, so a *palit* is a
**refugee**. *Klitah* is rescue
by **reception**: one is taken in, as a *miklat* receives him, so it is
**refuge**, **taking in**. For Malbim and Bedersi *sarid* is the one who was
caught and could not get out and lived anyway: **survivor** or **remnant**.
Rabbi Avraham Yitzchak Shain, accounting for another pattern in their usage,
explains that the *palit* was totally surrounded and spit out, while the *sarid*
was not necessarily totally enveloped.
*Sarid u'palit* is a stock pair.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What separates sarid from palit.**

- Malbim (Rabbi Meir Leibush Weiser, 1809 to 1879): a *palit* was in the
  trenches of war or another calamity and was able to flee, saving himself (in
  Rabbi Klein's terms, "spit out" of harm's way); a *sarid* was caught in the
  middle, unable to escape, and somehow survived anyway. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi
  (13th century) presents the same explanation in brief in *Chotam Tochnit*. So
  *palit* is "refugee," *sarid* more accurately "survivor" or "remnant."
- The Vilna Gaon (to Yeshayahu 1:9) implies that *sarid* has a quantitative
  aspect: a member of the exclusive few who survived when the vast majority did
  not.

Rabbi Avraham Yitzchak Shain notices another pattern: in general, a survivor
of a war waged by non-Jews is a *palit* in Tanach (e.g. Bereishis 14:13;
Yechezkel 33:21-22), and of a war waged by Jews, a *sarid* (Bamidbar 21:35;
Devarim 2:34, 3:3; Yehoshua 10:28, 10:30, 10:33, 10:37, 10:39, 10:40, 11:8;
Melachim II 10:11). For him *palit* is one who was totally surrounded
("swallowed" or "absorbed") by enemies and "spit out" so that he escaped, while
*sarid* is simply a survivor not necessarily totally enveloped by enemy troops. A Jewish
army may not besiege a city or enemy on all four sides but must leave one side
open for refugees (*Sifrei* to Matos §157; Rambam, *Hilchos Melachim* 6:7), so
its enemies were never surrounded to be "spit out," and their survivors are
generally called only *sarid*; non-Jewish armies typically attack from all
sides, so their survivors are *palit*, not *sarid*. Where Tanach says there was
neither *sarid* nor *palit* from Ai (Yehoshua 8:22), Rabbi Shain explains that the people of Ai
used the humanitarian corridor to attack the Jewish army, so the army was
allowed to surround them on all sides.

**Where klitah and pleitah come from.**

- Rabbi Yitzchak of Zeldin (*Shoresh Yesha*): ק-ל-ט is a metathesis of ל-ק-ט,
  "gathering" (*leket*, *yalkut*), since those sheltered together are as if
  gathered up into a safe spot.
- Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation takes a different
  approach: a metathesis of ק-ט-ל, "killing," since *klitah* often refers to
  those received by a sanctuary and so escaping death.
- Most philologists and lexicographers trace *pleitah* to the triliteral פ-ל-ט,
  as Rabbi Klein himself does, but some take the פ as not part of the core root:
  Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (1821 to 1898, *Otzar Nirdafim* §380) derives
  *pleitah* from the two-letter ל-ט, and Rabbi Steinberg, in an essay titled
  "Run, Lot Run," connects *pleitah* and many other words to that biliteral
  root; he even derives *klitah* from it, so that on his view the two words
  share an etymological source.

**Bigdei srad (Shemos 31:10, 35:19, 39:1, 39:41).**
- Rashi and his grandson Rashbam (to Shemos 31:10): not the bigdei kehunah,
  which are listed separately, but the cloth covers put over the Mishkan's
  vessels for travel (Bamidbar 4:1-15). Rashi cites Targum Onkelos for *srad*
  here as "service," but admits that ש-ר-ד is not used that way elsewhere.
- Ibn Ezra and Chizkuni (to Shemos 31:10) read *srad* as a cognate of *sarid*:
  the covers were made from the cloth left over after the donated materials were
  used for the other cloth parts of the Mishkan, so *srad* and *sarid* share the
  theme of remnants, survivors, leftovers.
- Ramban (to Shemos 31:10) takes them to be the bigdei kehunah, and so does the
  Gemara (Yoma 72a-b): were it not for the bigdei kehunah, through whose service
  Yisrael's sins are atoned, no *sarid* or *palit* of the nation would remain.

## Distinctions

### pleitah (פליטה)
Root פ-ל-ט. *Pleitah* typically refers to a survivor, remnant or refugee, as
the *palit* who brings Avraham word that Lot was captured (Bereishis 14:13). A
closer look at the root, though, shows that its core sense is spitting out,
releasing, emitting. Targum to Yonah 2:11 uses its Aramaic cognate for the fish
spitting Yonah out; the Gemara (Chullin 108b, 111a, 112b) uses *polet* for a
vessel expunging absorbed taste. So in the context of rescue, *pleitah* is being
spit out of harm's way, spared by being taken out of or excluded from a
destructive fate. Modern Hebrew keeps the dichotomy with *klitah* in computer
science, *kelet* for input and *pelet* for output, and in similar terms for
neurological processes. In the Mishnah, the property of individual idolaters,
who are stoned, is *palat*, spared their fate, unlike the property of the
idolatrous city, whose people die by the lighter death of the sword (Sanhedrin
10:4); and the Sotah's remaining body is not *palit* from her fate (Sotah 1:7).
Expulsion with nothing to do with saving: a woman who "emits" (*poletet*) semen
within three days of intimacy is tamei (Berachos 3:6, Shabbos 9:3, Mikvaos 8:3).
The expulsion can also be to one's detriment: the sinners in the wilderness
were expelled from the Clouds of Glory and became easy prey for Amalek
(*Midrash Tanchuma*, Ki Seitzei §10; Targum pseudo-Yonasan and Rashi to
Devarim 25:18), all of these sources using cognates of *pleitah*. Despite the
possible negative connotation, various Biblical personal names derive from the
root: Palti (Bamidbar 13:9; Shmuel I 25:44), Pilti (Nechemiah 12:17), Paltiel
(Bamidbar 34:26; Shmuel II 3:15), Platyah (Nechemiah 10:23; Divrei HaYamim I
3:21, 4:42) and Platyahu (Yechezkel 11:1, 11:13); the post-Biblical name Paltoi,
popular in Geonic and early Medieval times, is also seemingly from this root.

### sarid (שריד)
Root ש-ר-ד. The third Biblical term for a survivor of war or other calamity.
Malbim and Bedersi separate it from *palit* by the absence of flight; the Gra
implies a quantitative sense; Rabbi Shain ties it to wars in which the enemy was
not surrounded. After Yehoshua 8:22, "*sarid* and *palit*" became a stock phrase
in Tanach (Yirmiyahu 42:17) and the Gemara (Berachos 7a, Yoma 72b and 77a,
Megillah 12b, Sanhedrin 44b, 95b, 105b, Avodah Zarah 4b). Sarid is also a place
on the southern border of Zevulun's territory (Yehoshua 19:10, 19:12), and in
recent times a popular Jewish name.

### klitah (קליטה)
Root ק-ל-ט. The Masoretic siman at the end of Vayishlach gives *klitah* for the
parashah's 154 pesukim. The word is neither in the parashah nor a Biblical name,
but echoes Yaakov dividing his camp so that the rest would be "for survival"
(Bereishis 32:8), and the Haftarah's Har Tziyon as a place "for survival"
(Ovadiah 1:17); the word in both pesukim, though, is *pleitah*. Rabbi Klein
corrects himself on the name: Rabbi Nosson Kamenetsky (1930 to 2019) points out
that the name Klita appears three times in Tanach (Ezra 10:23; Nechemiah 8:7,
10:11), though spelled with a final aleph, not the final hei of the Masoretic
word.

The noun *klitah* itself does not appear in Tanach; two forms of the root do.
*Miklat* (its mem not part of the root), "place of receiving," appears twenty
times, all in Bamidbar and Yehoshua except two in Divrei HaYamim I (6:42,
6:52), and always of the *ir miklat*, which receives the one who killed by
mistake and protects him from his victim's avengers. In Modern Hebrew a *miklat*
is a bomb shelter, and *klitah* is reception: of an idea, of a cell-phone
signal, of immigrants. *Kalut* (Vayikra 22:23; Bechoros 6:7, 7:6) is an animal
whose hooves are not split: what a shelter receives is protected by being
closed in, and these hooves are likewise closed. The Mishnah's verb is plain
reception: an object thrown through a public domain on Shabbos and caught
(*kalat*) by a person or a dog (Shabbos 11:6); dye that, according to Beis
Shammai, must take (*kalat*) to the cloth before Shabbos (Shabbos 1:6). The
receiving sense is so central that it can even be reception to one's death, so
that *klitah* means "saving" only incidentally, when the one receiving is a
shelter: when David passed Shaul's family before the Aron to decide whom to put
to death for the Givonim, those whom the Aron was *kolet* were killed (Yevamos
79a), which Rabbi Yosef Chaim of Baghdad (1832 to 1909, *Benayahu* there)
explains as their being miraculously rendered immobile, held by a kind of force
field from the Aron.

## Rendering guidance

- The stock pair שריד ופליט: "neither survivor nor refugee," or "no remnant and
  no fugitive." Do not render both with "survivor"; the pasuk is exhaustive by
  covering two different ways out.
- פליט in a narrative (Bereishis 14:13): "a fugitive" or "a refugee," not "a
  survivor," since the point is that he got away.
- שריד standing alone: "remnant" where the sense is quantitative (Yeshayahu 1:9
  with the Gra), "survivor" where it is about a person who lived through it.
- Trap: English "escape" covers both the *palit* who fled and the *sarid* who
  merely was not killed; mark the flight in the *palit* line explicitly.
- Trap: קלט does not mean "save." Translate it "receive," "take in" or "catch,"
  and let the rescue follow from the context, since the same verb is used for
  being seized to one's death (Yevamos 79a).
