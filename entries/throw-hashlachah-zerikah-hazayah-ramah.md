# Throwing: hashlachah / zerikah / hazayah / ramah

## Bottom line

*Hashlachah* is usually careless, disparaging throwing: **throw away**, **cast
off**, or even **drop** (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim); it does not show whether the
thrower means to hit anything, and for Maharal it does not imply force, though
Rabbi Pappenheim concedes purposeful uses too. *Zerikah* is, for Rabbi
Pappenheim, purposely causing something to land in a chosen spot, typically
matter that spreads as it falls, so **throw**, and where the object is fine or
liquid, **sprinkle**; in Chazal it is a general term for throwing, which
sometimes connotes throwing purposefully and forcefully (Maharal: it implies
force). *Hazayah* is *zerikah* at a longer distance (Rabbi Pappenheim) or by
hand rather than with a vessel (Malbim): **sprinkle**. *Ramah* is throwing
upward in an arc so that it lands exactly on its target: **hurl**, **shoot**.
Maharal reads *hashlachah* as throwing without force, and so, Rabbi Yehoshua
Hartman suggests, does Rashbam.

## Distinctions

### hashlachah (השלכה)
The story of Yosef has *hashlachah* three times: as the brothers plan to throw
his body into a pit, as Reuven persuades them to throw him in alive, and as they
do (Bereishis 37:20, 22, 24). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau: *hashlachah*
is throwing in a way that does not show whether one means to hit something or
merely to be rid of it, and the word carries a disrespectful attitude toward
what is thrown. Because it is the casual or callous form, it usually means
throwing downward, the easiest direction, and "dropping" or "throwing away" may
be the more accurate English. Most of its occurrences, he notes, are
disparaging: Hagar casting off Yishmael (Bereishis 21:15), Yosef thrown into the
pit, Moshe throwing down the Luchos (Shemos 32:19), Pharaoh's decree against the
infants (Shemos 1:22), *tereifah* meat thrown to the dogs (Shemos 22:30). He
admits that some are deliberate, to bring about a result: Aharon throwing his
staff (Shemos 7:10) and the gold into the fire (Shemos 32:24). Rabbi Klein adds
that the same could be said of the cedar, hyssop and crimson thread thrown into
the burning of the *parah adumah* (Bamidbar 19:6).

Ramban (to Bereishis 21:15) offers two readings of *vatashlech*: Hagar
**abandoned** Yishmael, so *hashlachah* is a form of forsaking; or she **sent
him away**, proved from Devarim 29:27 and Tehillim 51:13, which in effect lets
ש-ל-כ mean the same as its near-homonym ש-ל-ח (near, since in the Ashkenazi
pronunciation ches and chaf sound alike).

On the root, most grammarians, such as Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak,
take it as the triliteral ש-ל-כ. Rabbi Pappenheim instead builds it from two
biliterals, ש-ל ("throwing out") and ל-כ ("going"). Linguists propose a similar
theory, cited by Ernest Klein and Avraham Even-Shoshan in their dictionaries:
the rare *shaphel* conjugation, in which an initial *shin* is not part of the
root but denotes creating the situation the root names (compare *shiabud* from
עבד, *shichrur* from חרות, and Modern Hebrew *shichpul* and *shichtuv*). On that
theory *hashlachah* comes from the two-letter root for going, and the *shin*
makes it causing something to go from one place to another by throwing. Rabbi
Moshe Shapiro, on a tangent, applies the same *shaphel* pattern to *shapir*,
from *tiferes*/*pe'er*. (A folk etymology ties the surname Shapiro to *shapir*;
historians derive it more likely from the German town Speyer.)

In Modern Hebrew *hashlachos* are consequences, a usage not attested in Tanach
or rabbinic writings, perhaps a result as a "throw-off" from its cause.

### zerikah (זריקה)
Rabbi Pappenheim: a portmanteau of ז-ר ("spreading, dispersal") and ר-ק
("emptying"). *Zerikah* is purposely causing something to land in a specific
spot, and it usually involves matter of many small parts (sand, ashes, any
liquid) that spreads as it falls; hence the ritual sprinkling of the *korbanos*'
blood is *zerikah*. (In Modern Hebrew a *zerikah* is a shot.) Chazal, he
explains, use it for any throwing, deliberate or not: the Mishnah (Shabbos 11:1)
uses *zarak* for throwing an object from one *reshus* to another, where the
destination is the point, and the Gemara (Chagigah 15a) uses *zarak* for Rabbi
Meir throwing away the peel of Acher's teaching, where the destination is
irrelevant.

Rashi (to Shemos 9:8): Moshe was to throw the ashes with one hand, since Hashem
wanted them thrown with full force and it is easier to throw forcefully with one
hand. Maharal explains how Rashi knew that force was meant: the Torah says
*zerikah*, which implies throwing with force, where *hashlachah* does not. Rabbi
Yehoshua Hartman suggests that this distinction underlies Rashbam to Shemos
32:19: Moshe was so enraged at the Golden Calf that he could not gather the
strength to throw the Luchos and merely dropped them; Rabbi Hartman infers that
Rashbam, like Maharal, took *vayashlech* as dropping without force.

### hazayah (הזיה)
Rabbi Pappenheim: the same kind of throwing as *zerikah*, only over a longer
distance. Malbim: *zerikah* is throwing or sprinkling with a vessel, *hazayah*
by hand.

### ramah (רמה)
The root ר-ם means "esteemed" or "high," and in Biblical Hebrew it occasionally
means throwing (Shemos 15:1, *ramah vayam*). Rabbi Pappenheim: *ramah* is
throwing or shooting something upward in an arc so that it lands precisely on
its target. The Targumim render *zerikah* with the cognate ז-ר-ק but render
*hashlachah* with ר-ם derivatives, so, Rabbi Klein concludes, in Aramaic the ר-ם
root seems to mean what *hashlachah* means, perhaps the lifting of an object in
order to throw it.

Rabbi Klein's conclusion: *hashlachah* and *zerikah* are the two basic words;
*hazayah* and *ramah* are like *zerikah* with a twist, *hazayah* throwing long
distance and/or by hand, *ramah* throwing upward to land on target.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "throw" absorbs all four. Reserve plain "throw" for *zerikah*
  and render *hashlachah* "cast away," "throw down," "fling aside," or "drop," so
  the contempt in the Hebrew survives; where it is purposeful (Aharon's staff),
  plain "cast" or "throw" is right.
- Yosef in the pit (Bereishis 37:24): "they flung him into the pit," not "placed"
  and not "hurled with all their strength"; the word marks that he was tossed
  there haphazardly.
- Moshe and the Luchos: if the passage follows Rashbam (as Rabbi Hartman reads
  him), "he let the Luchos drop" is the reading the Hebrew supports; "hurled"
  contradicts it.
- *Zerikas hadam* and *hazayah* in a *korbanos* context: Rabbi Klein calls
  *zerikas hadam* "sprinkling," but in a passage that lists both, keep them
  apart, "throwing" or "dashing" the blood for *zerikah* and "sprinkling" for
  *hazayah* (a judgment call); one English "sprinkle" for both loses the
  halachic distinction.
- Modern Hebrew *hashlachos*, "consequences," has no Biblical or rabbinic
  standing; do not read it back into a pasuk.
