# Throwing: hashlachah / zerikah / hazayah / ramah

## Bottom line

*Hashlachah* is careless, disparaging throwing: **throw away**, **cast off**, or
even **drop**; it says nothing about force or target. *Zerikah* is deliberate,
forceful throwing at a place: **throw**, and where the object is fine or liquid,
**sprinkle**. *Hazayah* is *zerikah* at a distance (or, per Malbim, by hand
rather than by vessel): **sprinkle**. *Ramah* is throwing upward in an arc onto
its target: **hurl**, **shoot**. The trap is treating *hashlachah* as vigorous;
Maharal and Rashbam both read it as the opposite.

## Distinctions

### hashlachah (השלכה)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau: *hashlachah* is throwing in a way that does
not show whether one means to hit something or merely to be rid of it, and the
word carries a disrespectful attitude toward what is thrown. Because it is the
casual form, it usually means throwing downward, the easiest direction. Most of
its occurrences are contemptuous: Hagar casting off Yishmael (Bereishis 21:15),
Yosef thrown into the pit (Bereishis 37:20, 22, 24), Moshe throwing down the
Luchos (Shemos 32:19), Pharaoh's decree against the infants (Shemos 1:22),
*tereifah* meat thrown to the dogs (Shemos 22:30). Pappenheim concedes a
purposeful use as well: Aharon throwing his staff (Shemos 7:10) and the gold
into the fire (Shemos 32:24), and the cedar, hyssop and crimson thread thrown
into the burning of the *parah adumah* (Bamidbar 19:6).

Ramban (to Bereishis 21:15) offers two readings of *vatashlech*: Hagar
**abandoned** Yishmael, so *hashlachah* is a form of forsaking; or she **sent
him away**, proved from Devarim 29:27 and Tehillim 51:13, which effectively lets
ש-ל-כ carry the sense of its near-homonym ש-ל-ח.

On the root, Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak take it as the triliteral
ש-ל-כ. Pappenheim instead builds it from two biliterals, ש-ל ("throwing out")
and ל-כ ("going"). Rabbi Ernest Klein records the *shaphel* theory, in which an
initial *shin* is a grammatical prefix creating the situation of the root
(compare *shiabud* from עבד, *shichrur* from חרות), leaving ל-ח, "going." Rabbi
Moshe Shapiro applies the same *shaphel* pattern to *shapir*, from
*tiferes*/*pe'er*.

### zerikah (זריקה)
Pappenheim: a portmanteau of ז-ר ("spreading, dispersal") and ר-ק ("emptying").
*Zerikah* is causing something to land in a chosen spot, and it typically
involves matter of many small parts (sand, ashes, liquid) that spreads as it
falls; hence the *zerikas hadam* of the *korbanos* is rendered "sprinkling."
Chazal widen it to any throwing, deliberate or not: the Mishnah (Shabbos 11:1)
uses *zarak* for throwing an object from one *reshus* to another, where the
destination is the whole point, and the Gemara (Chagigah 15a) uses *zarak* for
Rabbi Meir discarding the peel of Acher's teaching, where the destination is
irrelevant.

Maharal: *zerikah* implies throwing with force, *hashlachah* does not. He
derives this from Rashi to Shemos 9:8, where Moshe throws the ashes with one
hand precisely so as to throw them forcefully. Rabbi Yehoshua Hartman reads
Rashbam to Shemos 32:19 the same way: Moshe was too enraged to gather strength
to throw the Luchos, so he merely dropped them, and the Torah accordingly says
*vayashlech*.

### hazayah (הזיה)
Pappenheim: the same kind of throwing as *zerikah*, only over a longer distance.
Malbim: the difference is instrument, not distance; *zerikah* is done with a
vessel, *hazayah* by hand.

### ramah (רמה)
The root ר-ם means "esteemed" or "high," and in Biblical Hebrew it occasionally
means throwing (Shemos 15:1, *ramah vayam*). Pappenheim: *ramah* is throwing or
shooting something upward on an arc so that it lands exactly on its target. The
Targumim render *zerikah* with the cognate ז-ר-ק but render *hashlachah* with
ר-ם derivatives, so in Aramaic the ר-ם root appears to carry the sense of
*hashlachah*, perhaps the lifting of an object in order to throw it.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "throw" absorbs all four. Reserve plain "throw" for *zerikah*
  and force *hashlachah* into "cast away," "throw down," "fling aside," or
  "drop," so the contempt in the Hebrew survives.
- Yosef in the pit (Bereishis 37:24): "they flung him into the pit," not "placed"
  and not "hurled with all their strength"; the word marks carelessness.
- Moshe and the Luchos: if the passage is quoting Rashbam or Maharal, "he let
  the Luchos drop" is the reading the Hebrew supports; "hurled" contradicts it.
- *Zerikas hadam* and *hazayah* in a *korbanos* context: "throwing" or "dashing"
  the blood for *zerikah*, "sprinkling" for *hazayah*, and keep them apart in a
  passage that lists both; one English "sprinkle" for both loses the halachic
  distinction.
- Modern Hebrew *hashlachos*, "consequences," has no Biblical or rabbinic
  standing; do not read it back into a pasuk.
