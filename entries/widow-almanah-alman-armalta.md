# Widow: almanah / alman / armalta (karmelis)

## Bottom line

*Almanah* is **widow**, *alman* **widower**, *almenus* **widowhood**. The
Aramaic *armalta* is not a synonym but the same word in Aramaic, so a bilingual
text should render both "widow" and not manufacture a distinction. Watch the
Mishnaic widening: in Kesubos 1:2 *almanah* covers any previously married woman,
divorced as well as widowed, and translating it "widow" there is a halachic
error. And *almenos* in Yeshayahu 13:22 is not widows at all but **deserted
palaces** on the majority reading.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

| אלמן | alman | widower | once in Tanach (Yirmiyahu 51:5); the masculine that any etymology must also explain (Chasam Sofer) |
| אלמנות | almenus | widowhood | the state, four occurrences; masculine *almon* once (Yeshayahu 47:9) |
| ארמלתא | armalta | widow | Aramaic; standard in Targum and both Talmuds; the same word, not a second one |
| כרמלית | karmelis | intermediate domain | *k'armelis*, "like a widow," in between (Rambam to Shabbos 1:1) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where almanah comes from.** The word looks quadriliteral, א-ל-מ-נ, and
quadriliteral roots are generally held to be loanwords, so the mefarshim work to
reduce it.

1. **From א-ל-ם, muteness.** Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the widow is left
   muted, with nobody to argue for her if she is attacked, and the nun is the
   agent suffix, as *rachamim* yields *rachaman*. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*),
   Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*), and Rav Hirsch (to Shemos
   22:21) all adopt it. Menachem Ibn Saruk omits *almanah* and *alman* from
   *Machberes Menachem* entirely, so his view is unrecoverable.
2. **From al plus manah, "not a portion."** The Chasam Sofer (responsa *Even
   HaEzer* vol. 2 §170), reading א-ל as "not" and מ-נ as substance (as in
   *mahn*): husband and wife each sustain the other, and the survivor is left
   without that sustenance, which explains *alman* as readily as *almanah*.
   Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 22:21) says
   nearly the same: the two fused into one person, and the survivor lives with a
   portion (*manah*) that is not (*al*).
3. **From "emptied."** Rabbi Shlomo Eiger (*Gilyon Maharsha* to Kesubos 2a)
   argues backwards from *almenos* as deserted palaces: as a palace is deserted
   of its dwellers, so a wife is deserted by her husband's death. Yerushalayim
   after the Churban is "like an *almanah*" (Eichah 1:1) in exactly that sense.
4. **Two further proposals.** Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche: א-ל ("not") plus a
   metathesized מ-ל-א ("full"), the wife emptied of her husband; or, dropping the
   aleph and reversing, מ-ל, "cutting," her voice cut off, as Ibn Janach has it.

The Bottom line follows Ibn Janach, the reading the lexicographers and Rav
Hirsch carry, and treats the *al manah* readings as the derashah they are, which
is how the Chasam Sofer himself frames his: homiletic, since one may not expound
Biblical nouns as compounds. He makes the point with a joke, that one could as
easily derive *lechem* from *milchamah* and rule that a man may not eat bread
before fighting for it.

**How Chazal read the word.** Kesubos 10b cites Rav Chana of Baghdad that
*almanah* hints at the later enactment giving a widow's kesubah a *manah*, a
hundred zuz, against the besulah's two hundred (Kesubos 1:2). What the *al*
contributes is itself disputed: Rabbi Yehuda Aryeh of Modena (*HaBoneh* to *Ein
Yaakov* there) and the Geonim cited by Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi (*Shittah
Mekubetzes*) read "not a *manah*," she lacks the extra hundred a besulah has;
Rabbi Ashkenazi reads Rashi as taking *al manah* for *elah manah*, "only one
*manah*"; Rabbi Shmuel Strashun (*Hagahos Rashash*) reads *al* as the definite
article, as in Arabic. The Ritva stresses that the Gemara is not overturning the
grammarians: *almanah* does come from א-ל-ם, and the nun was added so the coined
word would allude to the kesubah's value. *Peirush HaRokeach* (to Vayikra 21:14)
adds that the gematria of *almanah* equals that of *meah zuzin*. The Chasam
Sofer presses two difficulties: the *manah* coin postdates Tanach, and it is odd
to name a widow for a sum she collects only on remarrying and being widowed or
divorced again, odder still that the derashah leaves *alman* unexplained.

**What almenos means in Yeshayahu 13:22** ("cats will inhabit their *almenos*").
Targum Yonasan, Rabbi Saadia Gaon, Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Balaam, Rashi, Mahari Kara,
Ibn Parchon, and Radak read it as *armenos*, palaces, on the interchange of
lamed and reish. Abarbanel and Malbim link it instead to *ulam*, a hall. Ibn
Ezra and Ibn Kaspi reject the lamed-reish interchange outright and read
*almenos* as palaces emptied of their dwellers, keeping the widow sense alive.

**Whether a man may marry a widow.** The Zohar (*Mishpatim* 102a) warns of
danger, the first husband's spirit opposing the match, and Rabbi Aharon
Berechiah of Modena (*Maavar Yabok*, *Sefas Emes* ch. 9) finds it hinted in
*al manah*, that such a match should not be one's portion. Rabbi Mordechai
Spielman (*Tiferes Tzvi* to that passage) writes at length that this concern
lives only in kabbalistic sources and that the revealed halachah sees nothing
wrong with marrying a widow, so a translator must not present the Zohar's
warning as halachah.

## Distinctions

### almanah (אלמנה) and its family
Fifty-six occurrences, many of them warnings not to oppress her (Shemos 22:21,
Devarim 24:17, Yeshayahu 1:17 and 1:23, Yirmiyahu 7:6 and 22:3, Zechariah 7:10)
or descriptions of Hashem as her protector (Tehillim 68:6, 146:9). Laws attach
to her specifically: a widow's vow stands (Bamidbar 30:10), and the tithes are
left for her (Devarim 24:19-21, 26:12-13). *Almenus*, widowhood, occurs four
times (Bereishis 38:14, 38:19, Shmuel II 20:3, Yeshayahu 54:1); *alman*
(Yirmiyahu 51:5) and *almon* (Yeshayahu 47:9) once each. In Rabbinic Hebrew the
noun becomes a verb, *nisalmenah* (Nedarim 11:9, Makkos 1:1), and alongside it,
already in the Mishnah, the Aramaic-shaped *nisarmelah* (Yevamos 6:3 and 13:4,
Kesubos 2:1, 4:2, 5:1).

### armela and armalta (ארמלא, ארמלתא)
The standard Aramaic word in the Targumim for *almanah*, and throughout both
Talmuds (Yerushalmi Shabbos 1:4, Yoma 1:1, Kesubos 9:3; Bavli Taanis 5b, Kesubos
17b and 54a, Nedarim 50a, Gittin 35a, Bava Metzia 71a, Sanhedrin 109a, Avodah
Zarah 22b, Niddah 69a). Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche reads it as א-ר, strength
used euphemistically for its opposite, plus מ-ל, cutting: the widow weakened and
unable to speak for herself.

### almanta (אלמנתא) and karmelis (כרמלית)
Rambam (*Hilchos Yibbum V'Chalitzah* 4:34) appears to coin an Aramaicized
*almanta* for halachic documents, a word nobody else uses; the Rabbi Shabsai
Frankel edition (*Yalkut Shinuyei Nuschaos* there) records that the Oxford and
Cambridge manuscripts read the ordinary *armalta*, so the coinage is probably a
printer's word. Rambam also reads the third Shabbos domain as *k'armelis*, "like
a widow," the kaf a prefix and an aleph hidden (commentary to Mishnah Shabbos
1:1): as a widow is neither maiden nor wife, so this domain is neither reshus
harabim nor reshus hayachid.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "widow" absorbs *almanah* in both its senses. In Kesubos 1:2 write
  "a previously married woman" or gloss it, since the halachah covers a divorcee
  too; in Tanach write "widow."
- *Armalta* and *almanah* both come out "widow." Where a sugya moves from the
  Mishnah's Hebrew to the Gemara's Aramaic, do not invent a difference in
  English; they are one word across two languages.
- Yeshayahu 13:22: render "their palaces," not "their widows," and footnote Ibn
  Ezra's "emptied palaces" if the drash on the word matters.
- Keep *alman* as "widower." English has the word, and dropping it hides that
  Hebrew marks the masculine at all, which is precisely the Chasam Sofer's
  question.
- Where a source expounds *al manah*, do not translate the compound into
  running English ("no portion"); quote the Hebrew and explain, or the derashah
  reads as an etymology, which the Chasam Sofer expressly says it is not.
