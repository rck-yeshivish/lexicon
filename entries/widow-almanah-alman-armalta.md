# Widow: almanah / alman / armalta (karmelis)

## Bottom line

*Almanah* is **widow**, *alman* **widower**, *almenus* **widowhood**. The
Aramaic *armalta* is, as the column argues from the fluidity of such consonants
and the Semitic cognates, not a synonym but the same word in Aramaic, so a
bilingual text should render both "widow" and not manufacture a distinction.
Watch the Mishnaic widening: in Kesubos 1:2 *almanah* covers any previously
married woman, divorced as well as widowed, and translating it "widow" there
misstates the halachah, though even in Rabbinic Hebrew it usually means widow.
The etymology is disputed: Ibn Janach's muteness (א-ל-ם), which Radak, Ibn
Parchon and Rav Hirsch cite approvingly and the Ritva calls the grammarians'
derivation, stands beside *al manah* readings, deserted palaces, and academic
proposals. And *almenos* in Yeshayahu 13:22 is not widows: Targum Yonasan,
Rashi, Radak and others read **palaces**, Abarbanel and Malbim relate it to a
hall, and Ibn Ezra and Ibn Kaspi read **palaces emptied** of their dwellers.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where almanah comes from.** On the surface the word is quadriliteral,
א-ל-מ-נ, with a feminine hei; four-letter roots are rare, and many grammarians
argue that words with ostensibly four- or five-letter roots are often loanwords.
The column's readings:

1. **From א-ל-ם, muteness.** Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the widow is left
   muted, with nobody to argue for her if anyone fights with her, and the nun is
   the agent suffix, as *rachamim* yields *rachaman*. Radak (*Sefer
   HaShorashim*), Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*), and Rav
   Hirsch (to Shemos 22:21) cite it approvingly. Menachem Ibn Saruk omits
   *almanah* and *alman* from *Machberes Menachem* entirely, so there is no way
   of knowing his view.
2. **From ל-מ-נ, poor.** The *Theological Dictionary of the Old Testament*
   mentions the possibility of a root ל-מ-נ, otherwise attested in Akkadian
   *lemnu*, "poor."
3. **From al plus manah, "no portion."** The Chasam Sofer (responsa *Even
   HaEzer* vol. 2 §170), reading א-ל as "not" and מ-נ as portion or substance
   (as in *mahn*): husband and wife each provide sustenance to the other, and
   the survivor is left bereft of it, hence *almanah* or *alman*. Rabbi Yaakov
   Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 22:21) offers a similar
   explanation: the two fused into one person, and the survivor lives as if a
   part (*manah*) of him is not (*al*). The Chasam Sofer makes clear that his
   reading is homiletic, since on the literal plane there is no basis for
   expounding Biblical nouns so; he illustrates it ironically, that one cannot
   derive *lechem* from *milchamah* and rule that a man may not eat bread until
   he has fought over it (though, the column notes, some exegetes have offered
   comparable explanations of *lechem*).
4. **From "deserted."** Rabbi Shlomo Eiger (*Gilyon Maharsha* to Kesubos 2a),
   the Chasam Sofer's brother-in-law, inverts the discussion of *almenos* in
   Yeshayahu 13:22 (below): since *almenos* are deserted castles, so *almanah*
   is a wife deserted by her husband's death.
5. **Two proposals of Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche** (1920 to 2016), the late
   Chief Rabbi of Netanya: א-ל ("not") fused with a metathesized מ-ל-א ("full"),
   the wife emptied of her husband; or, creatively dropping the alef and
   reversing, מ-ל, "cutting," her voice cut off in her extreme distress, with no
   husband to speak for her, similar to Ibn Janach's understanding.

Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionary, calls *almanah* and
*armela* of uncertain origin; he cites Jacob Barth deriving *armela* from
ר-מ-ל (compare Arabic *murmil*, *armal*, needy, helpless), and Theodor Noldeke
and Rudolf Ruzicka connecting both to Aramaic *alima*, feeling pain.

The Bottom line follows Ibn Janach, the reading the grammarians cite
approvingly; that choice is ours.

**How Chazal read the word.** On the Mishnah that a besulah's kesubah is two
hundred zuz and an *almanah*'s one hundred (Kesubos 1:2), the Gemara (Kesubos
10b; see *Ben Yehoyada* there) cites Rav Chana of Baghdad that the Biblical
word *almanah* alludes to the future rabbinic enactment giving a widow a
*manah*, a hundred zuz, should her next husband divorce her or die. It seems
that Chazal parse the word as *al* plus *manah*. What the *al* contributes is
itself disputed: Rabbi Yehuda Aryeh of Modena (*HaBoneh* to *Ein Yaakov*,
Kesubos 10b) reads "not a *manah*," she lacks the additional *manah* a
besulah's kesubah has, and Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi (*Shittah Mekubetzes*
there) cites a similar explanation in the name of the Geonim; Rabbi Ashkenazi
also reads Rashi as taking *al* for *elah*, "only one *manah*"; Rabbi Shmuel
Strashun (*Hagahos Rashash*) reads *al* as the definite article, as in Arabic.
The Ritva stresses that the Gemara grants that *almanah* derives from א-ל-ם, per
the grammarians, and says the added nun coins a word alluding to the kesubah's
value. In the same spirit *Peirush HaRokeach* (to Vayikra 21:14) notes that the
gematria of *almanah* equals that of the Aramaic *meah zuzin*. The Chasam Sofer
adds that the allusion must be to a post-Biblical enactment, since the *manah*
currency came only after the Biblical period, and raises difficulties: it is
illogical to name a widow for the sum written in her kesubah if she remarries,
which would fit a remarried widow rather than the widow herself; and the
derashah does not explain why a widower is called *alman*. These and other
difficulties lead him to his own reading above.

**What almenos means in Yeshayahu 13:22** ("cats will inhabit their *almenos*").
One group, Targum Yonasan, Rabbi Saadia Gaon, Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Balaam, Rashi,
Mahari Kara, Ibn Parchon, and Radak, reads it as *armenos*, castles or palaces,
on the interchange of lamed and reish; see also Abarbanel and Malbim, who
relate it to *ulam*, a hall. Ibn Ezra and Ibn Kaspi reject that, contending
(bizarrely, in the column's word) that lamed and reish do not interchange, and
read *almenos* as palaces emptied of their dwellers; so too Yerushalayim after
the destruction of the First Beis HaMikdash is "like an *almanah*" (Eichah 1:1),
emptied of its Jewish inhabitants.

**Whether a man may marry a widow.** The Zohar (*Mishpatim* 102a) speaks of the
danger, the first husband's spirit opposing the match, and Rabbi Aharon
Berechiah of Modena (*Maavar Yabok*, *Sefas Emes* ch. 9) finds it alluded to in
*al manah*, that such a match should not be one's lot. Rabbi Mordechai
Spielman (*Tiferes Tzvi* to Zohar *Mishpatim* 102a) writes at great length that
this "prohibition" exists only in kabbalistic sources and that by the revealed
law of the Gemara there is nothing wrong with marrying a widow, so a translator
must not present the Zohar's warning as halachah.

## Distinctions

### almanah (אלמנה) and its family
*Almanah* appears in Parashas Emor of the widow a Kohen Gadol may not marry
(Vayikra 21:14) and of the Kohen's daughter who returns to eat terumah (Vayikra
22:13). In all, fifty-six occurrences in Tanach, many of them warnings not to
afflict her (Shemos 22:21, Devarim 24:17, Yeshayahu 1:17 and 1:23, Yirmiyahu 7:6
and 22:3, Zechariah 7:10) or descriptions of Hashem as her protector (Tehillim
68:6, 146:9). Laws attach to her specifically: a widow's vow stands (Bamidbar
30:10), and the tithes are left for her (Devarim 24:19-21, 26:12-13). *Almenus*,
widowhood, occurs four times (Bereishis 38:14, 38:19, Shmuel II 20:3, Yeshayahu
54:4); *alman*, widower (Yirmiyahu 51:5), and *almon*, widowerhood (Yeshayahu
47:9), once each. In Rabbinic Hebrew *almanah* becomes a synecdoche, naming both
any formerly married woman and, usually, a widow. The noun also yields a
passive verb, *nisalmenah* (Nedarim 11:9, Makkos 1:1), and already in the
Mishnah the form *nisarmelah* (Yevamos 6:3 and 13:4, Kesubos 2:1, 4:2, 5:1).

### armela and armalta (ארמלא, ארמלתא)
By the Talmud a new Aramaic word has become the standard for widow, in the
Yerushalmi (Shabbos 1:4, Yoma 1:1, Megillah 3:1, Kesubos 9:3, 10:3, 11:2, Sotah
3:4, Nedarim 5:3, Kiddushin 1:3, Bava Basra 9:1, Shevuos 6:6, Avodah Zarah 2:8)
and the Bavli (Taanis 5b; Kesubos 17b, 54a, 97a, 100a; Nedarim 50a; Gittin 35a,
68a; Bava Metzia 71a; Sanhedrin 109a; Avodah Zarah 22b, 30a; Niddah 69a); it is
also the Targumim's standard for *almanah*. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche reads it
as Aramaic א-ר, strength, used euphemistically for its opposite, plus מ-ל,
cutting: the widow weakened and unable to speak for herself.

It might be tempting to call *armela* a synonym of *almanah*, but the column
holds it more cogent that they are different forms of the very same word.
Linguists teach that sporadic changes in dental and liquid consonants are
normal, and other Semitic languages have similar but not identical words:
Arabic *armla*, *armala*; Syriac *armalta*; Assyrian and Akkadian *almattu*;
Ugaritic *almnt*; Amharic *armoma*. So *armela* and *almanah* are not synonyms
but cognates from adjacent languages.

### almanta (אלמנתא) and karmelis (כרמלית)
Rambam (*Hilchos Yibbum V'Chalitzah* 4:34) seems to coin an Aramaicized
*almanta* for halachic documents, a word nobody else uses, which is strange for
so common a thing. But the Rabbi Shabsai Frankel edition (*Yalkut Shinuyei
Nuschaos* there) shows that the Oxford and Cambridge manuscripts do not have
*almanta* and read the ordinary *armalta*. Rambam also reads the third Shabbos
domain as *k'armelis*, "like a widow," the kaf a prefix and an alef hidden
(commentary to Mishnah Shabbos 1:1): as a widow is in between, no longer a
maiden and not a married woman, so this domain is neither fully reshus harabim
nor fully reshus hayachid.

### Armilus
The name of Armilus, the anti-Messiah figure of late Midrashic and medieval
texts, most famously the Geonic-era *Sefer Zerubavel*, has drawn various
etymologies: from the *armillatus* of Caligula, the emperor's bracelet; a
corruption of the Persian deity Ahriman or Angro-Mainyus, which most scholars
reject; most accepted, from Romulus, or a corruption of Aurelianus; or from a
Greek Eremolaos, Eremelaus, "destroyer of people." The column proposes, as a
plausible though admittedly speculative Semitic etymology, the Aramaic *armela*:
the name would then evoke the Jewish people's national bereavement, or
spiritual desolation, in the period before the redemption. Even if not
etymologically true, it says, the idea holds as *drush*.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "widow" absorbs *almanah* in both its senses. In Kesubos 1:2 write
  "a previously married woman" or gloss it, since the halachah covers a divorcee
  too; in Tanach write "widow."
- *Armalta* and *almanah* both come out "widow." Where a sugya moves from the
  Mishnah's Hebrew to the Gemara's Aramaic, do not invent a difference in
  English; they are one word across two languages.
- Yeshayahu 13:22: render "their palaces," not "their widows," and footnote Ibn
  Ezra's "emptied palaces" if the drash on the word matters.
- Keep *alman* as "widower." English has the word, and dropping it hides that
  Hebrew marks the masculine at all, which is precisely the Chasam Sofer's
  question.
- Where a source expounds *al manah*, do not translate the compound into
  running English ("no portion"); quote the Hebrew and explain, or the derashah
  reads as an etymology, which the Chasam Sofer expressly says his is not.
