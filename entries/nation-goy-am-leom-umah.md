# Nation: goy / am / leom / umah

## Bottom line

*Goy* is the loose word: a **nation** in the sense of a mass of people with no
one king over them. *Am* is the tight word: a **people** united under a single
king, leader, or under Hashem, and, unlike *goy*, it regularly
takes a possessive (*ami*, "My people"). *Leom* is a **nation** bound by a
shared religion, so it can cover more than one kingdom (Malbim). *Umah* stands with
*leom* against *goy* as a nation that does have a crown (Rabbi Yoel Ibn Shuaib). On Heidenheim's
approach it follows that every *am* is also a *goy*, but not every *goy* is an
*am*. Colloquially *goy* is
"gentile," but it literally means nation, and in Tanach it is applied to Yisrael
too.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

| עם | am | people, nation | united under one king or under Hashem (Heidenheim, Malbim); like-minded and acting alike (Wertheimer); takes the possessive (Mecklenburg) |
| לאום | leom | nation, kingdom | joined by common religion, may span kingdoms (Malbim); a clan within a nation (Edel) |
| אומה | umah | nation | a nation that is under one king, against goy which is not (Ibn Shuaib) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which word carries the shared character.** Malbim, Heidenheim, and Wertheimer
all make *am* the word of shared allegiance or shared conduct, and Malbim and
Heidenheim make *goy* the mere aggregate. The Vilna Gaon (*Biur HaGra* to
Yeshayahu 1:4) reverses it: *goy* denotes people who share a common manner, *am*
a mass of individuals.

**Whether goy can name one person.** Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi (to Bereishis
20:4): *goy* is only ever a nation, never an individual. Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Melech (*Michlal Yofi* there) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) in the name of
Rabbeinu Yonah: it can name an individual. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg proves
from Shemos 21:8 that both *am* and *goy* can point to any single member of the
group, which is the ground of the everyday usage.

**What leom is.** Malbim (on the Midrash): a religious grouping, which can be
wider than *am*. Wertheimer: either a common king (so Rashi and Targum to Bereishis 25:23
render it "kingdom") or a common religion. Edel: a family or tribe *inside* a
nation, probably tied to *em* and Aramaic *imma*, mother, which is narrower than either.

## Distinctions

### goy (גוי)
Malbim ties it to *gviah*, a body, so at its crudest a *goy* is a collection of
bodies joined into one unit. He lists four things that can do the joining:
living in one place, speaking one language, descending from one forefather, or
simply agreeing to a political union. None of these is a shared purpose, so the
bond is a marriage of convenience. Because the word leans on numbers, Tanach
occasionally calls Yisrael a *goy* precisely when it wants the multitude in
view (Malbim). Rabbi Yoel Ibn Shuaib: the Pelishtim, ruled by five lords
(Yehoshua 13:3), are an example of a *goy*.

### am (עם)
Heidenheim: a group united by one leader, king, or God. Malbim: a smaller, more
specific unit than *goy*, and bound up with sovereignty, which is why it is
almost the standing word for Yisrael. Wertheimer: like-minded people who act
alike, which is why the day of *Kabbalas HaTorah* makes Yisrael an *am*
(Devarim 27:9) and why a swarm of locusts is called an *am* (Yoel 2:2) though
locusts have no king (Mishlei 30:27). Mecklenburg and Malbim: Tanach almost never conjugates
*goy* in the possessive, but does conjugate *am*, *ami*, *amcha*, *amo*, because
a loose confederation cannot be attributed to one king while an *am* by
definition is ruled by one; with Yisrael the possessor is almost always
Hashem. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim, cited approvingly by Heidenheim: *am* is spelled like *im*, "with",
which says that the bond among its members is the closer one.

### leom (לאום) and umah (אומה)
Rashi (to Tehillim 2:1) in the name of Rabbi Menachem Ibn Saruk treats
*leomim*, *umim*, and *goyim* as bearing very close meanings; Heidenheim infers
from the wording that they are near synonyms, not identical. Ibn Shuaib sets
*umah* and *leom* together as the nation under one king, against *goy*. Malbim,
on *Bereishis Rabbah* 63, makes *leom* a religious body, which can be wider
than *am* when several kingdoms hold one faith. Edel takes *leom* or *om*, the
form some later poets use, as a family or tribe within a nation, probably related
to *em* and *imma*.

## Rendering guidance

- Bereishis 25:23 stacks all three: *goyim*, *leumim*, *leom*. Do not let
  "nation" absorb them. A workable rendering keeps "two nations" for *goyim*,
  "two peoples" or "two kingdoms" for *leumim*, and "one nation" for *leom*,
  with a note that the Hebrew shifts words.
- Trap: English "nation" covers *goy*, *am*, *leom*, and *umah* alike. Where
  the drashah turns on the difference (as it does on this pasuk), reserve
  "nation" for *goy*, "people" for *am*, and "kingdom" for *leom*.
- Never render *goy* as "gentile" inside a pasuk. The sense is nation; the
  gentile sense is a later usage that Mecklenburg's proof from Shemos 21:8
  accounts for, and it reads as an anachronism in Chumash.
- *Am Yisrael* is "the Nation of Israel" or "the Jewish People", not "the
  People of Israel" if that would suggest a geography rather than a body under
  Hashem.
