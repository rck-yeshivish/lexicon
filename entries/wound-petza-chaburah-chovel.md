# Wound: petza / chaburah (chovel)

## Bottom line

*Petza* is an **open wound**, a **laceration** that breaks the skin and bleeds;
*chaburah* is a **bruise**, blood pooled under skin that stayed whole (Rashi to
Shemos 21:25, and the Gra). *Chovel* is the Rabbinic verb, **to injure**. The
pair stands in one pasuk four times (Bereishis 4:23; Shemos 21:25; Yeshayahu
1:6; Mishlei 20:30), so rendering both "wound" erases the distinction the Torah
drew. Rav Saadia Gaon and the Rashbam divide the pair on other lines entirely.

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Rashi and the Gra:** *petza* is the wound that bleeds out through an opening in
the skin; *chaburah* is the wound where blood collects underneath and does not
emerge, showing as a red mark (Rashi to Shemos 21:25; Gra to Yeshayahu 1:6 and
Mishlei 20:30). The Bottom line follows this, both as the standard reading and
because the etymology of *petza* supports it.

**Rav Saadia Gaon,** cited by Ibn Ezra (long commentary to Shemos 21:25): the
reverse of what one would expect. *Petza* is a **broken bone**, *chaburah* the
**bleeding wound**. Ibn Ezra repeats it in his short commentary and to Yeshayahu
1:6, adding that *chaburah* means "attachment" in the sense of fluid attached to
the wound, pus and the signs of infection (compare Rashi to Iyov 9:17).

**Rashbam** (to Shemos 21:25): both are open wounds and the difference is
severity. *Petza* is the graver laceration, "the strike of a sword"; *chaburah*
the slighter, "with his finger or something insubstantial."

**Rashi against himself.** To I Melachim 20:37 he appears to equate the two, and
his definitions of *petza* shift: sword or arrow (Bereishis 4:23), sword or knife
(Yevamos 75a), any weapon (Shir HaShirim 5:7), sword (Sanhedrin 37b, Avodah Zarah
28a). The thread is something sharp, which is why "open wound" holds throughout.

**The root of chaburah.** Two opposite accounts. Either from ח-ב-ר as
"connection," the blood gathering beneath the skin (Malbim to Yeshayahu 1:6;
Wertheimer), the sense that also yields *chibbur*, *chaver* and *chover*; or the
same root in its opposite sense, "splitting," the body split open and no longer
held closed. The second fits Rav Saadia Gaon and the Rashbam, not Rashi.

## Distinctions

### petza (פצע)
Root technically פ-צ-ע, but generally derived from פ-צ-ה, *potzeh*, "to open," on
the interchange of ayin and hei: the ground opened for Kayin's burial (Bereishis
4:11) and to swallow Korach's men (Devarim 11:6). Malbim, Wertheimer, Rabbi Moshe
Ashkenazi-Tedeschi (*Otzar Nirdafim* §27) and Rav Hirsch all take it so, and Rav
Hirsch sharpens it: both are a *forced* opening, the ground having no more choice
than the man left with a *petza*. Ibn Janach and Radak define *petza* with
*bokea*, "breaking open." Rav Hirsch elsewhere (to Shemos 18:21) ties *petza* to
*betza*, "bribe," on the interchange of pei and beis: each gains the advantage in
a zero-sum encounter, the bribe in court and the wound in combat.

### chaburah (חבורה)
Rashi: blood collected beneath an unbroken surface. Yirmiyahu 13:23 asks whether
a leopard can change its *chavarburos*, which Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak
read as a declension of *chaburah*, a discoloration of the skin. Rambam
(*Hilchos Shabbos* 8:7) sets *chovel* and *chaburah* side by side, the bruise as
the product of the injuring.

### chovel (חובל)
Absent from Tanach, standard in Rabbinic Hebrew: one who injures another and owes
the five payments (Bava Kamma 8:1), and the melachah of wounding any living
creature that has skin on Shabbos (Shabbos 14:1). The root ח-ב-ל carries
plotting, rope, lot, pledge, a group of people, pain, and damage; the last two
are the likeliest parents here, and Rabbinic *chaval*, "a pity," belongs with
them. Rabbi Yaakov Berger of Kiryat Sefer (*Milon Leshon Mikra*, unpublished)
holds ח-ב-ל and ח-ב-ר are one root, on the interchange of lamed and reish, noting
Rashi to Yeshayahu 1:7 and Shir HaShirim 5:7; he adds candidly that he knows no
earlier authority who says so.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "wound" absorbs both. Where they stand together, render "a
  laceration for a laceration, a bruise for a bruise" (Shemos 21:25).
- Lemech's boast (Bereishis 4:23) puts the pair in parallel stichs; keep two
  distinct English nouns so the parallelism survives.
- Where a source follows Rav Saadia Gaon rather than Rashi, *petza* must be
  "fracture" and *chaburah* the bleeding wound. Check which view the sefer in
  hand is working with before translating the pasuk.
- *Chovel* in a halachic passage is "one who injures" or "wounding," not
  "bruising"; the bruise is the *chaburah* that results.
