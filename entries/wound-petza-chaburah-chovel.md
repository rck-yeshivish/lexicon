# Wound: petza / chaburah (chovel)

## Bottom line

On Rashi's reading (to Shemos 21:25), which the Vilna Gaon shares, *petza* is
an **open wound**, a **laceration** that breaks the skin and bleeds, and
*chaburah* a **bruise**, blood pooled under skin that stayed whole. Rav Saadia
Gaon, cited by Ibn Ezra, makes *petza* a broken bone and *chaburah* the
bleeding wound; the Rashbam seems to make both open wounds, *petza* the graver.
*Chovel* is the Rabbinic verb, **to injure**. The pair stands together in
Bereishis 4:23, Shemos 21:25, Yeshayahu 1:6 and Mishlei 20:30.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Rashi and the Gra:** *petza* is the wound that bleeds through an opening in the
skin; *chaburah* is the wound where blood collects underneath and does not
emerge, showing as a red mark (Rashi to Shemos 21:25). The Vilna Gaon (to
Yeshayahu 1:6, Mishlei 20:30) similarly: *petza* is an open wound or bleeding
laceration, *chaburah* a bruise. Rabbi Klein notes that the etymologies of
*petza* below from "opening" and "cutting" all account for this reading.

**Rav Saadia Gaon** (882 to 942), cited by Ibn Ezra after Rashi's view (long
commentary to Shemos 21:25): *petza* is a **broken bone**, *chaburah* a
**bleeding wound**. Ibn Ezra repeats this in his short commentary to Shemos
21:25 and to Yeshayahu 1:6, adding that *chaburah* means "attachment": a wound
with fluid attached to it, pus and other signs of infection (Rashi to Iyov 9:17
seems to explain *petza* the same way). Dr. Yoel Florsheim argues that Rashi's
repeated, varying definitions of *petza* (below) were meant to forestall Rav
Saadia Gaon's reading, making clear that *petza* is not a broken bone but some
kind of cut.

**Rashbam** (to Shemos 21:25) seems to take another approach: both are open
wounds and the difference is severity. *Petza* is the graver laceration, "the
strike of a sword"; *chaburah* the slighter cut, "with his finger or something
insubstantial," like a scratch.

**Rashi against himself.** To Melachim I 20:37 he seems to equate the two, and
his definitions of *petza* vary: sword or arrow (Bereishis 4:23), sword or knife
(Yevamos 75a), any weapon (Shir HaShirim 5:7), sword (Sanhedrin 37b, Avodah
Zarah 28a). It seems pretty clear that the thread is something sharp or
piercing, which is why Rashi defines *petza* as an open wound.

**The root of chaburah.** In light of Rashi, Malbim (to Yeshayahu 1:6) and Rabbi
Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935) derive it from *chibbur*, "connection,"
the blood pooling beneath the skin; Rabbi Klein places it with the other
offshoots of ח-ב-ר, *chibbur*, *chaver* and *chover*, all sharing the sense of
joining together. Alternatively, he suggests, since a Hebrew root can sometimes
carry both an idea and its opposite, ח-ב-ר may here mean "splitting," the body
split open and no longer held closed; he notes this does not fit Rashi but can
fit other commentators.

## Distinctions

### petza (פצע)
Root technically פ-צ-ע, but often derived from פ-צ-ה, *potzeh*, "to open," on
the interchange of ayin and hei: the ground opened for Kayin's burial (Bereishis
4:11) and to swallow Korach's men (Devarim 11:6). Malbim, Rabbi Wertheimer,
Rabbi Moshe Ashkenazi-Tedeschi (*Otzar Nirdafim* §27) and Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 4:11, Shemos 21:25) take it so, and Rav Hirsch notes that both are a
*forced* opening, the ground having no more choice than the man left with a
*petza*. The maskilic scholar Yitzchak Ber Levinsohn (1788 to 1860), in
*Shorashei Levanon*, relates *petza* to *botzea*, "cut open," as bread is cut in
Rabbinic Hebrew, on the interchange of the labials pei and beis. Ibn Janach and
Radak define *petza* with *bokea*, "breaking open"; though they do not say so,
Rabbi Klein suggests they may have seen the two as related, through pei and
beis and through the neighbouring tzadi and kuf. Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche
(1920 to 2016), the late Chief Rabbi of Netanya, connects *petza* with ב-ז-ע,
*baza*, "to cut," in Aramaic and Arabic. Rav Hirsch elsewhere (to Shemos 18:21)
ties *petza* to *betza*, "bribe," on the interchange of pei and beis: each gains
the advantage in a zero-sum encounter, the bribe in court and the wound in
combat.

### chaburah (חבורה)
Rashi: blood collected beneath an unbroken surface. Yirmiyahu 13:23 asks whether
a leopard can change its *chavarburos*, which Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak
read as a declension of *chaburah*, a discoloration of the skin. Rambam
(*Hilchos Shabbos* 8:7) sets *chovel* and *chaburah* side by side, as though the
bruise were the product of the injuring.

### chovel (חובל)
Absent from Tanach, standard in Rabbinic Hebrew: one who injures another and owes
the five payments (Bava Kamma 8:1), and the melachah of wounding any living
creature that has skin on Shabbos (Shabbos 14:1). The root ח-ב-ל carries
plotting, rope, lot, pledge, a group of people, pain, and damage. Which of these
is the direct parent of *chovel* is unclear, but pain and damage appear the
likeliest, and Rabbinic *chaval*, "a pity," and Modern Hebrew *mechabel*,
"terrorist," probably belong with them. Alternatively, Rabbi Klein suggests,
*chovel* may relate to "a group of people," as *chaburah* is a grouping of
blood: the one who wounds causes the blood to group beneath the skin. Rabbi
Yaakov Berger of Kiryat Sefer (*Milon Leshon Mikra*, unpublished) holds that the
roots of *chovel* and *chaburah* are one, on the interchange of lamed and reish,
as Rashi also implies when he explains *chaburah* with a cognate of *chovel*
(Yeshayahu 1:6; Shir HaShirim 5:7). Rabbi Berger cautiously notes that he knows
of no earlier authority who says so; Rabbi Klein found that the Hungarian
scholar Rabbi Baruch Schönfeld (1778 to 1852) suggested this very connection in a
note in *Bikkurei Ittim* (vol. 9, pp. 70-71).

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "wound" absorbs both. Where they stand together, on Rashi's
  reading render "a laceration for a laceration, a bruise for a bruise" (Shemos
  21:25), or keep the Hebrew beside "wound" as the column does.
- Lemech's boast (Bereishis 4:23) puts the pair in parallel stichs; keep two
  distinct English nouns so the parallelism survives.
- Where a source follows Rav Saadia Gaon rather than Rashi, *petza* is a
  "fracture" and *chaburah* the bleeding wound; where it follows the Rashbam,
  both are cuts, the graver and the slighter. Check which view the commentary in
  hand is working with before translating the pasuk.
- *Chovel* in a halachic passage is "one who injures" or "wounding," not
  "bruising"; the bruise is the *chaburah* that results.
