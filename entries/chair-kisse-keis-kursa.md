# Chair: kisse / kisseh / keis / kursa (kathedra)

## Bottom line

*Kisse* (כסא) is **throne**: not a neutral chair but a seat that marks rank, which is
why it is the word for Hashem's *Kisse HaKavod*. *Kisseh* (כסה) and *keis* (כס) are the
same word in two rarer spellings, and in the shortened *keis* of Shemos 17:16 the
missing letter is the whole point of the derashah. *Kursa* is simply the Aramaic of
*kisse*, so a Targum or Gemara *kursa* is still **throne**; the sense **armchair**
belongs to Modern Hebrew. *Kathedra* is the
Greek word behind English **chair** and **cathedral**, and it turns up four times in the
Mishnah.

## Where the authorities disagree

**The root of kisse.**

- The classical lexicographers take it as the triliteral כ-ס-א.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral כ-ס, "covering," which for
  him also yields *kisui*, *michaseh*, *kesus* (clothing), *kos* (a covered
  cup), *kis* (a pocket), *meches* (a tax paid into the king's pocket),
  *nechasim* and *michsah*.

**What the "covering" covers.**

- Rabbi Avraham Abulafia: *Kisse HaKavod* means not something enveloped
  (*mukaf*) but something that envelopes (*makif*), Hashem's all-encompassing
  Providence.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 41:40) reads it the other way: *kisse* is kin to
  *kisui*, *katzeh* and *miktzoa*, all about cutting off, so a throne raises a
  man above the rest and puts him out of reach. Hirsch gets there through the
  interchange of kaf with kuf, samech with tzadi, and aleph with hey and ayin.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche similarly explains that the king is set apart,
  covered and hidden away.

**Keis Yah: one word or two.** An Amoraic dispute in Pesachim 117a; *Minchas
Shai* (to Shemos 17:16) records that the later Masoretes divided the same way.

- Some early manuscripts, the Leningrad Codex and Sassoon 507, write two words,
  which is the accepted practice; the Aleppo Codex and Sassoon 1053 write one.
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) explains the missing aleph by the one-word
  reading.
- the Midrash keeps the absence as a statement in its own right.

On either reading the phrase means "the throne of Yah."

**The reish in kursa.** Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein put the question to linguists
and got two answers.

- The first, which he says seems to be the case: *kisse* without the reish is
  the older form and the Aramaic with it a later development, or corruption. The
  comparative evidence lines up neatly, on this view: Dr. Chaim Tawil relates
  Hebrew *kisse* to the Akkadian *kussu*, both of them very early Semitic and
  neither carrying a reish, while the Arabic cognate *kursiyy* does carry it,
  Arabic being later and, as Rabbi Ernest Klein notes, having probably borrowed
  the word from Aramaic. The reish's insertion the experts explained as
  dissimilation, and Rabbi Ernest Klein explains the rhotic as inserted (by
  epenthesis) to stand in for a doubled consonant, here the doubled samech
  marked by the dagesh.
- Other linguists hold that the rhotic form *kursa* may instead be the more
  original, Hebrew having dropped the reish, in which case the dagesh in the
  samech marks where the reish went, and the phenomenon is assimilation.

The meaning of the word does not turn on it.

## Distinctions

### kisse (כסא)
The workhorse: 135 occurrences, always with a final aleph. The haftarah for
Shabbos Rosh Chodesh opens: "So says Hashem: the heavens are My throne
[*kisse*], and the earth is My footstool. Which is the house that has been built
for Me? And which is the place of My resting?" (Yeshayahu 66:1).
- Pappenheim: primarily a chair with a cover or overhead canopy, the canopy
  itself displaying the honor of the one seated, especially a king; his family
  of words includes *michsah*, a central fund such as the one the members of a
  korban Pesach pool their money into (Shemos 12:4).
- Rambam (*Moreh Nevuchim* 1:9): a *kisse* implies importance and power,
  presumably, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says, because ordinary people in ancient
  times had no chairs but sat on whatever flat surface was at hand; the Beis
  HaMikdash is called the seat of Hashem's glory because prophecy issues from
  there.
- Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Shklov, if Rabbi Klein understood him correctly,
  similarly connects *kisse* to *ani*, kaf and samech following yod and nun, so
  that the throne connotes the Selfhood of Hashem rather than what merely
  emanates from Him; he bolsters it by noting that the Aramaic *kursa* shares
  its three letters with *keres*, the Aramaic of *beten*, so that as the *keres*
  is integral to a man, *kisse* names the "Person" of Hashem.

### kisseh (כסה) and keis (כס)
*Kisseh* three times: twice at Melachim I 10:19, of Shlomo HaMelech's throne, and Iyov
26:9. Radak identifies it with *kisse* and gives no account of the hey; Rav Hirsch (to
Tehillim 81:4) uses the spelling to read *keseh*, the hidden festival, plainly because
the moon of Rosh Hashanah is not yet visible and deeper because Hashem sits that day on
His throne as King. *Keis* once, Shemos 17:16, in the oath of perpetual war against
Amalek, where both the Divine Name and the word for throne appear shortened: while
Amalek's descendants remain, Hashem's Name and throne are, so to speak, incomplete
(Tanchuma *Ki Seitzei* §11, cited by Rashi).

### kursa (כורסא) and kathedra (קתדרא)
*Kursa* three times, all in the Aramaic of Daniel (5:20, 7:9). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur
(*Meturgaman*): Targum renders every Hebrew *kisse* as *kursa*; Rashi (to Daniel 7:9,
Yevamos 118b, Kesuvos 75a, Gittin 35a) glosses *kursa* as *kisse*. Modern Hebrew has
narrowed it to an armchair or recliner, no guide to the Aramaic. English "chair" comes
from the French *chaiere*, "seat," "throne," which first meant a bishop's throne, and
that from the Latin *cathedra* and the Greek *kathedra*, which are also the etymons of
"cathedral," the church where the bishop's throne stands. That Greek *kathedra* appears
four times in Mishnah: at Kesuvos 5:5, where a woman who brought four maidservants into
her marriage need do no housework but may simply sit on her *kathedra*, and at Keilim
4:3, 22:3 and 24:2, on vessels and their susceptibility to tumah.

## Rendering guidance

- Default *kisse* to **throne** wherever a king, a judge or Hashem is seated, and to
  **seat** or **chair** only where the context is plainly domestic. English "chair" is
  the trap: it absorbs *kisse* and *kathedra* alike and flattens the rank *kisse*
  carries.
- Shemos 17:16: render "the throne of Yah" but keep the Hebrew כס visible in a bracket
  or note, or the derashah on the missing aleph has nothing to stand on. The same holds
  at Tehillim 81:4, where the wordplay is *keseh*/*kisse*.
- A Targum or Gemara *kursa* facing a Hebrew *kisse* is not a second word; render both
  **throne** and do not manufacture a distinction.
- Pappenheim's family (*kisui*, *kesus*, *kos*, *kis*, *meches*) are cousins of *kisse*,
  not synonyms; translate each by its own sense and keep the covering idea for a note.
