# Compass directions: tzafon / darom (negev, teiman) / mizrach (kedem) / maarav (yam)

## Bottom line

Each direction has three Hebrew names, and each name says *why*: **east** is
*mizrach* (where the sun shines out), *kedem* (the sun's first, forward
position), or *panim* (facewards); **west** is *yam* (seaward, toward the
Mediterranean), *maarav* (where shadows blur), or *acharon* (the sun's last
place); **north** is *tzafon* (on one explanation, hidden from the sun), *smol*
(left), or the Aramaic *estan*; **south** is *darom* (the sun's daytime path),
*negev* (the dry country), or *teiman*/*yamin* (right). Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh
notes, though, that Tanach never actually uses *semol* for the north as it uses
*yamin* for the south. Bereishis 28:14 names the four directions *yam*, *kedem*,
*tzafon* and *negev*; Devarim 3:27 names them *yam*, *tzafon*, *mizrach* and
*teiman*. Rabbi Klein explains the doublets this way: the several names each fix
on a different aspect of the same direction, and are generally based either on
the movements of the sun or on certain topographical features of Eretz Yisrael.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether smol names the north.**

- Rabbi Klein counts *smol* among the names for north, from an east-facing
  stance, as *yamin* and *teiman* are south.
- Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 24:49): if *yamin* and
  *teiman* mean right and south, *semol* should mean north, yet Tanach never
  uses it so, as it uses *yamin* for the south.

## Distinctions

### East: mizrach (מזרח), kedem (קדם), panim (פנים)
*Mizrach* is from *zarach*, to shine: the quarter the sun first shines from.
*Kedem* is from *kodem*, early or forward, and points to the fact that the
rising sun first shines from the east. *Panim*, literally "facewards," because
when Adam was created the front of his body faced eastward, which makes east
the frame of reference for *smol* and *yamin* (see the north and south entries
below).

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo* s.v. קד): the east is sometimes marked
twice, *lif'as kedmah mizrachah* (Shemos 27:13), *vehachonim kedmah mizrachah*
(Bamidbar 2:3), because there is north-east, south-east and east-east, the
middle east; *kedmah mizrachah* means due east, leaning neither north nor south.
Rabbi Klein adds that the doubled form is used only for the east because it is
the most esteemed of the four directions.

Rashi (to *Bava Basra* 25a): the east is called *orient*, from *ohr*, light,
since the light comes from there in the morning (see *Chavos Yair* §219).

### West: yam (ים), maarav (מערב), acharon (אחרון)
Rabbeinu Bachya explains that *yam* literally means sea, and serves for west
because the Mediterranean lies at the west side of Eretz Yisrael. The west is
also called *maarav*, from *me'urav*,
mixed: as the sun sets, the shapes of the shadows become mixed up. Thirdly,
*acharon*, literally "last," because it is the last place along the horizon
where the sun is found before setting in the evening.

### North: tzafon (צפון), smol (שמאל), estan (אסתן)
The commentators offer several explanations of *tzafon*.
- Some compare it to *tzafun*, hidden, because in the northern parts of the
  world (the Arctic zone) the sun's influence is hidden, the cold there
  obscuring its warmth; or, alternatively, because the sun generally remains
  south of the celestial equator, so the north side is "hidden" from the sun's
  presence.
- Others claim it is related to *tzifiyah*, gazing, since one can fix north by
  gazing heavenward and following the northern star.
- Another approach argues that *tzafon* refers to Mount Tzafon, a mountain
  mentioned in Tanach which lies due north of Eretz Yisrael and which scholars
  identify as Jebel Aqra, on the border between modern-day Turkey and Syria.

*Smol*, left, follows from facing east. The Gemara sometimes uses the Aramaic
*estan* for north (e.g. Kesubos 23a); Rabbeinu Bachya (1255-1340) proposes that
it may be related to the Aramaic *asuta*, health, alluding to certain healing
properties of the north wind (see Yevamos 72a). Some people have a custom of
exclaiming *Asuta!* on hearing another sneeze, to bless him with good health.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 24:49): if *yamin* and
*teiman* mean right and south, *semol* should mean north, yet Tanach never uses
*semol* for the north as it uses *yamin* for the south. *Tzafon* is tied to
Hashem's attribute of *gevurah*, His holding back His bounty and so hiding it,
the opposite of *chessed*, and so it is associated with the angel of Esav,
Samael, a near-homograph of *semol*, sin and samech interchanging. He also
speculates that *semol* and Samael are related to *semel*, an image, without
explaining how.

### South: darom (דרום), negev (נגב), teiman (תימן) / yamin (ימין)
The commentaries explain *darom* as a portmanteau of *derech yom*, the path of
the day, because the sun generally remains in the southern hemisphere of the
celestial map during the day. *Negev* is literally "dry," because the southern
part of Eretz Yisrael is a dry and arid desert-land, so like *yam* it is a name
taken from the land itself. *Teiman* and *yamin* are "right," again from an
east-facing stance. Rabbi Klein points to a geographical anomaly: the Hebrew
words for left and right turn up in place-names near the Gulf of Aden between
Africa and Arabia, so that one travelling northwards there approaches Yemen
(related to *yemin*) on his right and Somalia (related to *smol*) on his left.

## Rendering guidance

- In ordinary narrative render the compass point: *tzafon* **north**, *negev*
  **south**, *kedem* **east**, *yam* **west**. Do not translate *yam* as "sea"
  in a direction list or *negev* as "the Negev" where the pasuk means south.
- Where a passage plays on the sense, gloss once and move on: *tzafon* as
  **the hidden side**, *negev* as **the dry country**, *kedem* as **the
  forward side**.
- Trap: English "east" absorbs *mizrach*, *kedem* and *panim*, and "south"
  absorbs *darom*, *negev* and *teiman*. When two of them stand in one pasuk,
  or when a source contrasts *kedem* with *mizrach*, keep them apart by
  glossing the second: "east (*kedem*, the forward side)."
- Second trap: *kedem* carries both space and time.
  Check whether the pasuk means eastward or aforetime before settling on
  either.
- *Smol* and *yamin* are only north and south when the speaker faces east.
  Where the orientation is a person's own body, they stay left and right.
