# Sibling-in-law: yavam / yevamah / giss

## Bottom line

*Yavam* and *yevamah* belong to *yibbum*: **the levir** and **the
widow**, the **brother-in-law** and **sister-in-law** of the mitzvah. *Giss* is the Mishnaic word, a man's word for his wife's brother and, on
some views, his sister's husband too. On the view of Rabbi Mecklenburg and
others, Biblical Hebrew has no general word for a sibling-in-law, only *eishes achiv*. English "brother-in-law" covers four
relations at once, a woman's husband's brother, a woman's sister's husband, a man's wife's brother and a man's sister's husband.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why the levir is a *yavam*.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814)
traces it to the biliteral ב-מ, whose core sense shows in *bam*, "in them" or
"through them," which he further breaks down into two monoliteral roots, the beis
of "in, through" and the mem of "them," so that the two-letter root denotes the
conglomeration and meeting point of several parties. *Bamah*, the private altar,
he derives from it as a site where many gathered in communal worship; *bamah*
also means "high place," which many commentators take to be the
word's original meaning, since such gatherings were usually held at elevated
spots to be seen. Pappenheim then sets *yavam* against the other in-law terms: a
spouse has only one father-in-law and one mother-in-law, so *choten* / *chosenes*
and *cham* / *chamos* are always limited to one person, whereas marriage can
bring any number of siblings-in-law at once, a kind of mass gathering of family.
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865) cites Pappenheim but ultimately prefers
another approach, connecting *yibbum* to *bamah* through the traditional sense of
a high place: *yibbum* lifts up a downtrodden childless widow by marrying her and
making her feel important again, or, he alternatively suggests, elevates the
deceased brother's neshamah by establishing a family in his name.

**What the verb *yibbum* does to her status.**
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: at the husband's death she was no longer actively
  considered a sister-in-law to the surviving brothers, and one of them marrying
  her brings her back into the family as a *yevamah* to the rest, which is why
  the mitzvah is called *yibbum*.
- Rabbi Wolf Heidenheim (1757-1832) takes the exact opposite approach: *yibbum*
  does not reinstate a lost status, particularly since halachically she never
  stopped being a sister-in-law to her husband's brothers; rather it makes her a
  wife instead of a *yevamah* and so eliminates that status as regards him, on
  the pattern of *shoresh*, a root, against *l'sharesh*, to uproot.
- Ibn Ezra (Bereishis 38:8) seems to explain, alternatively, that *yibbum*
  refers not to the *yevamah* but to the *yavam*: it is the quintessential act
  of brother-in-lawing, the *yavam* acting as brothers-in-law should who take
  charge of a widowed sister-in-law and bring her into their own family (see
  also *Meshech Chochmah* to Devarim 25:5).

**Whether *yavam* is a kinship word at all.** Rabbi Mecklenburg and others, cited
by Rabbi Heidenheim: Biblical Hebrew has no special word for brother-in-law or
sister-in-law, the latter being *eishes achiv*, "his brother's wife" (Bereishis
38:8-9; Vayikra 18:16, 20:21), and only later Rabbinic Hebrew introduced *giss* /
*gissa*. On their view *yavam* / *yevamah* name specifically the parties to the
mitzvah of *yibbum* and are not general kinship terms. Rabbi Heidenheim himself
cites Ibn Ezra and HaBachur as clearly disagreeing, taking *yavam* / *yevamah* as
kinship terms even outside a *yibbum* context.

**What *giss* covers.**
- Rabbi Wolf Heidenheim: only a spouse's sibling or that sibling's spouse, so
  two men are *gissim* only if they married two sisters, and a man's sister's
  husband is not his *giss* even though English would call him a brother-in-law.
  His proof is the Mishnah (*Sanhedrin* 3:4), which lists the relatives
  disqualified from testifying and mentions *baal achoso*, his sister's husband,
  separately from *gisso*.
- Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi, a thirteenth-century exegete who lived in Eretz
  Yisrael, defines *giss* as a man's wife's sister's husband, implying that his
  sister's husband is not his *giss*.
- Rabbi Klein holds that Heidenheim's proof from the Mishnah is not foolproof:
  Chullin 18b has Rabbi Yochanan calling Reish Lakish *gissa gissa*, which the
  Ritva says recalls that Reish Lakish was married to Rabbi Yochanan's sister
  (Bava Metzia 84a), and on that basis the *Nimukei Yosef* (*Sanhedrin* 6a in
  the Alfasi pagination), written by a student of the Ritva, explains that a
  sister's husband can evidently still be called a *giss* despite the Mishnah's
  separate listing.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549) cuts it by the speaker: a man calls his
  wife's brother a *giss*, a woman calls her husband's brothers her *yavam*,
  even where the mitzvah of *yibbum* does not apply, as when the Midrash speaks
  of Moshe from the standpoint of his sister-in-law Elisheva, Aharon's wife, and
  the Gemara (Zevachim 102a) calls him her *yavam*; so too two women married to
  brothers call each other *yevamos* (Rus 1:15). For HaBachur both a man's
  sister's husband and his wife's brother may be called *giss*, so two men
  married to two sisters are *gissim*; he concedes that a man might call his
  brother's wife or his wife's sister *gisaso*, but holds that a borrowed rather
  than the primary sense.

**Whether *choten* can mean brother-in-law.** Ibn Janach (990-1050) says yes,
adducing it from his understanding that Chovav ben Reuel was a son of Moshe's
father-in-law Yisro/Reuel (Bamidbar 10:29), while Shoftim 4:11 describes Chovav
as Moshe's *choten*. Radak (1160-1234) disagrees and clarifies that Chovav is
another name for Yisro and Reuel was Chovav's father, so Chovav was Moshe's
father-in-law and there is no proof at all that *choten* can mean brother-in-law.
Rabbi Klein observes that both of them seem to disregard the Midrashic position
that Reuel and Chovav are both alternate names for Yisro. There is
precedent for Ibn Janach's assertion in other languages: the Akkadian cognate of
*choten* can mean both father-in-law and brother-in-law, and in Yiddish
*shvugger*, brother-in-law, and *shver*, father-in-law, ultimately derive from a
shared etymon, *swehuraz* in proto-Germanic and *swekuros* in proto-Indo-European.

## Distinctions

### yavam and yevamah (יבם, יבמה)
The starting point is the mitzvah of *yibbum*: when a married man dies childless,
one of his brothers must marry the widowed sister-in-law (Devarim 25:5). The
*yavam* is the deceased's brother and the *yevamah* the deceased's widow, the two
commanded to marry each other; the English "levirate marriage" comes from the
Latin *levir*, brother-in-law. On the Talmudic principle that a man and his wife
are considered one, Rabbi Pappenheim broadens *yavam* / *yevamah* to take in a
spouse's sibling's spouse as well, citing Rus 1:15, where Naomi calls Orpah Rus's
*yevamah* although she was Rus's husband's brother's wife; the same usage
appears in the Mishnah (*Yevamos* 15:4), where *yevamah* is a woman's
husband's brother's wife.

### giss (גיס)
Root ג-ס, which does not appear in Tanach at all; the two letters never stand
adjacent anywhere in it. In Mishnaic Hebrew the two-letter string yields various
senses, each attested at several loci, including "mixing" (among them
*Pesachim* 5:10), "army" (*Rosh Hashanah* 2:5), "thick" (*Shabbos* 4:1), and
"familiar, intimate" (*Yevamos* 4:10, *Avos* 4:7). Rabbi Yaakov Emden (1697-1776)
submits that *giss* as brother-in-law relates to the last of these, connoting
closeness and family intimacy. Rabbi Klein suggests that
all the various senses may derive from a core meaning of mixing: an army is fighters
mixed for a common cause, thickness is mass concentrated in one place, and
intimacy is two parties joined close, so that *giss* / *gissa* fits a root about
families mixing together in matrimony, which he finds reminiscent of Rabbi
Pappenheim's ב-מ.

The same root gives *gassus* or *gassus ruach*, haughtiness (*Avos* 4:7 and
Targum to Tehillim 10:2, 10:4, 76:13, 101:5, and Mishlei 16:18), the arrogant man
feeling higher and more important than others, which, Rabbi Klein notes, would make
words related to *giss* semantically close to Rabbi Mecklenburg's reading of
*yibbum* as elevation. In Talmudic Aramaic *gissa* also means "side" (see Targum
to Vayikra 3:4 and Yeshayahu 60:4, 66:12), which Rabbi Klein suggests, as a
"perhaps," may relate to the sibling-in-law standing figuratively on the other
side of the family tree.

HaBachur vocalizes the word *gass*; Rabbi Yosef Teomim-Frankel (1727-1792),
author of the *Pri Megadim*, points out that it is sometimes written with a yod
between the gimmel and the samech, which supports vocalizing the gimmel with a
chirik as is the common practice. Some versions of the Mishnah, that printed in
the Yerushalmi and the Kaufmann manuscript, spell it with an initial aleph,
*agiss*. In Modern Hebrew aleph-gimmel-samech, *agas*, is a pear, and in the
Mishnah (*Kilayim* 1:4, *Maasros* 1:3, *Uktzin* 1:6) it certainly names some sort
of fruit; ironically, *agiss* has become the accepted
Israeli spelling of the diaper brand "Huggies."

## Rendering guidance

- In a *yibbum* sugya, render *yavam* "the levir" and *yevamah* "the widow";
  "brother-in-law" will not tell a reader which relation is meant.
- Rus 1:15, Naomi to Rus of Orpah: *yevimtech* is her husband's brother's wife,
  not a widow awaiting *yibbum*. Render "your sister-in-law" and note it.
- Bereishis 38:8-9 and Vayikra 18:16, 20:21 say *eishes achiv*. Keep "his
  brother's wife"; "sister-in-law" erases the point, on Rabbi Mecklenburg's
  view, that Biblical Hebrew lacks the term.
- Trap: "brother-in-law" absorbs *yavam*, *giss*, and on Ibn Janach's reading
  *choten*. Where two appear together, as in *Sanhedrin* 3:4's list of
  disqualified relatives, spell out each relation.
