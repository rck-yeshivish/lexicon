# Betrothal: erusin / kiddushin / nissuin (hekdesh, shidduchin)

## Bottom line

*Erusin* and *kiddushin* name the same halachic act, the first of the two stages
of marriage: **betrothal**. After it she is halachically his wife and adultery
applies, though the couple may not yet live together. *Nissuin*, effected by
chuppah, is the second stage: **marriage** proper. *Shidduchin* is what English
calls **engagement**, an agreement to marry later. Modern Hebrew uses *erusin*
for engagement, but Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 22:15) already makes clear that the
Torah's *erusin* is not that. The Rabbanan Savorai (*Kiddushin* 2b) derive
*kiddushin* from *hekdesh*, since betrothal, like consecration, forbids her to
all others, and where the two words are contrasted *kiddushin* carries that
sense. Tosafos (to *Kiddushin* 2b) derive it instead from designating, not
consecrating. *Erusin*, on Rabbi Shalom Mansoura's reading, carries the sense of
binding or tying.

## Distinctions

### erusin (אירוסין)
The Biblical term (Shemos 22:15, Devarim 20:7, 22:23-27, 28:30). Chazal read
*morashah* in Devarim 33:4 as though *me'orasah*, betrothed, so that the Torah
is betrothed to Klal Yisrael as a wife to her husband (*Berachos* 57a,
*Pesachim* 49b).
- Rabbi Shalom Mansoura of Sanna: *erusin* is an expression of tying. Rabbi
  Yitzchak Ratzabi gives two ways to take that. Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein
  reads א-ר-ש as equivalent to א-ר-ז, since sin/samech and zayin interchange,
  and א-ר-ז is something packed tightly (Yechezkel 27:24; modern Hebrew
  *arizah*, a package), and Rabbi Epstein adds that א-ר-ז is related by
  metathesis to [*eizor*](belt-avnet-chagor-eizor.html#%D7%90%D7%96%D7%95%D7%A8), a tight belt. Alternatively, Rabbi Ratzabi suggests,
  Rabbi Mansoura may mean a simpler metathesis with no letter replaced:
  transpose the last two letters and get א-ס-ר, to tie or bind, the betrothed
  woman being tied to her husband and released only by a get or by his death.
- Rabbi Yitzchak Vana: *arusah* is from [*eres*](poison-eres-rosh-chamat-sam-hamaves.html#%D7%90%D7%A8%D7%A1), poison, since one who approaches
  her illicitly incurs the death penalty as from a snake or scorpion.
- Another Yemenite scholar cited by Rabbi Ratzabi: *arusah* as in *aris*, a
  sharecropper who holds a partial claim, since the betrothed husband has a
  partial claim on her (adultery applies) but not a full one (he does not yet
  inherit her).
- Rabbi Ratzabi's own two suggestions turn on the interchange of aleph and ayin:
  ע-ר-ס, to mix (as in [*arisah*](dough-arisah-batzek-issah.html#%D7%A2%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%94), kneaded dough), for the joining of man and
  wife, or *eres*, a bed.

### kiddushin (קידושין)
The term of the Mishnah, and the name of the masechta. *Kiddushin* 2b: the
Rabbanan Savorai ask why the Mishnah (*Kiddushin* 2:1) uses it rather than the
Biblical word, and answer that it is from *hekdesh*, consecrated property: he
forbids her to all others as consecration forbids all men to benefit.
- Rabbi Yitzchak Vana: the parallel to *hekdesh* is that betrothal, like
  consecration, raises the severity of the prohibition on one who transgresses
  with her.
- Rabbi Alexander Sender Shor and Rabbi Elazar Moshe Horowitz: mid'oraisa the
  betrothed couple may already live together, and only the rabbinic enactment
  forbids her to her own husband until nissuin (*Kallah* 1:1, Rashi to *Kesubos*
  8a); so only after that enactment does she resemble *hekdesh*, forbidden to
  everyone including her husband, and that is why the term is the Rabbis' and
  not the Torah's.
- The Taz (to *Even HaEzer* 34:2) holds that the two-stage structure itself
  confers a kedushah found nowhere else; Rabbi Klein offers, as his own
  extension of that, the argument that the Rabbis coined *kiddushin* because
  they were the ones who instituted the two-step framework, while from the
  Torah's perspective that added holiness does not yet exist and betrothal is
  simply *erusin*, a word implying nothing about holiness.
- Rabbi Immanuel Chai Ricci (*Hon Ashir*, introduction to *Kiddushin*): a man
  becomes holy by marrying a woman, who saves him from sin, and since every
  fence against forbidden relations is called *kedushah*, marriage is called
  *kiddushin*.

- Tosafos (to *Kiddushin* 2b) read *kiddushin* differently, from *kiddush* in
  the sense of preparing or designating (Shemos 19:10, Bamidbar 11:18);
  *kedeishah*, a harlot, is a cognate because she is set aside for a purpose
  (Rashi to Bereishis 38:21 and Devarim 23:18).
- Others take *kedeishah* as related to the Akkadian *qadistu*, "woman of
  special status," a definition that fits both a harlot and a wife, which led
  Professor Shamma Friedman of the Academy of the Hebrew Language to entertain
  it as a possible etymology for *kiddushin* itself.

Either way, these approaches hold that *kiddushin* has nothing to do with
holiness or consecration as such. Two supports: Rabbi Aharon HaLevi of Barcelona
(*Chiddushei HaRa'ah* to *Kiddushin* 2b) and Rabbi Yosef Ibn Ezra (*Atzmos
Yosef*) note that if *mekudeshes* meant consecrated, the groom would be
forbidding her to himself, which he plainly does not intend; and Rabbi Eliezer
Ashkenazi notes that consecration would call for *mukdeshes*, whereas the
formula reads *mekudeshes*, set aside for him. Tosafos (to *Kiddushin* 7b) rule
that one who betroths "half" a woman with the language of *kiddushin* effects a
full betrothal, but one who uses the language of *erusin* effects only half.
Rabbi Klein explains the ruling by what he calls their unspoken logic, the
*hekdesh* paradigm: consecrating half an animal sanctifies all of it, and
*kiddushin* is the term related to *hekdesh*.

Dr. Michael Satlow suggests that the Mishnaic *kiddushin* is a loanword from the
Greek legal term *ekdosis*, the bride's father handing the girl over to her new
husband. Rabbi Klein calls the proposal fascinating but hard to accept, since
handing the bride over is the definition of *nissuin* (*Kesubos* 4:5), not of
*kiddushin*; he allows, though, that the Rabbis may have adopted the Greek word
and slightly modified its pronunciation and sense, as they often did with foreign words.

### nissuin (נישואין)
The second stage, effected by chuppah, after which the couple live together.
*Kesubos* 4:5 defines it as the father handing the daughter over to the husband.
Only after nissuin is the rabbinic prohibition between the betrothed pair lifted.

### shidduchin (שידוכין)
The Talmudic term for what is colloquially called engagement, a couple who have
agreed to marry but have not done so.

## Rendering guidance

- *Erusin* and *kiddushin*, standing alone for the halachic act: "betrothal."
  Never "engagement," and avoid "inchoate marriage," which is heavy and obscure.
- *Nissuin*: "marriage," or "the second stage of marriage" where the two-stage
  structure is the subject. *Shidduchin*: "engagement" or "the match."
- Where a passage contrasts *erusin* with *kiddushin* (Tosafos to *Kiddushin*
  7b), English "betrothal" will absorb both. Keep the Hebrew terms and translate
  the concept: "betrothal effected in the language of *kiddushin*."
- *Mekudeshes* in the formula under the chuppah: "you are betrothed to me," or
  "designated to me," following Tosafos and the Ra'ah. "Consecrated to me" imports
  the very sense those Rishonim rule out.
- *Hekdesh* in this discussion: "consecrated property," so that the analogy to
  *kiddushin* stays visible.
- Trap: a Hebrew passage may use *kiddushin* for the act and *kedushah* for the
  holiness of the union in adjacent lines (the Taz). Do not render both by the
  same English word; the argument depends on their being felt as related but
  distinct.
