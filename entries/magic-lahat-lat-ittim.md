# Magic: lahat / lat (ittim)

## Bottom line

*Lahat* (Shemos 7:11) is the trick the practitioner works himself, **sleight of
hand** or **magic arts**; *lat* (Shemos 7:22, 8:3, 8:14) is the covert method
that works through *sheidim*, **secret arts**. Both are **incantation** if you
follow Onkelos, who treats them as one word; do not let a translation settle the
machlokes silently. *Lahat* elsewhere means **flame** (Bereishis 3:24).

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| להט | lahat | sleight of hand, magic arts | the magician acts himself (Rashi to Sanhedrin 67b); flame-quick movement (Ibn Ezra); "flame" in Bereishis 3:24 |
| לט | lat | secret arts | concealed; *sheidim* do the work (Sanhedrin 67b, Ramban) |
| אטים | ittim | soothsayers | root א-ט, "slow"; the deliberate, drawn-out performance (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Are *lahat* and *lat* one word or two?**

1. **Synonyms.** Targum Onkelos (to Shemos 7:11, 7:22) renders both as
   *lachash*, whispered incantation. Ibn Janach and Radak support this
   lexically: both derive from ל-א-ט, "covered, hidden," the middle *aleph*
   dropping in *lat* and turning to *hey* in *lahat*. Rashi (to Shemos 7:11)
   cites Onkelos, and on 7:22 glosses *lat* as something said quietly. Rabbi
   Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) likewise makes them one thing, the speed
   and dexterity of a conjuror's hand.
2. **Two distinct modalities.** The Gemara (Sanhedrin 67b) reads *lat* as the
   use of *sheidim* and *lahat* as *kishuf*. What that means is itself
   disputed: Rashi (to Sanhedrin 67b), the practitioner acts himself versus
   sending *sheidim*; the Tosafists (*Moshav Zekenim* to Shemos 7:11), sleight
   of hand versus compelling *sheidim*; Rabbi Avraham Saba (*Tzror HaMor*),
   speech-acts versus magical instruments; Ramban (to Shemos 7:11), destructive
   angels (*aish lohet*, Tehillim 104:4) versus the imperceptible *sheidim*.
3. **Two words, one trick.** Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 7:11, 7:22) rejects the
   missing-*hey* derivation, holding that a radical *hey* does not sit in the
   middle of a triliteral root. He keeps the words separate but makes them two
   aspects of one act: *lahat* the lightning speed of the hand, *lat* the
   concealment (compare Shoftim 4:21). Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi says much the same,
   and Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 7:11) is in this family: *lahat* the shiny object
   that distracts the crowd, *lat* the quick hand while they are looking away.

The Bottom line follows the Gemara, since a translator rendering a Chumash is
bound by it, and the Gemara's distinction is the one the pesukim's own shift
from *lahat* to *lat* invites. Where a passage turns on Onkelos, render both
"incantations" and say so.

**Whether any of it worked.** Rabbi Saadia Gaon (*Emunos v'Deos* 3), Rabbeinu
Chananel, Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi, and Rabbi Avraham Maimuni deny *sheidim* and the
efficacy of *kishuf*; for them, and for Ralbag, both words mean "done in
concealment," and the *chartumim* produced illusion only. The Vilna Gaon (*Biur
HaGra*, Yoreh Deah 179:13) rejects that position from these very pesukim, which
credit the *chartumim* with real results. Rabbi David HaNaggid (*Klil HaYofi*)
holds both: *lahat* the sleight of hand, *lat* the arousing of the onlooker's
imagination, *sheidim* in the Rambam's sense (*Moreh Nevuchim* 3:46).

## Distinctions

### lahat (להט)
Menachem Ibn Saruk lists it under ל-ה-ט, a root carrying two senses, "flame or
blade" and "a type of *kishuf*." The two are one for Radak, who suggests some
magic calls for a sharp sword. Rashi (to Shemos 7:11) connects the magical sense
to the *lahat hacherev hamis'hapeches* of Bereishis 3:24: the ever-turning sword
looks to an onlooker as though something had been said over it. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) works from the biliteral ט-ל, "folding": a blade
or flame seems to fold through what it cuts or burns, and *lahat* was borrowed
for the fast folding motion of a conjuror's hands.

### lat (לט)
Ramban derives it from the biliteral ל-ט, "clandestine" (Shmuel I 18:22), fitting
*sheidim*, whose presence men cannot ordinarily detect. Ibn Ezra points to
Shoftim 4:21, Yael approaching Sisera *balat*, quietly. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
gets there through "folding" too: folded cloth conceals what is inside, and
*lat* is the trick of folding a stone into a cloth and drawing out a bird.

### ittim (אטים)
Yeshayahu 19:3, among Egypt's diviners. Menachem and Radak trace it to the
biliteral א-ט and define it as a kind of *kishuf*; Ibn Janach makes it a kind of
astrologer. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reads the root as "slow movement": *ittim*
are magicians who worked deliberately slowly to heighten the drama. Rashi (to
Yeshayahu 19:3) takes it as the name of an idol instead. The same root gives
*l'at*, "slowly, gently" (Bereishis 33:14; Shmuel II 18:5; Yeshayahu 8:6; Iyov
15:11), and the lexicographers dispute whether the opening *lamed* is a radical.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 7:11 בְּלַהֲטֵיהֶם: "with their sleight of hand," or "with their magic
  arts." Shemos 7:22 and 8:3 בְּלָטֵיהֶם: "with their secret arts."
- Trap: English "magic" or "sorcery" absorbs both, and "incantation" absorbs both
  in Onkelos's direction. The Chumash deliberately switches words after the first
  plague, so one English word for both erases the shift every meforash is
  commenting on. Keep two English phrases.
- *Lahat* in Bereishis 3:24 is "the flame of the ever-turning sword," not magic;
  the magical sense is borrowed from it, not the reverse.
- *Ittim* in Yeshayahu 19:3 is a different root. Render it "soothsayers" and
  footnote Rashi's reading that it is an idol's name.
