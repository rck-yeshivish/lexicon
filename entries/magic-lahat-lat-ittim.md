# Magic: lahat / lat (ittim)

## Bottom line

On the Gemara's reading (Sanhedrin 67b) as Rashi and Ramban explain it, *lahat*
(Shemos 7:11) is the trick the practitioner works himself, **sleight of
hand** or **magic arts**; *lat* (Shemos 7:22, 8:3, 8:14) is the covert method
that works through *sheidim*, **secret arts**. For Onkelos, who treats them as
one word, both are **incantation**. *Lahat* elsewhere means **flame** (Bereishis
3:24).

The pesukim set the question. Pharaoh's *chartumim* (usually rendered
"necromancers") match Aharon's staff with their *lahat* (Shemos 7:11), then match
blood and frogs with their *lat* (7:22, 8:3), and at lice fail with their *lat*
altogether and concede "the finger of God" (8:14-15). The word changes after the
first plague, and that is what the meforshim are commenting on.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

| לט | lat | secret arts | concealed; *sheidim* do the work (Sanhedrin 67b, Ramban) |
| אטים | ittim | soothsayers | root א-ט, "slow"; the deliberate, drawn-out performance (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Are *lahat* and *lat* one word or two?**

1. **Synonyms.** Targum Onkelos (to Shemos 7:11, 7:22) renders both as
   *lachash*, whispered incantation. Ibn Janach and Radak support this
   lexically: the triliteral ל-א-ט means "covered, hidden," and its middle *aleph*
   can drop out or morph into a *vav* (Shmuel I 21:10; Yeshayahu 25:7); they suggest that it
   drops in *lat* and turns to *hei* in *lahat*. Rashi (to Shemos 7:11)
   cites Onkelos, and on 7:22 glosses *lat* as something said quietly. Rabbi
   Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) likewise makes them one thing, the speed
   and dexterity of a conjuror's hand.
2. **Two distinct modalities.** The Gemara (Sanhedrin 67b) reads *lat* as the
   use of *sheidim* and *lahat* as *kishuf*. What that means is itself
   disputed: Rashi (to Sanhedrin 67b), the practitioner acts himself versus
   sending *sheidim*; the Tosafists (*Moshav Zekenim* to Shemos 7:11), sleight
   of hand versus compelling *sheidim*; Rabbi Avraham Saba (*Tzror HaMor*),
   seemingly explaining the Gemara, magical instruments versus speech-acts; Ramban (to Shemos 7:11), destructive
   angels (*aish lohet*, Tehillim 104:4) versus the imperceptible *sheidim*.
3. **Two words, one trick.** Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 7:11, 7:22) rejects the
   missing-*hei* derivation, holding that a radical *hei* does not sit in the
   middle of a triliteral root. He keeps the words separate but makes them two
   aspects of one act: *lahat* the lightning speed of the hand, *lat* the
   concealment (compare Shoftim 4:21). Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi says much the same, and
   so *Chemdas HaChemdah* (to Shemos 7:11): *lahat* is speed, as *lahat
   hacherev*, and *lat* from *vayalet*, covering, the sorcerer's hidden devices.
   Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 7:11): *lahat* is the shiny object
   that distracts the crowd, *lat* the quick hand while they are looking away.

**Whether any of it worked.**

- Rabbi Saadia Gaon, Rabbeinu Chananel ben Chushiel, Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi, and
  Rabbi Avraham Maimuni deny *sheidim* and the efficacy of *kishuf*. On their
  reading "they also did so" does not mean the *chartumim* achieved what Moshe
  and Aharon achieved; they performed the speech-acts, but those dry actions
  created nothing new and only *seemed* to copy.
- Rav Saadia Gaon (*Emunos v'Deos*, maamar 3) and Ralbag (to Shemos 7:11, 7:22)
  accordingly take both words to mean something done in a concealed or
  clandestine way: the *chartumim* pulled off the illusion at the staff, the
  blood and the frogs, and at the lice could not manage even the illusion.
- The Vilna Gaon (*Biur HaGra*, Yoreh Deah 179:13) turns a variation of this
  question against the Maimonidean position on *kishuf*, noting that the Torah
  itself seemingly attests to the efficacy of the *chartumim*'s efforts.
- Rabbi David HaNaggid, the Rambam's great-great-grandson, in *Klil HaYofi* (to
  Shemos 7:11), answers: he assumes as an axiom that the *chartumim* did no more
  than an illusory ploy, and finds two dimensions in it, *lahat* the sleight of
  hand, and *lat* the arousing of the onlooker's imagination so that he thinks
  he sees what is not there, which relates to *sheidim* in the Rambam's sense
  (*Moreh Nevuchim* 3:46).

## Distinctions

### lahat (להט)
- Menachem Ibn Saruk lists it under ל-ה-ט, a root carrying two senses, "flame or
  blade" and "a type of *kishuf*." Radak also entertains this possibility,
  suggesting that some forms of magic call for a sharp sword. Menachem never
  lists *lat* at all in *Machberes Menachem*, so there is no telling which root
  he would have put it under.
- Rashi (to Shemos 7:11) connects the magical sense to the *lahat hacherev
  hamis'hapeches* of Bereishis 3:24: the ever-turning sword looks to an onlooker
  as though something had been said over it.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) works from the biliteral ט-ל,
  "folding": a blade or flame seems to fold through what it cuts or burns, and
  *lahat* was borrowed for the fast folding motion of a conjuror's hands.
- *Chemdas HaChemdah* (to Shemos 7:11): *lahat* is speed, as in *lahat hacherev
  hamis'hapeches*, sorcery done by quick deception of the eye; *lateihem* is
  from *vayalet*, covering, the hidden devices sorcerers keep out of sight.

### lat (לט)
- Ramban derives it from the biliteral ל-ט, "clandestine" (Shmuel I 18:22),
  fitting *sheidim*, whose presence men cannot ordinarily detect.
- Ibn Ezra points to Shoftim 4:21, Yael approaching Sisera *balat*, quietly.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gets there through "folding" too: folded cloth
  conceals what is inside, and *lat* is the trick of folding a stone into a
  cloth and drawing out a bird.
- *Igeres Shmuel*, citing Rabbi Yehudah Provencal, reads *vatavo balat* (Rus
  3:7) as her working a charm.

### ittim (אטים)
Yeshayahu 19:3, among Egypt's diviners.
- Menachem and Radak trace it to the biliteral א-ט and define it as a kind of
  *kishuf*.
- Ibn Janach makes it a kind of astrologer.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reads the root as "slow movement": *ittim* are
  magicians who worked deliberately slowly when performing their rites, to
  heighten the drama and persuade the onlookers that the acts worked.
- Rashi (to Yeshayahu 19:3) takes *ittim* as the name of an idolatrous deity
  instead, which is often identified with the Egyptian god Atum or the Sumerian
  Ittyi.

The same root gives *l'at*, "slowly, gently" (Bereishis 33:14; Shmuel II 18:5;
Yeshayahu 8:6; Iyov 15:11), and the lexicographers dispute whether the opening
*lamed* is a radical. Rabbi Klein raises א-ט as a root seemingly related to all
of this, but says plainly that he has found no one who explicitly connects *lat*
or *lahat* to it.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 7:11 בְּלַהֲטֵיהֶם: "with their sleight of hand," or "with their magic
  arts." Shemos 7:22 and 8:3 בְּלָטֵיהֶם: "with their secret arts."
- Trap: English "magic" or "sorcery" absorbs both, and "incantation" absorbs both
  in Onkelos's direction. The Chumash deliberately switches words after the first
  plague, so one English word for both erases the shift every meforash is
  commenting on. Keep two English phrases.
- *Lahat* in Bereishis 3:24 is "the flame of the ever-turning sword," not magic;
  the magical sense is borrowed from it, not the reverse.
- *Ittim* in Yeshayahu 19:3 is a different root. Render it "soothsayers" and
  footnote Rashi's reading that it is an idol's name.
