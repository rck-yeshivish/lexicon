# Explanation: peirush / beiur / hesber / pesher / pitaron

## Bottom line

*Peirush* is the plain **explanation** or **commentary**: it makes known what a
text says on its surface, its legal and practical sense (Malbim: exoteric).
*Beiur* is **elucidation**: it goes beneath the surface to bring up what is
hidden (Malbim), at length and in writing, so that the matter is clear (Shadal).
*Hesber* is **explanation of the reasoning**, the *svara*: it answers *why*,
where *beiur* answers *what* (Rabbi Reuven Subar). *Pesher* and *pitaron* are
**interpretation**, the solving of an enigma; *pitaron* in Tanach is dream
interpretation only (Radak, Malbim). A *beiur* promises more than a *peirush*
does.

## Distinctions

### peirush (פירוש)
Root פ-ר-ש (kin to *parashah*, *prishah*, *parush*, *parash*, *peresh*; and to
*perushim*, which in the Mishnah, Yadayim 4:6 to 8, names the Torah-loyal Jews
against the antinomian *Tzedokim*, and today the non-Chassidic Yerushalmim of the
Old Yishuv). Shadal: the means of making known what was unknown, kin to פ-ר-ש
with a sin, "spreading out," since new information spreads the enigmatic matter
open; so the blasphemer and the mekoshesh waited because Hashem had not yet
*porash*, made known, their sentence (Vayikra 24:12, Bamidbar 15:34). Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim: the root means "separating"; the verdict was separated out
from the possible outcomes, and *meforash*, "explicitly," is the true reading
separated from the ambiguous alternatives. On the same root he explains *parash*,
cavalry, as troops kept separate from the infantry, and *peresh*, dung, as what
is separated off from the body. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi:
clarifying in an unambiguous way, as evidence is spread out (Devarim 22:17).
Malbim (*Yair Ohr*): *peirush* reveals the exoteric, the surface facts and the
normative implications, what everyone can see once it is spread out.

### beiur (ביאור)
Root ב-א-ר, which the classical lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak)
read as two senses, wellspring (*be'er*) and elucidation (*ba'er*). Pappenheim
treats the aleph as non-essential and drops to the biliteral ב-ר, "separated,
choicest, clear" (*barah*, *bari*, *bahr* for grain parted from its chaff, *bar*
for a son parted from his mother's womb, *bor*/*boris* for the soap that
separates off filth, *birah*, *bahir*, and more); *bor*, a pit, and *be'er*, a
well, are both products of digging the earth away to separate the dirt from the
water underneath, and a *beiur* separates the correct explanation from the
mistaken ones. Shadal: *beiur* is a clear manner of *writing*,
expanding on an idea with words; the verb appears only three times in Tanach,
each about writing to clarify (*ba'er heitiv* on the stones, Devarim 27:8;
Moshe's *be'er* of the Torah, Devarim 1:5; Chavakuk's tablets, Chavakuk 2:2).
Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah*) cites Shadal's explanations of *poser*,
*beiur* and *peirush*. Tedeschi-Ashkenazi: the bes is non-essential and the core
root is א-(ו)-ר, light; one who elucidates metaphorically sheds light on the
matter, an etymology that, as the column notes, parallels English "elucidate"
itself, from Latin *lux*/*lucidus* and ultimately the proto-Indo-European *leuk*,
whence *lucid*, *translucent* and *pellucid*. Malbim: *beiur* is clarification of
the *esoteric*, an in-depth probing of the core elements that lie beneath the
surface, as against *peirush*, which is occupied with a matter's external
trappings. The column adds on its own account that *beiur* accordingly relates to
*be'er* in the sense of a wellspring, whose waters are hidden deep underground
and which is the mechanism for drawing them up into daylight. What *beiur* adds
over *peirush*, then, is depth and extent: it digs, and it writes out what it
finds. Commonly pronounced *biur*.

### hesber (הסבר)
Clearest from the Aramaic *svar*, to think or hold a position (Daniel 7:25;
Talmudic *sover*, *mistaber*, *svara*, the *Rabbanan Savora'ei* who added a layer
of logical structure to the sugyos after Ravina and Rav Ashi, the *savri* called
out before Kiddush). Two related usages: the anonymous *Masbir* whose novellae
appear in Rabbi Chaim Dov Altusky's *Chiddushei Basra*, whom yeshivish lore takes
for Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik on the strength of the acronym; and Modern
Hebrew *hasbarah*, still "elucidation," but often specifically pro-Israel
advocacy.

Similar Hebrew words might also account for the origin. The root ש-ב-ר appears
ten times in Tanach, usually for waiting, anticipating or hoping (Tehillim
145:15, 119:166, 104:27, 146:5), sometimes for planning or thinking (Esther 9:1).
Shammai's *sever panim yafos* (Avos 1:15) spells the word with an opening samech,
as a Biblical sin often becomes a rabbinic samech; per Meiri it means greeting
people so cheerfully that they think you are glad to see them. Ibn Janach and
Radak present the two senses as discrete meanings of one root, and Ibn Saruk
lists only the first; the column bridges them by observing that waiting and
hoping are done in the mind, so that all these verbs name abstract acts of
thought. Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 145:15): *sover* is a mental process that
computes separate pieces of information and joins them into one complete, logical
thought, so that one who puts his hope in Hashem concludes intellectually that He
is the One who powers all of creation; by the interchange of bes and peh Rav
Hirsch connects *sover* to *sofer*, counting, likewise a mental joining, and by
the interchange of sin/samech with tzadi to *tzover*, gathering, the verb behind
*tzibbur*.

Rabbi Reuven Subar, whose surname may or may not be related to the word, proposes
a way of splitting *hesber* from *beiur*: both relieve perplexity, but *beiur*
answers *what* is being said and *hesber* answers *why*, laying out the *svara*
behind the matter and meeting the questions it raises. This is why *ba'er heitiv*
is associated with translation (Rashi to Devarim 27:8), which renders the *what*
in a plainer register. The Aramaic *sover* also means "bear" or "lift up" (Targum
to Bereishis 13:6, Bamidbar 11:12, Yeshayahu 40:11, 63:9), so a *hesber* lifts
the idea by supplying its rational support, the give and take of *masa u'matan*.
(The column adds in a bracket that this carrying sense of *savar* may be related
to the Hebrew *sovel*, to bear a burden, reish and lamed interchanging.)

### pesher (פשר) and pitaron (פתרון)
Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*), reasoning only from where inflections of *pitaron*
actually appear, concludes that in Tanach the word means the interpretation *of a
dream* and nothing else; Malbim (*Yair Ohr*) makes the same point and uses it to
separate *pitaron* from *peirush* and *beiur*, which interpret other things, like
enigmatic texts. The root פ-ת-ר runs fourteen times through Bereishis 40 to 41
and appears nowhere else in Tanach. Berachos 55b keeps the sense, with its
twenty-four *posrei chalomos* in Yerushalayim. The Gemara does stretch *poser* to
reinterpreting a teaching that has nothing to do with dreams (Eruvin 32b, Kesubos
107b), and Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) calls a word's definition
its *pitaron*, a usage taken up by his interlocutor Donash Ibn Labrat and later
by Rashi and his grandsons Rashbam and Rabbeinu Tam; in Modern Hebrew a *pitaron*
is a solution to anything at all.

Chazal even read the place-name Pesor as dream interpretation. Balak sent
messengers to Pesor for Bilam (Bamidbar 22:5), and the Midrash (*Bamidbar Rabbah*
20:7, *Tanchuma Balak* 4, see Rashi there) offers two readings: either Pesor, or
Pesorah, was the name of Bilam's town in Aram, or it points to his original trade
as a professional dream interpreter (*poser*) before he turned to sorcery. The
Maharal (*Gur Aryeh* to Bamidbar 22:5) synthesizes the two: the town came to be
called Pesor for the fame it won through its own resident, once he was a
world-renowned dream interpreter. (Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Ferber of London identifies
the place with the ancient Patru on the west bank of the Euphrates, just south of
Carchemish; Chullin 54b has an Amora named Rav Chana of Pesor.) The column
wonders further whether the root's turning up specifically in Yosef's Egyptian
story has something to do with *Patros*, a name for Egypt made of those three
letters plus a final samech.

On the etymology of *poser*, the classical lexicographers take the triliteral
פ-ת-ר. Shadal reads *poser* instead as a contraction of *posei'ach ohr*, "he
opens light," one who sheds light on what perplexes others. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos
13:2) connects *poser* with a tav to *peter* with a tes, both expressions of
opening and revealing, since a *peter rechem* is the firstborn who opens the
mother's womb and reveals her motherhood realized, and a *pitaron* likewise opens
a closed communication to reveal its inner meaning; and he conjectures (to Shemos
25:39) that *kaftor*, a button, may be a portmanteau of *kafut*, tied up, and
*pitaron*/*poser*, opened, since buttons are made to open and close. Rabbi
Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*) takes the trouble to clarify that *poser* is
not a synonym of the Aramaic-Persian *patshegen* (Esther 3:14, 4:8, 8:13): a
*poser* gives the explanation of a text, a *patshegen* is its copy or wording,
a different concept altogether. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei
Yehuda*) holds the reverse, that *patshegen* derives from *poser* plus *ken*,
"so," the kaf of *ken* turning into a gimmel, though he does not quite account
for how the reish of *poser* became a shin.

*Pesher* appears only once in the Hebrew of Tanach, "who knows the *pesher* of a
matter" (Koheles 8:1), where Rashi takes the wise man to be Moshe, who knew how
to mediate between Hashem and Klal Yisrael and so reached the *psharah* that
stopped the destruction after the Eigel. Its Aramaic cognate *p'shar* runs
thirty-three times through Daniel, in connection with dream interpretation, and,
ironically, Targum in the Yosef narrative consistently renders the
seemingly-Aramaic *poser*/*pitaron* by inflections of the seemingly-Hebrew
*pesher*. Ibn Saruk defines each of *pesher* and *pitaron* by the other; Ibn
Janach defines *pitaron* as *peirush* and Radak as *peirush* and *beiur*, while
both follow Ibn Saruk in defining *pesher* as *pitaron*. The column adds that the
Dead Sea Scrolls include works called *pesharim*, interpretations of parts of
Tanach along that sect's lines, on Yeshayahu, Tehillim, Hoshea, Michah,
Tzefaniah, Nachum and Chavakuk, much as Chazal used *midrash*; and that the
Judeo-Arabic *tafsir*, Rav Saadia Gaon's rendering of the Torah, seems to derive
from *pesher*, the shin becoming a sin or samech as often happens in moving from
Hebrew to Arabic.

Beyond the classical triliteral reading, other grammarians account for *pesher*
otherwise. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) derives it from the
biliteral ש-ר, untie or open, as in the Aramaic *um'shareh kitrin*, "and untying
knots" (Daniel 5:12; see also 5:6, 3:25), kin to the Talmudic *sharei*,
permitted, and parallel to the Hebrew *matir*: interpreting a dream, or any
enigma, is untying a knot. On his account *pesher* and *pitaron* are cognates,
with *pesher*, which he holds to be the Aramaic word, the etymon of the later
Hebrew *pitaron*. Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*, and in his glosses to
Radak's *Sefer HaShorashim*) relates *pesher* to *poser* by the interchange of
shin and tav, a Hebrew shin regularly becoming an Aramaic tav
(*shemonah*/*tamnei*, *shor*/*tor*, *yeshivah*/*metivta*) which, the column notes
in a bracket, seems to point the opposite way, making *pesher* Hebrew and
*pitaron* Aramaic. Bachur alternatively suggests that *pesher* is *peirush*
metathesized, shin and reish trading places, an explanation offered as well by
Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Melech (*Michlal Yofi* to Koheles 8:1) and by Bedersi. *Ohalei
Yehuda* instead makes *pesher* a portmanteau of *peh*, mouth, and *yashar*,
straight, perhaps alluding to the part spoken mediation plays both in reaching
correct interpretations and in reaching compromises.

The root's rabbinic offshoots: *psharah*, compromise, a conflict-resolution in
which neither side wins outright and each gives something up to reach a happy
medium (Rambam's commentary to Kesubos 10:6); and *efshar*, possible, on the
assumption that its opening aleph is extraneous to the root. Rabbi Avraham Vilner
(*Tirgem Avraham* to Bereishis 4:14) ties *efshar* to *pesher*, since a *pesher*
solves a conundrum and what is merely "possible" is itself something of a
conundrum, lying between the necessary and the impossible. Dr. Alexander Kohut
(*Aruch HaShaleim*) likewise derives *efshar* from *pitaron* or *pesher*, as does
Rabbi Chaim Gelernter of Kitov in his glosses to *Raglei Mivaser* on *Sefer
HaTishbi*; Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein rejects the etymology. Rabbi Vilner further
gathers in *poshrim*, lukewarm water (Shabbos 20:3, Menachos 5:2, Parah 8:9,
Niddah 7:1), and *posher*, dissolving or melting (Targum Onkelos to Shemos
16:21), as middle states, neither hot nor cold and neither wholly solid nor
wholly liquid, and the Aramaic *pashra*, an animal's cud (Targum Onkelos to
Vayikra 11:3), as the middle stage between food eaten and food digested, though
Rashi there ties *pashra* more directly to *posher*, dissolving and melting the
food.

## Rendering guidance

- פירוש: "explanation," or "commentary" for the written genre (Rashi's
  *peirush*). מפורש: "explicitly," "expressly."
- ביאור: "elucidation" (best), "clarification"; as a genre title keep it
  transliterated. Never "commentary" when it stands beside *peirush*.
- הסבר: "explanation" with the reasoning audible: "the reasoning," "an account
  of why"; סברא: "reasoning," "logic," or transliterate in a learning context.
- פשר / פתרון: "interpretation," "meaning," "solution"; for a dream, always
  "interpretation."
- Trap: English "explanation" absorbs *peirush*, *beiur*, and *hesber*. Where
  a text pairs them (a *peirush* that is not yet a *beiur*; a *beiur* without a
  *hesber*), grade the English: explanation (surface), elucidation (depth,
  written out), reasoning (why).
- Together in a list, keep Malbim's ladder: *peirush* outside, *beiur* inside;
  and Subar's axis: *beiur* what, *hesber* why.
