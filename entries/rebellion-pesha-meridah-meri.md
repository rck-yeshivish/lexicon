# Rebellion: pesha / meridah / meri (mamre)

## Bottom line

Three words that all come out "rebel," and the article's own summary separates
them cleanly. *Pesha* at its core is **negligence** born of laziness, and means
**rebellion** only by extension. *Meridah* is **revolt** as a counter-response,
throwing off a present ruler. *Meri* and *mamre* are **defiance** as switching
allegiance, replacing one authority's word with another's. The trap: a lazy
*poshea* is not a revolutionary, and in the *shomrim* sugya (Shemos 22:8)
*poshea* must be "negligent," never "rebel."

## Distinctions

### pesha (פשע)
The classic rebellion sense: Meisha of Moav, a vassal of the king of Yisrael,
rebelled (*vayifsha*) and asserted his own independence (Melachim II 3:4-5); that
war, in which the kings of Yehudah, Yisrael and Edom united against Moav, is told
both in Tanach and on the Mesha Stele. *Midrash Tanchuma* darshens "a brother
rebelled (*nifsha*)" (Mishlei 18:19) of Korach rebelling against his "brother,"
that is his cousin, Moshe. The Gemara (*Yoma* 36b) makes *pesha* a sin of
rebellion, over against *chet* and *avon*, which are less overtly rebellious, and
this matches the biblical usage.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim denies that rebellion is the primary sense. A *poshea*
is at bottom one who is negligent out of indolence. He is not trying to defy or
provoke; he simply does not care enough about the consequences. Hence the
*poshea* of Shemos 22:8 and *Bava Kama* 21b and 29a, the watchman who failed the
vigilance expected of him without ever meaning to undermine anyone's property
rights. The column then draws the extension in its own voice, continuing from
Pappenheim rather than quoting him: before a king, and all the more before the
King of kings, a higher degree of mindfulness is expected, so that laziness and
apathy are tantamount to rebellion, since failing to live up to what is expected
slights the king's honor. It is in that sense that *pasha* was borrowed to mean
rebellion. In many cases, including the story of Meisha, Targum renders *pasha*
as *mored* (Melachim II 8:22, Yeshayahu 1:2, Yirmiyahu 33:8), which implies
active defiance rather than merely passive languor. (Targum also uses the
Aramaicized form of *meridah* itself to render instances of Hebrew *meridah*; see
Bereishis 14:4, Melachim II 18:20, Yeshayahu 36:5, Yechezkel 2:3.)

### meridah (מרידה)
Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach and Radak assign it the triliteral מ-ר-ד, which carries
two senses, rebellion and lowliness: Tzidkiyahu rebelling against Nevuchadnetzar
(Divrei HaYamim II 36:13) and the five kings against the four (Bereishis 14:4)
on one side, *aniyim merudim* (Yeshayahu 58:7), the utterly downtrodden poor, on
the other. Neither lexicographer joins the two senses. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis
14:4) does: the *aniyim merudim* are so lowly that abusing them draws no
pushback, no rebellion, because the victim is thought too unimportant for his
mistreatment to provoke a response. Yerushalayim after the churban of the first
Beis HaMikdash is described in just that state (Eichah 1:7). *Machberet
Menachem* splits them instead: *meridah* from מ-ר-ד, but *merudim* from the
biliteral ר-ד, downward motion, the crushed pauper having fallen from the
standing of a respected human being. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim also takes ר-ד as
the root, the root of *rodeh*, lording over others and keeping them down
(Bereishis 1:28, Vayikra 25:43); in his system a mem prefixed to a biliteral
reverses it, so מ-ר-ד is the opposite of lording over, namely the subjects
asserting control. The Gemara (*Sanhedrin* 49a) learns the death penalty for a
*mored b'malchus* from Yehoshua 1:18, "any man who defies (*yamreh*) your
mouth."

### meri and mamre (מרי, ממרה)
Moshe calls the nation *mamrim* for refusing Hashem's word (Devarim 9:7, 9:24,
31:27); the Mishnah's renegade elder who defies the Sanhedrin is a *zaken mamre*
(*Sanhedrin* 11:2); the Gemara's apostate is a *mumar* (*Shabbos* 69a, 87a,
*Eruvin* 69a-b, *Yevamos* 47b); and the rebellious son is a *ben sorer u'moreh*
(Devarim 21:18), *moreh* being a cognate of *mamre*. The *Sifrei* (*Ki Seitzei*
218) darshens that *moreh* on *moreh* meaning "teaching": the boy has taught
himself another path. But that exegesis, the column notes, does not tell us the
etymological basis of *mamre*, and it is for that that the article turns to the
lexicographers. Ibn Janach and Radak give the roots מ-ר-ה and מ-ר-ר, both of
which can refer to rebelling or to changing from what one was commanded;
*Machberet Menachem*, as one would expect, links them all to the biliteral מ-ר.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim develops מ-ר as changing, switching, clearing one thing
away so another can take its place, and lists: *mohar* (bride price, given in
exchange for a woman's maidenhood), *maher* (quickly, constantly changing
motion), *mar* (bitter, a taste
the palate wants replaced), *morah* (razor, which clears hair so new hair
replaces it), *temurah* (transferring *kedushah* from one *korban* to another),
and *amirah* (speech, the exchange of ideas, the parties alternating). *Meri*
and *mamre* are then the reaction to something unpalatable: whatever a man
repudiates because it does not agree with his sensibilities. Applied to a ruler,
it is switching out his word for another's and installing someone else in his
place.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus blurs the distinction between *meri*/*mamre* and
*meridah*/*mored*, proposing that both are rooted in the two-letter מ-ר, whose
core meaning is reflected in the honorific *Mar*, an expression of dignity and
sovereignty. As Marcus *seems* to understand it, one who rebels against authority
is poised to assert his own self-possession and dignity, reestablishing himself
as an autonomous being. The article notes that Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim's list cannot include *meridah*, because his system admits
only hei, aleph, mem, nun, tav, yud or vav as the added third letter, and
*meridah*'s is a dalet.

## Rendering guidance

- פשע: "rebellion" where a vassal or a nation throws off a ruler; "negligence"
  in the *shomrim* sugya and wherever the actor is merely careless; "willful
  sin" in the *chet*/*avon*/*pesha* triad (see the separate entry on that
  cluster).
- מרידה / מורד: "revolt," "rebel against." *Mored b'malchus*: "one who rebels
  against the kingship."
- מרי / ממרה: "defiance," "defy." *Zaken mamre*: "the rebellious elder,"
  established usage. *Ben sorer u'moreh*: "wayward and rebellious son."
- מרודים: "downtrodden," not "rebellious," even though the letters are shared.
  Yeshayahu 58:7 and Eichah 1:7 are about destitution.
- Trap: English "rebel" absorbs all three. Where Rashi's opening to Korach
  (Bamidbar 16:1) sends the reader to *Midrash Tanchuma*, which characterizes
  Korach's actions as both *pasha* and *meridah*, two "rebellions" makes the
  Midrash repeat itself; render "he was faithless and he revolted," or gloss the
  first as the affront of not caring.
- Trap: do not let Targum's *mored* for *pasha* set the English. Targum is
  rendering into idiom; Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's point is that the Hebrew word
  carries slackness, not defiance.
