# Word: milah / teivah / davar

## Bottom line

*Milah* is the biblical term (Tehillim 19:5, 139:4; Iyov throughout), and for
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur the **spoken word**; *teivah* in this sense appears only
in later rabbinic writing, a grammarians' term as Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim seems
to understand it, and for HaBachur the **written word**, the one behind *roshei
teivos*. Rabbi Yosef Teomim differs slightly: *milah* can be written or spoken,
*teivah* only written. Rabbi Pappenheim, from מ-ל, "edge," makes *milah* the
word cut off as a unit from the sentence. *Davar* is a **word** that is also a
**thing**, and for Rabbi Moshe Shapiro that double sense is the point.

## Distinctions

### milah (מילה)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) seems to understand *milah* as the
original Hebrew term for "word," and *teivah* as a later neologism coined by
grammarians for the grammatical concept. *Milah* is in Tanach many times:
Tehillim 19:5, 139:4; Shmuel II 23:2; Mishlei 23:9; over thirty times in Iyov;
and in the Aramaic of Daniel. *Teivah* in the sense of "word" first appears
only in later rabbinic writing.

Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549, also known as Elias Levita), in *Mesores
HaMesores* and *Sefer Tishbi*, notes that many treat the two as identical and
disagrees: *milah* is the spoken word, *teivah* the written one. Rabbi Yosef
Teomim (1727 to 1792) differs slightly: *milah* covers both written and spoken,
while *teivah* is written only.

On the etymology there are two lines. Rabbi Shlomo Zalman of Hanau (1687 to
1746) cites HaBachur's distinction and adds that *milah* comes from מ-ל-ל,
"speech," the root of Sarah's *millel* (Bereishis 21:7) and of David HaMelech's
*mi yemallel gevuros Hashem* (Tehillim 106:2). Rabbi Klein adds that *millel* is
the standard Targumic rendering of *amirah*, and that the Eretz Yisrael saying
in *Megillah* 18a, a *milah* is worth a sela and silence two, sets *milah*
against *shesikah*, which also implies that it refers to speech. On this
reading, he explains, a *milah* is the smallest unit of speech with a meaning of
its own, as against the phonemes or syllables it is built from, and he compares
Greek *lego*, "to speak," the source of *lexis* and *logos*, "word."

Rabbi Pappenheim takes a slightly different approach, to the biliteral מ-ל,
"edge, extremity." *Milah* in *bris milah* is cutting off the extremity;
*milah* as "word" is an independent unit cut off from the rest of the sentence.

### millel (מלל) as a verb
How does *millel* differ from *amirah*, *dibbur*, *sichah*, *ne'um*, *yechaveh*
and *yabia*? *Peirush HaRokeach* and *Siddur HaRokeach*: *millel* is speaking
elaborately and at length, as in Bildad's *ad an temallel eileh* (Iyov 8:2).
Rabbeinu Efrayim (to Bereishis 21:7): *millel*, unlike the other verbs of
speech, is speaking the truth. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743 to
1826) makes a similar point: *millel* is speaking so as to explain a thing as
clearly and accurately as possible. They adduce *v'daas sefasai barur mileilu*
(Iyov 33:3).

### teivah (תיבה)
In Tanach *teivah* is an ark or chest: Noach's *teivah* (Bereishis 6 to 9), and
the basket in which Moshe Rabbeinu was placed (Shemos 2:3). In Mishnaic Hebrew
it is the shul's ark, or the table (*bimah*) on which the Torah is laid for
reading. HaBachur brings his proof that *teivah* is the written word from
*Yevamos* 13b, where any *teivah* needing a lamed prefix may take a hei suffix
instead, which he understands to refer mainly to how the word is written; and
from *roshei teivos*, written abbreviations. Rabbi Shlomo Zalman of Hanau
explains the transfer differently: *teivah* means box, and it refers to the
written word because the books that hold written words are stored in one.

On the root: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) gives ת-ב-ה; Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) mentions ת-י-ב as a possible alternative. Following Radak, Rabbi
Yosef Teomim suggests tying Aramaic ת-י-ב to Hebrew ש-ו-ב, "return" (a
connection that, Rabbi Klein notes, may be justified by the common shift of
Hebrew shin to Aramaic tav): what a person stores in a *teivah* he means to
come back for, unlike what he leaves on the floor. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis
6:14) similarly suggests it comes from Aramaic י-ת-ב, Hebrew י-ש-ב, sitting or
dwelling: the *teivah* was a temporary dwelling. Rabbi Klein suggests that
perhaps *teivah* as "word" is then a place where letters are nestled. Rabbi
Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) links *teivah* to *bayis* by metathesis, the same
letters rearranged, so a *teivah* is in some ways a home; on that basis Rabbi
Klein would say that a *teivah*, as "word," is a house or box in which the
letters come together. Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit, an early
twentieth-century exegete, reads *teivah* as a blend of Aramaic *ta*, "come,
enter" (Hebrew *bo*), and Hebrew *bah*, "into it." Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842 to
1894) reports that some derive *teivah* as "word" from an Arabic root meaning
"to cut," which would make it, like *milah* on Rabbi Pappenheim's reading,
related to cutting; others, he notes, derive it from *teivah* as "box," a word
being a box that holds its letters, as Rabbi Klein suggests.

### davar (דבר)
Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935 to 2017) finds it especially telling that ד-ב-ר means
both "word" or "speak" and "thing," alluding to what *Sefer Yetzirah*
describes, that Hashem created the world by combining the letters of the
alef-beis into Divine words. Rabbi Akiva Tatz puts it that all things in the
world are Divine words crystallized into material existence. Rabbi Shapiro
brings *Shabbos* 58b, where sound-producing implements have the status of
*keilim*, "things," for the laws of tumah, as a passage that explicitly links
*davar* the thing to speech. The Aramaic *milta* has the same double sense,
"word," as a cognate of *milah*, and "thing." Rabbi Matityahu Glazerson proposes
that English *word* comes from *davar* by metathesis and the interchange of the
w and b or v sounds; if he is right, Rabbi Klein adds, the same could be said of
German *wort*, Yiddish *vort*, and Latin *verb*.

Mrs. Faigy Peritzman (*Mishpacha*, 1 April 2020) muses on how words box abstract
thought into something finite: *davar* also means a thing, because a word makes
abstract thought concrete; *milah* also means cutting, because a word cuts
limitless thought down to something real; *teivah* also means a box, because a
word squeezes infinite thought into a finite casing.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *milah* "word" (spoken, on HaBachur); *teivah* "word" in
  a grammatical or Masoretic context, otherwise "ark" or "box" by plain sense;
  *davar* "word," "thing," "matter," chosen by context, and where a source plays
  on both, say so in a note rather than picking one.
- Trap: a discussion of the text may use *teivah* for the word on the page and
  *milah* for the word in the mouth. Rendering both as "word" erases HaBachur's
  distinction. Use "the written word" or "the word as written" for *teivah*
  where the contrast is live.
- *Roshei teivos*: "initial letters" or "abbreviation"; "the heads of the
  words" is the literal gloss.
- *Millel*: Rabbi Klein renders it "spoken" and "say" (Bereishis 21:7; Tehillim
  106:2), and it is the Targum's standard word for *amirah*. Where a commentary
  builds on the Rokeach's sense (at length) or Rabbeinu Efrayim's (truly), bring
  that out, as at Iyov 33:3, "my lips speak knowledge clearly."
- Rabbi Pappenheim's *milah* from מ-ל, "edge," is the etymology behind any
  reading that links *bris milah* to speech; where a text makes that link, it is
  his, and should be attributed rather than presented as the plain meaning.
