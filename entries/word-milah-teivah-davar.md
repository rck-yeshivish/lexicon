# Word: milah / teivah / davar

## Bottom line

*Milah* is the **spoken word**, the biblical term (Tehillim 19:5, 139:4; Iyov
throughout). *Teivah* is the **written word**, a later grammarians' term, and
the one behind *roshei teivos*. *Davar* is a **word** that is also a **thing**,
and that double sense is the point wherever a source works with it. The trap:
English "word" flattens all three. If a passage distinguishes what was said from
what is written on the page (a Masoretic or dikduk discussion), *milah* and
*teivah* must not both come out as "word."

## Distinctions

### milah (מילה)
The original Hebrew term for "word," so Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814).
It is in Tanach many times: Tehillim 19:5, 139:4; Shmuel II 23:2; Mishlei 23:9;
over thirty times in Iyov; and in the Aramaic of Daniel. *Teivah* in the sense
of "word" surfaces only in later rabbinic writing.

Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549), in *Mesoras HaMesores* and *Sefer
Tishbi*, notes that many treat the two as identical and rejects it: *milah* is
the spoken word, *teivah* the written one. Rabbi Yosef Teomim (1727 to 1792)
narrows it from the other side: *milah* covers both written and spoken, while
*teivah* is written only.

On the etymology there are two lines. Rabbi Shlomo Zalman of Hanau (1687 to
1746) derives *milah* from מ-ל-ל, "speech," the root of Sarah's *millel*
(Bereishis 21:7) and of David HaMelech's *mi yemallel gevuros Hashem* (Tehillim
106:2). *Millel* is the standard Targumic rendering of *amirah*. The Gemara
(*Megillah* 18a) quotes the Eretz Yisrael saying that a *milah* is worth a sela
and silence two, where the opposite of *milah* is *shesikah*, which again puts
it on the side of speech. On that reading, a *milah* is the smallest unit of
speech that carries a meaning of its own, as against a syllable or a phoneme,
which does not.

Pappenheim goes the other way, to the biliteral מ-ל, "edge, extremity." *Milah*
in *bris milah* is cutting off the extremity; *milah* as "word" is the unit cut
off and standing free of the rest of the sentence.

### millel (מלל) as a verb
Distinct from *amirah*, *dibbur*, *sichah*, *ne'um*, *yechaveh* and *yabia*.
*Peirush HaRokeach* and *Siddur HaRokeach*: *millel* is speaking at length and
elaborately, as in Bildad's *ad an temallel eileh* (Iyov 8:2). Rabbeinu Efrayim
(to Bereishis 21:7): *millel*, unlike the other verbs of speech, is speaking the
truth. Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743 to 1826) is close to that:
*millel* is speaking so as to explain a thing as clearly and exactly as one can,
and both cite *v'daas sefasai barur mileilu* (Iyov 33:3).

### teivah (תיבה)
In Tanach *teivah* is an ark or box: Noach's *teivah* (Bereishis 6 to 9), and
the basket in which Moshe Rabbeinu was placed (Shemos 2:3). In Mishnaic Hebrew
it is the shul's ark, or the *bimah* on which the Torah is laid. HaBachur brings
his proof that *teivah* is the written word from *Yevamos* 13b, where any
*teivah* needing a lamed prefix may take a hei suffix instead, which is a
statement about how the word is written; and from *roshei teivos*, written
abbreviations. Rabbi Shlomo Zalman of Hanau explains the transfer differently:
*teivah* means box, and the written word lives in a box, since the books that
hold written words are kept in one.

On the root: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) gives ת-ב-ה; Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) offers ת-י-ב as an alternative. Following Radak, Rabbi Yosef
Teomim ties Aramaic ת-י-ב to Hebrew ש-ו-ב, "return," on the regular Hebrew
shin to Aramaic tav correspondence: what a man puts in a *teivah* he means to
come back for, unlike what he leaves on the floor. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis
6:14) reads it from Aramaic י-ת-ב, Hebrew י-ש-ב, sitting or dwelling: the
*teivah* was a temporary dwelling, and a word, on that reading, is where letters
are lodged. Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) links *teivah* to *bayis* by
metathesis, the same letters rearranged, so the *teivah* is a kind of home; the
article extends that to the word as the house in which its letters come
together. Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit reads *teivah* as a compound of
Aramaic *ta* (come in, Hebrew *bo*) and *bah* (into it).

### davar (דבר)
Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935 to 2017): the striking fact about ד-ב-ר is that it
means both "word" and "thing," which points at what *Sefer Yetzirah* describes,
that Hashem made the world by combining the letters of the alef-beis into
Divine words. Rabbi Akiva Tatz puts it that everything in the world is a Divine
word crystallized into material existence. Rabbi Shapiro brings *Shabbos* 58b,
where implements that produce sound are *keilim*, "things," for the laws of
tumah, as a place where Chazal themselves link *davar* the thing to speech. The
Aramaic *milta* runs the same double sense, word and thing, as a cognate of
*milah*.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *milah* "word" (spoken); *teivah* "word" in a dikduk or
  Masoretic context, otherwise "ark" or "box" by plain sense; *davar* "word,"
  "thing," "matter," chosen by context, and where a source plays on both, say so
  in a note rather than picking one.
- Trap: a discussion of nusach or of a variant reading will use *teivah* for the
  word on the page and *milah* for the word in the mouth. Rendering both as
  "word" erases HaBachur's whole distinction. Use "the written word" or "the
  word as written" for *teivah* where the contrast is live.
- *Roshei teivos* stays as it is, or "initial letters"; never "heads of words"
  in running prose.
- *Millel* in a pasuk: not simply "said." Take the Rokeach's sense (spoke at
  length) or Rabbeinu Efrayim's (spoke truly) as the context supports, for
  example Iyov 33:3, "my lips speak knowledge clearly."
- Pappenheim's *milah* from מ-ל, "edge," is the minority etymology but the
  useful one for a derashah linking *bris milah* to speech; where a sefer makes
  that link, it is his, and should be attributed rather than presented as the
  plain meaning.
- Mrs. Faigy Peritzman (*Mishpacha*, 1 April 2020) collects the three as a
  mnemonic: *davar* because a word makes an abstract thought a thing, *milah*
  because it cuts limitless thought down to something finite, *teivah* because
  it boxes the infinite into a casing. Fine as a homiletical aside, not as
  lexicography.
