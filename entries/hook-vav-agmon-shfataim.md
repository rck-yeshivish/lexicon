# Hook: vav / agmon / shfataim (ankliyot)

## Bottom line

There are seemingly three words for "hook" in Biblical Hebrew and one in
Mishnaic. *Vav* seems to be the most basic, the fittings of the Mishkan's
pillars, and it occurs nowhere else in Tanach. *Agmon* is a **hooked reed** or
thistle, used, as Rabbi Pappenheim explains it, as a fishhook and then for
anything bent. *Shfataim* occurs once and is disputed: **hooks** (Targum, Rashi)
or a **setting for pots** (Radak, Abarbanel) or a **ledge** (Rabbi Eliezer of
Beaugency). *Ankliyot* is the Mishnaic word for a hook, especially its barbed
part, and appears to be a Greek loanword; it is what Targum uses for *shfataim*
and, at least at Iyov 40:26, for *agmon*.

## Distinctions

### vav (וו)
Thirteen appearances, all in the Mishkan chapters of Shemos (26, 27, 36, 38).

- Targum Onkelos never translates it; he leaves *vav* standing as *vav*.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak all take the root as ו-ו.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim seems somewhat inclined to the single letter ו.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) supposes that *vav* is not a root-word at all:
  the sixth letter is shaped like a hook, much as the Arabic numerals 6 and 9
  are, so the letter itself came to stand for "hook" in early Hebrew, without
  the word *vav* actually meaning "hook." He goes as far as to claim that the
  crude usage was abandoned once Klal Yisrael entered Eretz Yisrael, on the
  evidence that the word appears nowhere else in Tanach.
- Rashi (to Shemos 27:10) glosses *vavim* as *ankliyot*.

### ankliyot (אנקליות)
The Mishnaic word (Pesachim 5:9, Tamid 3:5, Middos 3:5, Keilim 12:2-3),
especially the barbed part of a hook. It appears to be a Greek loanword, and
seems to be related to the English *anchor*, *angle*, *angling*, *ankle*
and *hang*: on the linguists' account Greek and English/Germanic both descend
from Proto-Indo-European, and the forebear of the family is the PIE *ang*/*ank*,
"to bend." Some scholars even trace to that word the name of the Angles, the
Anglo-Saxon tribe behind the name England, arguing either that their homeland was
a hook-shaped peninsula off the continent or that they were expert fishermen
adept at angling; if so, *onkliyot* is also the ultimate etymon of the word
*English*. Targum uses *ankliyot* to render *shfataim* at Yechezkel 40:43, where
it is spelled with an initial ayin, and to render *agmon* at Iyov 40:26, at
least there.

Rabbi Klein adds a speculation of his own: he began to wonder whether the
Greek *onkliyot* is somehow related to the Greek personal name Onkelos, borne by
the author of the Targum; a similar Greek name, Akylas, belonged to another
famous translator of the Torah, and some have argued the two translators were one
man. The question is treated at length in A. E. Silverstone's *Aquila and
Onkelos* (Manchester University Press, 1970), which cites a letter Shadal wrote
to the Maskilic scholar Rabbi Shlomo Yehuda Rappaport of Prague (1786-1867).
There Shadal argues that the Greek *onkliyot* actually derives from the Hebrew
root ע-ק-ל, "crooked," and that people who knew the Hebrew term therefore
mispronounced Onkelos as Akylas. The aleph/ayin difference between the two need
not trouble us, Rabbi Klein remarks, since in this Greek word the two letters
interchange anyway.

Rabbi Moshe Yitzchak Ashkenazi Tedeschi (*Hoil Moshe* to Yechezkel 40:43):
*unkelin* comes from ע-ק-ל with a nun added, as *sha'ananim* and *shenanim*.

### agmon (אגמון)
Five appearances (Iyov 40:26, 41:12; Yeshayahu 9:13, 19:15, 58:5). This is the
third biblical word, one that Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino does not explicitly list as
a synonym of *vav* and *shfataim*.
- *Machberes Menachem* defines it as a cane with a hooked top.
- Radak takes it as a kind of reed, reading א-ג-ם against ג-מ-א by metathesis,
  from which *gome*, reed.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim works the same ground from the two-letter root ג-ם,
  absorbent or spongy matter: *agam* is a swamp, which soaks up water and stays
  wet; *gome* is the spongy reed that grows there; *agmon* is primarily a
  hook-topped thistle that also commonly grows in such marshland; it was
  commonly used as a fishhook, and from there the word came to mean anything
  hooked or bent.
- Targum renders *agmon* differently in Yeshayahu 9:13 and 19:15, as *hegmon*,
  itself a word of Greek origin and the antecedent of English *hegemony*, a
  reading Radak cites in *Sefer HaShorashim*; at Yeshayahu 58:5 Targum leaves it
  untranslated.

### shfataim (שפתים)
Once only, Yechezkel 40:43.
- Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino, in his lexicon of Hebrew synonyms *Ohel Moed*, gives
  *shfataim* as an alternate word for "hook, hanger." Targum there renders
  *ankliyot*, hooks, and Rashi and Radak cite that reading.
- But Radak himself (in his commentary and in *Sefer HaShorashim* s.v. ש-פ-ת)
  and Abarbanel explain *shfataim* instead as the **place** on which pots were
  set, a sort of stovetop; *Machberes Menachem* implies the same by filing the
  word under his fifth category of the ש-ף root. Rabbi Pappenheim also follows
  this approach, deriving it from the two-letter root ש-ף, moving without
  lifting the feet from the ground: Bilaam who went *shefi* (Bamidbar 23:3), the
  *ashafim* (Daniel 1:20, 2:2, 2:10), and the *shefifon*, the snake that never
  lifts itself off the floor. On that reading *shfataim* is where the pots sit
  and are not lifted until the cooking is done.
- Rabbi Eliezer of Beaugency (to Yechezkel 40:43) reads it as a ledge or lip
  running around the perimeter of the Table.

## Rendering guidance

- *Vav* in the Mishkan chapters: **hook**. The pillars' *vavim* are their
  hooks; the word does not need transliteration except where the letter-name
  play is the point.
- *Agmon*: **reed** where the plant is meant (Yeshayahu 58:5, the bowed head
  like a reed), **hook** where the fishing sense is (Iyov 40:26). Choose by
  context; the word carries both.
- *Shfataim*: give the Targum's **hooks** with a gloss for Radak's **hearths**
  or Rabbi Eliezer of Beaugency's **ledges**, or the reverse; do not print one
  silently.
- Trap: English "hook" absorbs *vav*, *agmon*, *ankliyot* and one reading of
  *shfataim*. Where a source glosses one Hebrew word with another (Rashi
  explaining *vavim* as *ankliyot*), keep the gloss visible: "*vavim*, that is,
  *ankliyot*, hooks," rather than "hooks, that is, hooks."
- Second trap: *agmon* and *gome* both name marsh plants, and "reed" will cover
  both. Where they stand together, take *gome* as **bulrush** and *agmon* as
  **hooked reed**.
