# Speech: ne'um / yichaveh / yabia / siach

## Bottom line

The article glosses *ne'um* as "word," and its opening question is why a word
for "word" is used overwhelmingly for the Word of Hashem, what its etymology is,
and how it differs from *amirah*, *dibbur* and *sichah*. The answer it develops
is that *ne'um* is the solemn declaration whose whole force is that it is true
and settled: render it **declaration**, **utterance**, or in a *nevuah* **thus
says**, and never flatten it to "said." *Yichaveh* is
speech that gives an idea life by bringing it out into the open, or, on
Pappenheim's alternative, speech that joins speaker to listener as *ach* to
*ach*: **express**, **declare**. *Yabia* is speech welling up from the depths like a
spring: **pour forth**, **utter**. *Siach* is discursive, wandering speech that
unburdens the speaker: **converse**, **muse**, **pour out one's talk**. The trap
is that "speak" and "say" absorb all four.

## Distinctions

### ne'um (נאם)
It occurs 373 times in Tanach, and 362 of them are the word of Hashem. The only
human speakers whose words are called *ne'um* are David HaMelech (Shmuel II
23:1), Shlomo HaMelech (Mishlei 30:1), and Bilaam (Bamidbar 24:3, 24:4, 24:15,
24:16). When Yirmiyahu attacked the false *nevi'im* for speaking a *ne'um*
(Yirmiyahu 23:31) he used the verb *vayinamu*, which appears nowhere else in
Tanach.

Menachem, Ibn Janach and Radak take the root as נ-א-ם. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
of Breslau (1740 to 1814, *Yerios Shlomo*) offers what the article calls a more
thorough approach: *ne'um* is a poetic word used to stress the veracity of a
statement, marking that what is said is no collection of random words
haphazardly spewed out but deliberate and accurate declarations.
He derives it from the biliteral א-ם, "if," the root that also yields *emes* and
*amen*/*ne'eman*. One who speaks in the *ne'um* register speaks in absolutes, as
if all he says is simply true; Yirmiyahu's charge is exactly that the false
*nevi'im* affected that register while he knew they were lying. Radak in *Sefer
HaShorashim* (under א-מ-ן and under נ-א-ם) likewise ties *ne'um* to truth. In
*Cheshek Shlomo* Pappenheim gives a second account of א-ם, "if, on condition":
*ne'um* stresses the gravity of the circumstance that moved the speaker to
address his hearers.

The article adds a modern echo of the same equation of "word" with truth: per
the *Oxford English Dictionary*, English *word* in Ebonics and hip-hop slang
expresses affirmation or agreement, so that "Word!" amounts to "That's the
truth!" or "There's no denying it!", a usage that likely stems from the
influence of Christian preachers reading the Bible and translating *ne'um* as
"word."

One of the Dead Sea Scrolls (4Q339) contains a list of false prophets with
Bilaam on it. Against that, rabbinic tradition maintains that Bilaam was not a
*false* prophet as such but an *evil* one: he tried to use his jaundiced outlook
to have Hashem convey to him a malevolent *nevuah* against the Jews, and in the
end quite the opposite happened. When all is said and done, his declarations
were true in the same way the Word of Hashem elsewhere is true. In that spirit,
*Peirush HaRokeach* and Rabbeinu Efrayim explain that *ne'um* conveys the
content of an irrevocable decree or oath, even for Bilaam's *ne'um*. The article
adds, with its own hedge ("it seems"), that David and Shlomo could use it of
themselves because as Divinely chosen kings they were able to speak assertively
and decisively in a way that whatever they said was or became true.

The verb form appears only once in Tanach but is much more common in later
post-biblical Hebrew: *numasi*/*nimasi*, "I said," in the Mishnah (Yevamos
16:7), in Rabbi Akiva's report of what he said to a Sage in Babylonia about a
complex halachic question, and *numeinu*, "we said" (Gittin 6:7; also Tosefta
Sanhedrin 2:1 and Nazir 4:7). The halachic *Midrashim* use *nam lo* for "he said to him" in
place of *amar lo* (*Mechilta* to Shemos 12:6, 12:21, 12:43; *Sifrei*
Beha'alosecha 65, Shelach 110, Pinchas 142), and, remarkably, only in the
disputes of Rabbi Yonasan and Rabbi Yoshiyah.

*Sefer HeAruch* files these rabbinic forms under נ-ם without explicitly linking
them to *ne'um*. Rabbi Gershon Shaul Yom Tov Lipmann Heller (1579 to 1654) holds
they are cognates even though spelled without an *aleph*, since the *aleph*
often disappears from a word's morphological inflections. Rabbi Yechezkel Landau
(1713 to 1793, *Noda B'Yehudah*) adds that the common Talmudic *neimah*, "let us
say," is a cognate too, of Hebrew *ne'um* and Aramaic *nam*; and both he and
Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia note that the *aleph*-less form is already Biblical
Aramaic (Ezra 4:8, 5:4, 5:9, 6:13). Ibn Ezra (to Yeshayahu 1:24) seems to treat
that spelling as a mistake, though it is the standard rabbinic form.

On the Yom Kippur *Mussaf piyyut* "*zechor... namta*": Abudraham reads *namta*
as a cognate of *ne'um*. Pappenheim objects that it cannot possibly be read as
*namta*, since that would mean "You who slumbered," from the noun *tenumah*,
"sleep"; he suggests *ne'umta* if the poet meant to follow a biblical Hebrew
style, or *numisa* if a rabbinic one, and he mentions and endorses an alternate
version registering the word as *sachta*, a cognate of *sichah*. That alternate
version is also found in the *machzor* edited by Ernst Daniel Goldschmidt (1895
to 1972). Rabbi Landau nevertheless concludes that *namta* as "You said" is also
correct.

Dr. Shlomo Mandelkorn (1846 to 1902), in his concordance of biblical Hebrew
*Heichal HaKodesh* (page 710), notes that an Arabic cognate of *ne'um* means "to
whisper." The article records this and says outright: "I am not sure what to
make of that."

### yichaveh / achaveh (יחוה / אחוה)
Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and Radak: root ח-ו-ה (Tehillim 19:3; Iyov 13:7, 32:10,
32:17). Menachem Ibn Saruk: the biliteral ח-ו. Pappenheim derives ח-ו from ח-י
("life") and/or א-ח ("brotherhood, unity"), both of which he traces further back
to the monoliteral root ח. On the first, this speech gives an idea life by
expressing it verbally instead of leaving it hidden away in the thoughts; for
the *vav*/*yod* interchange he adduces the name *Chavah*, which the Torah itself
ties to *chai*, spelled with a *yod* (Bereishis 3:20). Alternatively, he relates
it to *ach*, "brother," because speech creates connection and comradery by
linking speaker with listener. *Peirush HaRokeach*: *yichaveh* is speech for the
purpose of explaining the reasoning behind something, though he offers no
etymological account of it.

### yabia / abiah (יביע / אביעה)
Pappenheim: from the biliteral ב-ע, revealing what lies beneath the surface. At
its plainest the root gives *mabua*/*novea*, a wellspring rising out of a hidden
source (Mishlei 18:4, Koheles 12:6, Yeshayahu 35:7). So *yabia* (Tehillim 19:3)
and *abiah* (Tehillim 78:2) are speech flowing up from the depths of the heart
and breaking out in an attention-grabbing way. A similar point had already been
made by Ibn Janach and Radak in their respective *Sifrei HaShorashim*. *Siddur HaRokeach* and *Peirush HaRokeach*:
*yabia* is speaking on and on without stopping, like a spring that never runs
dry.

### siach / sichah (שיח / שיחה)
*Yasiach* (Tehillim 119:23), *asichah* (Tehillim 55:18, 77:4 and 77:13, 119:15,
145:5; Iyov 7:11) and *lasuach* (Bereishis 24:63). Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and
Radak: root ש-ו-ח. Menachem: the biliteral ש-ח. Pappenheim takes ש-ח from ס-ח
("uprooting, removing, transferring"): *sichah* is stream-of-thought speech,
the mind wandering, by which a man uproots his sorrows and lets them go.
*Peirush HaRokeach* reads the transferring differently: *sichah* is speech that
moves from topic to topic within one conversation.

## Rendering guidance

- *Ne'um Hashem* is "the declaration of Hashem" or "says Hashem," and in a
  passage that turns on Pappenheim's reading, the English should carry the
  finality: "so declares Hashem," not "so speaks Hashem." Bilaam's
  *ne'um Bilam beno Beor* (Bamidbar 24:3) gets the same weight; the whole point
  is that his words were binding truth.
- Trap: English "utterance" would cover *ne'um*, *yabia* and *yichaveh* alike.
  Assign one each: *ne'um* "declaration," *yabia* "pours forth," *yichaveh*
  "expresses."
- Tehillim 19:3 puts *yabia* and *yechaveh* in one pasuk, "*yom l'yom yabia omer
  velailah lelailah yechaveh daas*." They must differ in English: "day to day
  pours forth speech, and night to night expresses knowledge." Two identical
  verbs would erase the parallelism the *meforshim* are reading.
- Bereishis 24:63, *lasuach basadeh*: "to meditate," "to pour out his thoughts."
  Following Pappenheim, this is unburdening speech, not conversation with
  another person.
- Rabbinic *nam lo*, *numeinu*, *neimah*: translate as ordinary "he said," "we
  said," "let us say." Do not import the Biblical solemnity into a *sugya*; the
  register shifted.
