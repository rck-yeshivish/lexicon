# Table: shulchan / petora / taka / tavla

## Bottom line

*Shulchan* is the Biblical **table**, and the Aramaic *petora* is its standard
equivalent. Rashi glosses *taka* as *shulchan* too, and the variant readings of
one Talmudic story seem to make it interchangeable with *petora*. But Rabbi
Mordechai Dov Yudelovitch, whose view Rabbi Klein adopts, holds that *taka* is
the small, private **tray-table** for one, and *petora* the longer dining table.
For Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur, *tavla* is simply the Rabbinic Hebrew for
*shulchan*; for Rash MiShantz it differs, a **board** or table top without legs
attached.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether petora and taka are one word or two.** Rashi glosses both with
*shulchan*: *petorah* at Beitzah 29b, *taka* at Berachos 46b, Gittin 67b, and
Kiddushin 81a. The texts seem to pull the same way. In the story of Abaye at
Rabbah's Seder, where the table was lifted away at Maggid and Abaye asked why
(Pesachim 115b), our printed text reads *taka*; the Rif (Pesachim 25b in his
pagination) reads *petora* both times; Rabbi David Abudraham's commentary to the
Haggadah has *petora* in the narration and *taka* in Abaye's question; and all
three readings are attested in manuscripts on the *Hachi Garsinan* website.
Rabbi Klein's first conclusion is that it seems this interchangeability points
to the two being synonymous. The Arizal, in his Aramaic *Atkinu Seudata* songs
for the Shabbos meals, writes *petora chadata* (Friday night) and *l'ater
petora* (Shabbos morning), but *hai taka* for the third meal.

But do they mean the same thing? Rabbi Mordechai Dov Yudelovitch of Lida (d.
1951; also spelled Judilovitz) says no: *petora* is a large table, *taka* a
small, private table on which people would customarily eat on festive occasions
or in the presence of esteemed guests, that is, a portable personal tray-table
for one, while *petora* is a longer table of the sort one has in a dining room.
Rabbi Shaul Goldman arrived at the same conclusion independently. So, Rabbi
Klein concludes, despite Rashi's glossing both as *shulchan*, the two do not
mean exactly the same thing, and every *taka* example he cites refers to a
private table at which one person ate, as against the more communal
*petora*: Rava's own *taka* removed while the Reish Galusa's still stood
(Berachos 42a), and the bad wife who prepares her husband's *taka* (Yevamos
63b).

There is an etymological difference as well. Dr. Michael Sokoloff, in his
dictionary of Jewish Palestinian Aramaic of the Byzantine period, writes that
*petora* is a cognate, or even a derivative, of the Akkadian *passuru* (plural
of *passurum*), itself borrowed from the Sumerian *bansur*, "table"; in his
dictionary of Jewish Babylonian Aramaic of the Talmudic and Geonic periods he
notes that the etymology of *taka* is unknown.

**Whether tavla is a shulchan.**

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549, *Sefer HaTishbi*): *tavla* is the
  Rabbinic Hebrew equivalent of the Biblical *shulchan*.
- Nonetheless, Rash MiShantz (Rabbi Shimshon of Sens, to Ohalos 15:2) writes
  that they differ: a *shulchan* is a flat surface attached to four legs that
  hold it up, while a *tavla* is a table top not attached to legs, so it can
  even be a flat board or plank.

Rabbi Klein leaves the two views standing.

**The root of shulchan.**

- Ibn Janach (990 to 1050) and Radak (1160 to 1235): the triliteral ש-ל-ח,
  "dispatch, send away, sword," though exactly how it connects to those meanings
  is not readily apparent.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970, *Machberes Menachem*): a quadriliteral ש-ל-ח-ן
  of its own.

## Distinctions

### shulchan (שולחן)
All 21 occurrences in the Chumash are the ritual *shulchan* of the Mishkan
bearing the lechem hapanim; in the other 50 in Tanach it can be an ordinary
table at which a king or great man eats and feeds his household. The Mishnaic
*shulchani*, "moneychanger" (Maaser Sheini 4:2; Bava Metzia 2:4, 3:11, 4:6,
9:12; Shevuos 7:6; Meilah 6:5; Keilim 12:5), is so called because he typically
worked from behind a table on which he laid the money. The Gemara also uses *shulchan*
euphemistically for marital intimacy (Nedarim 20a-b).

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bamidbar 36:4): a table is like a
tree, which offers its fruit to be eaten, and a tree sending forth is sometimes
*shalach* (Yeshayahu 16:8); so a *shulchan* sends forth and presents the foods
of a meal. Rabbi Dr. Eliyahu Rachamim Zini, in his glosses there, suggests
instead that in ancient times small tables were sent out from the kitchen to the
dining room with the food on them.

### petora (פתורא)
The Targumim consistently render *shulchan* as *petora*, and Rashi (Beitzah 29b)
defines Talmudic *petorah* as *shulchan*; just so, Aramaic *petoraah* is Hebrew
*shulchani*, a moneychanger (see Rashi to Chullin 54b).

- Balak sent for Bilaam *pesorah* (Bamidbar 22:5), literally "to Pethor"; the
  Midrash (Bamidbar Rabbah 20:7; Tanchuma Balak 4) and Rashi there explain that
  Bilaam functioned, after a fashion, like a moneychanger, all the kings running
  to him to do business as merchants run to a moneychanger. The *Matnos Kehunah*
  explains the connection: *petora* in Aramaic is "table," and the Mishnah's
  word for a moneychanger, *shulchani*, comes from *shulchan*.
- Rabbi Avraham Menachem Rappaport (1520 to 1596, *Minchah Belulah* to Bamidbar
  22:5) offers two more readings of *pesorah*: from *pisaron*, interpretation,
  for Bilaam's occupation as a dream interpreter; and from *petora*, table,
  since Bilaam's pagan rites included "setting a table for Gad" (Yeshayahu
  65:11): as the Mikdash had a ritual *shulchan* for the holy service of Hashem,
  the idolaters set a ritual table for their deities, the holy and the unholy
  often paralleling each other as mirror images.

Shabbos 36a records that *patora* and its diminutive *patorta* once meant a
large table and a small one respectively, and that after the Churban the two
senses switched.

### taka (טכא)
Rashi glosses it *shulchan* (Berachos 46b, Gittin 67b, Kiddushin 81a). The word
appears many times in the Gemara. Once the *taka* is
removed at the end of a meal one has essentially declared intent to bench and may not eat
again until he does (Berachos 42a); Rava, eating at the Reish Galusa's, went on
eating food the Reish Galusa sent him after his own *taka* was taken away,
explaining that he did not yet intend to bench, relying on the Reish Galusa's
*taka*, which had not been removed. The bad wife, for Abaye, sets her husband's
*taka* and meanwhile curses him, and for Rava sets his *taka* and turns her back
on him, not eating with him (Yevamos 63b). The word does not seem to appear in
Tanach, but some commentators explain a similar word in one pasuk as related to
it: Moshe describes Yisrael's servility, "and they are *tuku* at Your feet"
(Devarim 33:3), where *tuku* is obscure and variously explained. Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto
(Shadal, 1800 to 1865) and Rabbi Ezra Reuven Dangoor (1848 to 1930) relate it to
*taka*, "sitting at a table": Yisrael sit at Hashem's table to accept His
directives and receive His blessings, as a guest at his host's.

### tavla (טבלא)
Mishnaic (Eruvin 5:1, Yoma 3:10, Keilim 2:3, 16:8, 25:1, 27:1, Mikvaos 4:2) and
Talmudic (e.g. Pesachim 57a).

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) equates it with *shulchan*, and in
  his entry connects it to the Latin *tabula*, the forebear of English *table*,
  *tablet*, *tabulate*, *tabloid* and more.
- Rash MiShantz distinguishes it, as above.

- Rabbeinu Manoach (to Rambam, Hilchos Chametz uMatzah 6:7) relates *tevel*,
  produce not yet tithed, to *tavla*: as one does not eat a table but eats from
  it, so one may not eat *tevel* but must separate the tithes and eat only from
  a subset of it.
- Alternatively he takes *tevel* as a portmanteau of *tav*, good, and *lo*, not,
  an explanation also offered by *Sefer HeAruch*, Bartenura (to Berachos 7:1),
  Rabbi Chaim Vital (*Eitz Chaim*, Shaar 50 ch. 3) and *Kli Yakar* (to Shoftim
  11:3).
- He also suggests that *tevel* is related to the Arabic *mitabel*, food with
  something mixed into it, which, Rabbi Klein notes though Rabbeinu Manoach does
  not, is a cognate of Hebrew *tavlin*, spice (see also *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to
  Shemos 22:28).

Names: the Gemara has sages named for *petora* (Ben Petora, also spelled
Betorah) and *tavla* (Rav Tavla, Bava Basra 111a, Chullin 132b), but none for
*taka*. The Yiddish names Tevle/Tevele are unrelated to *tavla*: Alexandre
Beider explains them as forms of David (the t- and d-sounds interchanging)
and/or Tuvia, with the diminutive -le.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: שולחן and פתורא = "table"; טכא = "table" in ordinary narrative, but
  "his own table" or "tray-table" where the sugya contrasts it with another's;
  טבלא = "board" or "table top," "table" only where no legs are at issue.
- Trap: English "table" absorbs all four without a murmur. Two places where it
  must not: Berachos 42a, where the point is that Rava's personal *taka* went
  and the host's remained, so "the table was removed" flattens the story; and
  Ohalos 15:2, where a *tavla* rendered "table" imports legs the Rash denies.
- Where Aramaic *petora* renders Hebrew *shulchan* in a Targum, use one English
  word for both; the Targum is translating, not distinguishing.
- שולחני = "moneychanger," but keep the table in view in the Bilaam derashah,
  where the whole reading depends on it.
