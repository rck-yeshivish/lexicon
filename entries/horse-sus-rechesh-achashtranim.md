# Horse: sus / rechesh / achashtranim / remachim

## Bottom line

*Sus* is the ordinary **horse**. *Rechesh* is a particular kind, fast, used for
couriers or for pulling: **swift steed** or **courier horse**, not plain "horse."
*Achashtranim* and *remachim* occur together only in Esther 8:10, and Megillah
18a says outright that we do not know what they mean; the majority reading makes
*achashtranim* **mules** and *remachim* the **mares** that bore them. Never
render the four words of that pasuk as four synonyms for "horse."

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| סוס | sus | horse | the general term; root tied to *sasson*, gladness (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, Rav Hirsch) |
| רכש | rechesh | courier horse, swift steed | a specific fast or wagon-pulling horse, not the hypernym (Ralbag, Rav Hirsch) |
| אחשתרנים | achashtranim | mules | born of a male donkey and a mare (Rav Saadia Gaon and most); Rashi: fast camels |
| רמכים | remachim | mares | the mares that bore them (Radak); a wild horse (Rambam); or simply young (Lekach Tov) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Is *rechesh* a horse at all?** A superior, especially fast young horse (Ralbag
to Esther 8:10; Ibn Janach in *Sefer HaShorashim*; and in a similar vein the
German linguist Wilhelm Gesenius, 1786-1842, who ties *rechesh* to the Arabic verb
for a horse's running or galloping and so takes it as a superior breed known for
remarkable speed). Some unspecific swift-running animal, not necessarily a horse,
since Esther 8:10 and Melachim I 5:8 list *rechesh* beside *sus* (Radak, who
elsewhere identifies it with the mules). A permutation of *rechush*, property, so
the king's own horses (Ibn Ezra, Rabbi Moshe Ibn Chalava; Solomon Rabinowitz of
New York, d. 1943, in *Sefer HaMishkalim*, reads *rechush* in Ezra 1:4 the other
way about, as the *rechesh* horse rather than ordinary property). A horse decked
in its rider's weapons, itself a kind of *rechush*, or from *reches*, "tying,"
the horse-and-rider team bound together (*Ohalei Yehuda*, Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of
Carpentras). From ר-כ-ס, tying, by the shin/sin-samech interchange, hence the
wagon-horse (Rav Hirsch to Bereishis 12:5), so *sus* is general and *rechesh*
specific. The Maskilic Hebrew scholar Naftali Torczyner, Tur-Sinai (1886-1973),
reaches the wagon-horse independently, taking ר-כ-ש as a metathesis of ק-ש-ר,
tying and tethering, probably on the kuf/kaf interchange; he goes further and has
*rechush* derive from *rechesh*, since a man's main property was his livestock,
or because moveable goods were carried by *rechashim*. Our own working choice,
not the column's, is Rav Hirsch and Ralbag.

**What *achashtranim* are.** Mules, from a male donkey and a mare, as against the
weaker hinnies (Rav Saadia Gaon, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Rabbi Moshe Ibn Chalava,
Ralbag); Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (*Sefer HaGalui*) reports Rav Saadia Gaon's
etymology, Persian *achash*, "big," plus Aramaic *trein*, "two," one beast
merging two species. An adjective on *rechesh*, from CHET-SHIN, speed, plus
TAV-REISH, scouting, the mounts spies used (*Midrash Lekach Tov*), or the same
roots with TAV-REISH as "jumping," so that the animal's swiftness is in its leap
rather than its run (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim). Fast-running camels (Rashi), whom
the column marks as somewhat an outlier. No animal at all but an ordinary
adjective, "government-owned," from the Old Persian *khshathrana*/*khshathra*,
"dominion, government, province" (Rabbi Ernest Klein), another outlier. Ibn
Janach adds that it is not too farfetched to presume *achashtranim* and
*rechashim* are the same thing. Eight-legged beasts that run on one set of four
legs, then draw those into the body and let the other four down when tired, with
האחשתרנים in *gematria* (1014) equalling *shel shemonah raglaim*, "of eight legs"
(*Peirush HaRokeach* to Bereishis 1:25 and Esther 8:10, who reads the extra *es*
in *v'es habehemah* as including the otherwise unmentioned *achashtranim*, though
he does not say what sort of animal they are). A similar tradition is in Rabbi
Yosef Kara's commentary to Esther, though he attaches it to "sons of the
*remachim*" and makes them **flying camels** with two sets of four legs, close to
Rashi's camels.

**What "sons of the *remachim*" means.** A wild horse, as against the
domesticated *sus*, which one might have thought a separate species for the
interbreeding prohibition (Rambam to Kilayim 8:5 and Hilchos Kilayim 9:5); a
zebra (Rabbi Shlomo Sirilio); the mares that bore the mules (Radak); simply young
or small horses (Rabbi Tuviah ben Eliezer, Rashi to Taanis 23a, Ibn Yachya); or
the riders, REISH-MEM, lifting, with MEM-KAF, striking (Rabbi Tuviah). Against
Megillah 18a's flat "we do not know," Rabbeinu Nissim of Gerona (*Teshuvos HaRan*
79, in *HaRivash* 390-391) holds that only the masses do not know.

## Distinctions

### sus (סוס)
Roughly 140 times in Tanach, and in the name Gadi ben Susi (Bamidbar 13:11); at
Yeshayahu 38:14 the same word is a bird. Ibn Janach and Radak take the root as
SAMECH-VAV-SAMECH, Menachem Ibn Saruk as biliteral SAMECH-SAMECH. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim offers two: in *Yerios Shlomo*, the one-letter root SAMECH,
repellence, yielding *nisah*, fleeing, the animal one flees on; in *Cheshek
Shlomo*, SIN-SIN, liveliness, the root of *sasson*. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis
1:21) reads SAMECH-VAV-SAMECH alongside ZAYIN-VAV-ZAYIN, SIN-VAV-SIN, and
TZADI-VAV-TZADI as degrees of movement: *zuz*, shifting; *sasson*, the movement
of joy; *tzitz*, blossoming. The link predates the grammarians: Shir HaShirim
Rabbah 1:49 (on 1:9) has Hashem glad to drown Pharaoh's cavalry, which *Eitz
Yosef* and Rabbeinu Bachaya ground in this very pun.

### rechesh (רכש)
Four times in Tanach: Esther 8:10, 8:14; Melachim I 5:8 (tribute to Shlomo
HaMelech); Michah 1:13 (harnessing a wagon). The Gemara's *arkavei a'trei
richashei*, "he mounted him on two *rechashim*" (Kesubos 55b, Bava Basra 152a,
Yerushalmi Kiddushin 1:5), settles on two horses pulling together, so a recipient
holding two claims to one gift has the stronger case.

### achashtranim (אחשתרנים) and remachim (רמכים)
Both are confined to Esther 8:10. Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, and Radak parse
*achashtranim* from a six-letter root, Ibn Janach holding the NUN, and probably
the ALEPH, not root letters; Ibn Ezra's rule is that four- and five-letter roots
are compounds or loanwords, and both routes are taken here. REISH-MEM-KAF is
nowhere else in Tanach, but *ramach* is in Kilayim 8:5, which Yerushalmi Kilayim
8:4 ties to the Megillah's word.

## Rendering guidance

- Esther 8:10 is the whole problem in one verse. A defensible rendering: "the
  couriers riding swift steeds, the royal mules, offspring of the mares," with a
  note that Megillah 18a says the last two words are not known. Rendering all
  four as "horses" erases the difficulty the Gemara itself flags.
- Trap: English "horse" swallows *sus* and *rechesh* together. Where a pasuk has
  both (Esther 8:10, Melachim I 5:8), give *sus* "horses" and *rechesh* "swift
  steeds," as Radak's argument from those pesukim requires.
- Kilayim 8:5: render *ramach* "wild horse" after Rambam, not "mare."
