# Prohibition: azharah / issur / lav

## Bottom line

*Azharah* is the prohibition as **forewarning**, the notice given before the
penalty can attach; several authorities read its root as light, so it is closer
to "putting a person on notice by making his duty clear" than to scolding.
*Issur* is the prohibition as a **binding**, the rule that ties a person's hands
and takes the act off the table. *Lav* is simply a **negative commandment**, a
"thou shall not," named for the word *lo*. The one thing not to get wrong:
*azharah* and *issur* are not two names for one thing. *Azharah* is the warning
half of a prohibition as against the *onesh*, its punishment half (*Yevamos*
2b); *issur* is the substantive prohibited status.

## Distinctions

### azharah (אזהרה)
Root ז-ה-ר. Cognates appear some twenty-two times in Tanach, beginning with
Yisro's advice, "and you shall forewarn (*ve'hizhartah*) them concerning the
statutes and the teachings" (Shemos 18:20). The noun *azharah* in the sense of
a prohibition is mishnaic (*Pesachim* 3:1 and 4:1, *Sanhedrin* 7:7, *Kerisos*
3:10), and in that usage it names the warning side of a prohibition, the
"thou shall not," against the punishment side (*Yevamos* 2b; *Sifrei Naso* 1).

The early lexicographers (Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak, Rabbi Shlomo
Ibn Parchon) all take ז-ה-ר as one root carrying two unrelated senses, warning
and light (Hoshea 7:16, Tehillim 132:12), and none of them joins the two.
Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam joins them: the root means light or brilliance
(*zohar*), and to warn a person is to shed light on what is expected of him, so
both senses are enlightenment, one literal and one figurative. Rabbi Jose Faur
infers from this that *azharah* covers positive commandments as well as
negative ones, which is why the *azharos* piyutim recited on Shavuos enumerate
all 613 and not only the prohibitions.

Others take the root as two letters, ז-ר. Rabbeinu Efrayim: to warn a person is
to tell him to make himself *zar*, estranged, from the thing warned against, and
one cognate is in fact spelled without the *hei*, "and you shall separate
(*ve'hizartem*) the Children of Israel from their impurity" (Vayikra 15:31).
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim also works from ז-ר, whose derivatives head up in
*zoreh*, to scatter (Shemos 32:20, Vayikra 26:33, Rus 3:2, Tehillim 106:27);
*zohar*, light, is a sub-derivative, light being what scatters and spreads out.
Just as rays spread to illuminate as much as they can, a person must spread his
attention across a wide field of knowledge in order to be careful. Pappenheim is
explicit that *azharah* does not mean a warning at all: *lehazhir* means to
enlighten, as in "you shall enlighten them and they shall not bear guilt"
(Divrei HaYamim II 19:10) and "and you shall enlighten them from Me" (Yechezkel
3:17). So Pappenheim's two-letter root and Rabbeinu Avraham's three-letter root
land in the same place.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus goes further back, to the single letter *zayin*, which he
takes as visual revelation, what the eye can see: *zeh* (which Chazal read as
pointing at something seen), *zach*, *zahav*, *ziv*, *zikim*, *zanav*, *ozen*,
*chazah*. *Zohar* on this reading is the light that reaches the eye. Elsewhere
Marcus derives *zohar* from the earlier *tzohar* and *tzoharayim* (Vayikra
noon-light), with *tzadi* shifting to *zayin*.

### issur (איסור)
In Tanach *assur* is literally tied up with ropes. One pasuk already uses it
abstractly: one who vows "to bind a binding (*le'esor issar*) upon his soul"
(Bamidbar 30:3), that is, to create a prohibition on himself, much as English
says "my hands are tied." That abstract use becomes standard in the Mishnah, and
*issur* as a noun is the rule that renders a thing forbidden. Rabbi Pappenheim
ties *assur* to the two-letter ס-ר, removal: one who is tied has his freedom of
movement removed. Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Chayyuj had already treated the *aleph* as
outside the root, noting that *beis ha'surim* (Koheles 4:14) is the equivalent
of *beis ha'asurim* (Bereishis 39:20, 40:3).

### lav (לאו)
Not in Tanach; rabbinic. Formed from *lo*, "no" or "do not," used as a noun. It
denotes an act the Torah has said not to do, commonly one whose penalty is less
severe than *kares* or death.

## Rendering guidance

- Render *azharah* as "forewarning" or "prohibition," and where a sugya sets it
  against *onesh*, always "warning" against "punishment"; "prohibition" alone
  destroys the pair. The classic formulation *lo anash ela im ken hizhir* is
  "He did not punish unless He first forewarned."
- Trap: English "prohibition" will swallow *azharah*, *issur* and *lav* alike.
  When two of them stand in one sentence, keep "forewarning" for *azharah*,
  "prohibition" for *issur*, and "negative commandment" for *lav*.
- Second trap: "forbidden" for *assur* is right in halachic prose but wrong in a
  pasuk, where *assur* is literally bound or imprisoned (Bereishis 39:20).
- *Azharos* as the name of the Shavuos piyutim is best transliterated and
  glossed once, "the *Azharos*, the liturgical enumerations of the mitzvos,"
  rather than translated "Warnings," since on Rabbi Faur's reading they cover
  the positive commandments too.
- Where an author is building on Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam or Pappenheim,
  "enlighten" is the load-bearing word and must survive into the English, even
  though it reads oddly: "you shall enlighten them," not "you shall warn them."
