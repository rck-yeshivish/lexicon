# Prohibition: azharah / issur / lav

## Bottom line

*Azharah* is the prohibition as **forewarning**; the column's own summary is
that it *seems* to focus on a prohibition as a way of "enlightening" a person as
to his duties and expectations, and several authorities read its root as light,
so on their reading it is closer to putting a person on notice by making his
duty clear than to scolding him. *Issur* is the prohibition as a **binding**,
the rule that ties a person's hands and takes the act off the table. *Lav* is
simply a **negative commandment**, a "no-no" people are enjoined from
committing, named for the word *lo*. The one thing not to get wrong: *azharah*
and *issur* are not two names for one thing. *Azharah* often names the warning
aspect of a prohibition as against its punishment aspect (*Yevamos* 2b);
*issur* is the substantive prohibited status.

## Distinctions

### azharah (אזהרה)
Root ז-ה-ר. Cognates appear some twenty-two times in Tanach, among them Yisro's
advice, "and you shall forewarn (*ve'hizhartah*) them concerning the statutes
and the teachings" (Shemos 18:20). The noun *azharah* in the sense of a
prohibition first appears in the Mishnah, where it occurs a handful of times
(*Pesachim* 3:1 and 4:1, *Sanhedrin* 7:7, *Kerisos* 3:10), and it often denotes
the warning aspect of a prohibition, the "thou shall not," as against the
punishment aspect (*Yevamos* 2b; *Sifrei Naso* 1 are the column's examples).

The early lexicographers (Menachem Ibn Saruk, Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach, Radak,
and Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon, the twelfth-century author of
*Machberes He'Aruch*) all take ז-ה-ר as one triliteral root carrying two very
distinct senses, warning and light (Hoshea 7:16, Tehillim 132:12), and none of
them intimates a connection between the two. Maimonides' son, Rabbi Avraham
Maimuni, joins them: the root means light or brilliance (*zohar*), and one
illuminates another's intellectual perception by instructing him as to his
responsibilities, so to warn a person can be said to be shedding light on what
is expected of him; both senses are enlightenment, one literal and one
figurative. The late Rabbi Dr. Jose Faur infers from this that at its core
*azharah* covers what the Torah prescribes and not only what it outlaws, and
that this is why the *azharos* recited on Shavuos list both kinds of mitzvah
and not only the prohibitions.

Not everybody agrees that the root is the triliteral ז-ה-ר. Some trace the word
to the two-letter ז-ר, estrangement, as in *zar*; others go as far as the single
letter *zayin*. The column notes that one cognate is in fact spelled without the
*hei*, "and you shall warn (*ve'hizartem*) the Children of Israel from their
impurities" (Vayikra 15:31), and that Rabbeinu Efrayim touches on this point in
noting that to warn a person is to tell him to make himself *zar*, estranged,
from the thing he has been warned against. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau
similarly works from ז-ר, one group of whose derivatives is headed by *zoreh*,
to scatter (Shemos 32:20, Vayikra 26:33, Rus 3:2, Tehillim 106:27); *zohar*,
light, is a sub-derivative, light being what scatters and spreads out. Just as
rays of light spread out to illuminate as much as possible, an enlightened
person must spread his intellectual purview across a wide body of knowledge in
order to be careful. Pappenheim explicitly notes that *azharah* does not mean a
warning, as many have understood it: *lehazhir* means to enlighten, as in "you
shall enlighten them and they shall not incur guilt" (Divrei HaYamim II 19:10)
and "and you shall enlighten them from Me" (Yechezkel 3:17), enlightening a
person as to his obligations being tantamount to forewarning him about what he
should be careful of. So Pappenheim's two-letter root and Rabbi Avraham
Maimuni's three-letter root land in the same basic explanation.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus traces *zohar* to the monoliteral root *zayin*, which he
takes as the visual revelation of what the eye can see, the Hebrew words derived
from it belonging to the realm of the ocular: *zeh*, "this," which by rabbinic
tradition always refers to something perceivable by sight; *zach*, pure;
*zahav*, gold, shiny and sparkling; *ziv*, countenance, which dazzles the eyes;
*zikim*/*zekukin*, sparks of fire, which glitter and shimmer; *zanav*, tail, and
*ozen*, ear, both of which protrude and are quite visible; and *chazah*, either
vision itself or *chazeh*, the chest, a very visible part of the body. *Zohar*
on this reading is the light that illuminates the eye. Marcus notes further that
the Germanic words for seeing (*sehen* in Modern German, *zien* in Dutch, *zen*
in Yiddish, *sien* in Afrikaans, *see* in English) also seem to be phonetically
related to the letter *zayin*. Elsewhere Marcus contends that *zohar* with a
*zayin* derives from the earlier Biblical *tzohar*, light, and *tzoharayim*,
noon, when the sun's light reaches its peak, the *tzadi* having later morphed
into a *zayin*, the two letters often being considered interchangeable.

### issur (איסור)
In Tanach *assur* means tied up with actual ropes or cables. In at least one
instance the term takes on a more abstract sense: one who vows "to tie a tying
(*le'esor issar*) upon his soul" (Bamidbar 30:3), that is, to create a
prohibition on himself, much as English says "my hands are tied." That abstract
use of *assur* and its cognates appears countless times through the Mishnah,
until *assur* in post-Biblical Hebrew came to mean forbidden, and *issur* is the
more abstract derivative, the rule that renders a thing forbidden. Rabbi
Pappenheim
ties *assur* to the two-letter ס-ר, removal: one who is tied has his freedom of
movement removed. Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Chayyuj had already treated the *aleph* as
outside the root, noting that *beis ha'surim* (Koheles 4:14) is the equivalent
of *beis ha'asurim* (Bereishis 39:20, 40:3).

### lav (לאו)
Not in Tanach; found often in later rabbinic writings. An anthimeria formed from
*lo*, "no," "not," "do not," used as a noun. In most cases it simply denotes a
prohibited act that the Torah has warned a person not to do, even where the
punishment for the proscribed action is not as severe.

## Rendering guidance

- Render *azharah* as "forewarning" or "prohibition," and where a sugya sets it
  against the punishment aspect, always "warning" against "punishment";
  "prohibition" alone destroys the pair.
- Trap: English "prohibition" will swallow *azharah*, *issur* and *lav* alike.
  When two of them stand in one sentence, keep "forewarning" for *azharah*,
  "prohibition" for *issur*, and "negative commandment" for *lav*.
- Second trap: "forbidden" for *assur* is right in halachic prose but wrong in a
  pasuk, where *assur* is literally bound or imprisoned (Bereishis 39:20).
- *Azharos* as the name of the Shavuos piyutim is best transliterated and
  glossed once, "the *Azharos*, the liturgical enumerations of the mitzvos,"
  rather than translated "Warnings," since on Rabbi Faur's reading they cover
  the positive commandments too.
- Where an author is building on Rabbi Avraham Maimuni or Pappenheim,
  "enlighten" is the load-bearing word and must survive into the English, even
  though it reads oddly: "you shall enlighten them," not "you shall warn them."
