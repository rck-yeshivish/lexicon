# Prohibition: azharah / issur / lav

## Bottom line

*Azharah* is the prohibition as **forewarning**. Rabbi Klein concludes that it
*seems* to focus on a prohibition as a way of "enlightening" a person as to his
duties and expectations, and several authorities read its root as light, so that
on their reading it puts a person on notice by making his duty clear. It often
names the warning aspect of a prohibition, as opposed to its punishment aspect
(*Yevamos* 3b). *Issur* is the prohibition as a **binding**, the rule that ties
a person's hands and bars him from certain actions; it is the substantive
prohibited status. *Azharah* and *issur* are not two names for one thing. *Lav*
is simply a **negative commandment**, a "no-no" people are enjoined from
committing, named for the word *lo*.

## Distinctions

### azharah (אזהרה)
Root ז-ה-ר. Cognates appear some twenty-two times in Tanach, among them Yisro's
advice, "and you shall forewarn (*ve'hizhartah*) them concerning the statutes
and the teachings" (Shemos 18:20). The noun *azharah* in the sense of a
prohibition first appears in the Mishnah, where it occurs a handful of times
(*Pesachim* 3:1, *Sanhedrin* 7:7, *Kerisos* 3:10), and it often denotes
the warning aspect of a prohibition, the "thou shall not," as against the
punishment aspect (for example, *Yevamos* 3b and *Sifrei Naso* 1).

- The early lexicographers (Menachem Ibn Saruk, Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach, Radak,
  and Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon, the twelfth-century author of *Machberes
  He'Aruch*) all take ז-ה-ר as one triliteral root carrying two very distinct
  senses, warning and light (Yechezkel 8:2, Daniel 12:3), and none of them
  intimates a connection between the two.
- Rasag, in his commentary to Koheles 12:12, seems to connect the two senses.
  Rabbi Klein learned this from Mossad HaRav Kook's *Toras Chaim* to Shemos
  18:20 and had not seen the Koheles commentary himself.
- Rambam's son, Rabbi Avraham Maimuni, does connect them: the root means
  light or brilliance (*zohar*), and one illuminates another's intellectual
  perception by instructing him as to his responsibilities, so to warn a person
  can be said to be shedding light on what is expected of him; both senses are
  enlightenment, one literal and one figurative. The late Rabbi Dr. Jose Faur
  infers from this that at its core *azharah* covers what the Torah prescribes as well as what it outlaws, and that this is why the *azharos* recited on
  Shavuos list both kinds of mitzvah, positive commandments alongside the prohibitions.

Not everybody agrees that the root is the triliteral ז-ה-ר. Some trace the word
to the two-letter ז-ר, estrangement, as in *zar*; others go as far as the single
letter *zayin*.

- One cognate is in fact spelled without the *hei*, "and you shall warn
  (*ve'hizartem*) the Children of Israel from their impurities" (Vayikra 15:31),
  and Rabbeinu Efrayim touches on this point in noting that to warn a person is
  to tell him to make himself *zar*, estranged, from the thing he has been
  warned against.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau similarly works from ז-ר, one group of
  whose derivatives is headed by *zoreh*, to scatter (Shemos 32:20, Vayikra
  26:33, Rus 3:2, Tehillim 106:27); *zohar*, light, is a sub-derivative, light
  being what scatters and spreads out. Just as rays of light spread out to
  illuminate as much as possible, an enlightened person must spread his
  intellectual purview across a wide body of knowledge in order to be careful.
  Pappenheim explicitly notes that *azharah* does not mean a warning, as many
  have understood it: *lehazhir* means to enlighten, as in "you shall enlighten
  them and they shall not incur guilt" (Divrei HaYamim II 19:10) and "and you
  shall enlighten them from Me" (Yechezkel 3:17), enlightening a person as to
  his obligations being tantamount to forewarning him about what he should be
  careful of.

So Pappenheim's two-letter root and Rabbi Avraham Maimuni's three-letter root
land in the same basic explanation.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus traces *zohar* to the monoliteral root *zayin*, which he
takes as the visual revelation of what the eye can see, the Hebrew words derived
from it belonging to the realm of the ocular: *zeh*, "this," which by rabbinic
tradition always refers to something perceivable by sight; *zach*, pure;
*zahav*, gold, shiny and sparkling; *ziv*, countenance, which dazzles the eyes;
*zikim*/*zekukin*, sparks of fire, which glitter and shimmer; *zanav*, tail, and
*ozen*, ear, both of which protrude and are quite visible; and *chazah*, either
vision itself or *chazeh*, the chest, a very visible part of the body. *Zohar*
on this reading is the light that illuminates the eye. Marcus notes further that
the Germanic words for seeing (*sehen* in Modern German, *zien* in Dutch, *zen*
in Yiddish, *sien* in Afrikaans, *see* in English) also seem to be phonetically
related to the letter *zayin*. Elsewhere Marcus contends that *zohar* with a
*zayin* derives from the earlier Biblical *tzohar*, light, and *tzoharayim*,
noon, when the sun's light reaches its peak, the *tzadi* having later morphed
into a *zayin*, the two letters often being considered interchangeable.

### issur (איסור)
In Tanach *assur* means tied up with actual ropes or cables. In at least one
instance the term takes on a more abstract sense: one who vows "to tie a tying
(*le'esor issar*) upon his soul" (Bamidbar 30:3), that is, to create a
prohibition on himself, much as English says "my hands are tied." That abstract
use of *assur* and its cognates appears countless times through the Mishnah,
until *assur* in post-Biblical Hebrew came to mean forbidden, and *issur* is the
more abstract derivative, the rule that renders a thing forbidden.

- Rabbi Pappenheim ties *assur* to the two-letter ס-ר, removal: one who is tied
  has his freedom of movement removed.
- Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Chayyuj had already treated the *aleph* as outside the root,
  noting that *beis ha'surim* (Koheles 4:14) is the equivalent of *beis
  ha'asurim* (Bereishis 39:20, 40:3).
- Malbim, for whom *asirah* is tying done without the consent of what is tied,
  as a captive is detained against his will, notes that *issur* in the sense of
  prohibition likewise refers to one's actions being precluded, by law or by
  nature, against his will.

### lav (לאו)
Not in Tanach; found often in later rabbinic writings. An anthimeria formed from
*lo*, "no," "not," "do not," used as a noun. In most cases it simply denotes a
prohibited act that the Torah has warned a person not to do, even where the
punishment for the proscribed action is not as severe.

## Rendering guidance

- Render *azharah* as "forewarning" or "prohibition," and where a sugya sets it
  against the punishment aspect, always "warning" against "punishment";
  "prohibition" alone destroys the pair.
- Trap: English "prohibition" will swallow *azharah*, *issur* and *lav* alike.
  When two of them stand in one sentence, keep "forewarning" for *azharah*,
  "prohibition" for *issur*, and "negative commandment" for *lav*.
- Second trap: "forbidden" for *assur* is right in halachic prose but wrong in a
  pasuk, where *assur* is literally bound or imprisoned (Bereishis 39:20).
- *Azharos* as the name of the Shavuos piyutim is best transliterated and
  glossed once, "the *Azharos*, the liturgical enumerations of the mitzvos,"
  rather than translated "Warnings," since on Rabbi Faur's reading they cover
  the positive commandments too.
- Where an author is building on Rabbi Avraham Maimuni or Pappenheim,
  "enlighten" is the load-bearing word and must survive into the English, even
  though it reads oddly: "you shall enlighten them," not "you shall warn them."
