# Writing: ketivah / charut / charushah / chakuk / pasal / pituach

## Bottom line

*Ketivah* is the general word for **writing**, about 260 times in Tanach, used
both of the Luchos and of a Sefer Torah written in ink on parchment. Most of the
others are ways of cutting. *Charut* is writing pressed or cut deep into the
material: Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, Rav Hirsch and, centuries earlier, Rabbi
Moshe Zacuto take it from *chor*, a hole, writing that went clean through the
stone, while Rashi and Rashbam relate it to ח-ר-ט, the work of the *cheret*
(stylus). *Charushah* is **engraving**, which Ibn Saruk files under plowing.
*Chakuk* is **incising** small grooves with a stylus (Pappenheim). *Pasal* is
technically **engraving or hewing** rather than writing. *Pituach* is the
opposite of engraving, **embossing**: the background is cut or pressed down so
the letters stand out (*Gittin* 20a). Aramaic *galif*, Targum's word for
*pituchei chosam*, is tied by Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg to ג-ל-ב, the barber,
and derived by Rabbi Ernest Klein from Greek *glyphein*. *Resham* is **noting
down**, for Pappenheim the writing of only the heads (*rosh*) of a matter. The
trap: English "engrave" will take *charut*, *charushah*, *chakuk* and *pasal*
all at once, and will also wrongly take *pituach*, which is its opposite.

## Distinctions

### ketivah (כתיבה)
Root כ-ת-ב, the most common verb for writing, about 260 times in Tanach. It is
used for the Luchos (Shemos 31:18, 32:16, 34:28; Devarim 9:10, 10:4) and for
writing a Sefer Torah in ink on parchment (Devarim 31:9, 31:22, 31:24).

A long-standing tradition going back to the Maharal holds that the first time a
word appears in Tanach epitomizes its essence. By that measure Rabbi Klein finds
it telling that the first כ-ת-ב is *k'sov zos zikaron ba'sefer*, after the war
with Amalek (Shemos 17:14): writing makes words last longer, and more robustly,
than words merely said. Rabbi Yitzchak Ginsburgh points out a similar theme in
the last occurrence of כ-ת-ב in Nevi'im, *vayikasev sefer zikaron* (Malachi
3:16), again writing as a lasting reminder. Rabbi Shem Tov Gaguine goes as far
as to infer, from the fact that writing is never mentioned in all of Bereishis,
that Moshe Rabbeinu was the first person ever to write anything down, and the
Amalek account the first thing written. Rabbi Klein expects most historians to
disagree, but calls it a fascinating suggestion.

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 34:27) connects כ-ת-ב with כ-ת-ף (shoulder), ק-ט-ף
(plucking, detaching) and ק-ט-ב (killing), by the interchange of peh and beis,
kaf and kuf, tes and tav. Writing, for him, is a worthy bearer of ideas but a
dangerous medium: one has exactly what was written and is in danger of losing
whatever of the idea was left unwritten. That is *detaching* a part of an idea
from the whole edifice, which can even *kill* the whole idea; and the *shoulder*
is only a corner of the body, not the body itself. Rav Hirsch stresses that
because of this danger Hashem gave not only the Written Torah but the Oral
Torah, which takes in so much more; only the two in tandem give the complete,
accurate picture of Hashem's will.

Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) uses the word *mechokek* to define
כ-ת-ב. Rabbi Klein does not think this means Menachem read every *ketivah* as
engraving, since *ketivah* is also used for writing a Sefer Torah (Devarim 31:9,
31:22, 31:24), which is ink on parchment, not engraving.

### charut (חרות) and cheret (חרט)
The writing on the Luchos is *charut* (Shemos 32:16). The classical
lexicographers, Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak, take it from ח-ר-ת, which
occurs only here, a *hapax legomenon*. That unique word in the context of the
Luchos is likely what led Chazal (Eruvin 54a; *Shemos Rabbah* 32:1; *Shir
HaShirim Rabbah* 8:3; and elsewhere) to connect *charus* with *cheirus*,
freedom, and to explain in various ways how the Torah's strictures are freedom
rather than servitude.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim relates *charut* to *chor*, a hole, from the biliteral
ח-ר: writing that cuts an open hole in the material. The word alludes to the
writing on the Luchos going through the stone, so that it could miraculously be
read from both sides (see Rashi to Shemos 32:15; Shabbos 104a). Rav Hirsch makes
a similar point (to Shemos 32:2, 32:15). Several centuries before Pappenheim,
Rabbi Moshe Zacuto (in his commentary to *Zohar Korach* 177a) had already made
the same claim, noting that had the Torah meant simply that the Luchos were
engraved, it should have said *charushah*, not *charut*.

Other commentators, Rashi and Rashbam among them (to Shemos 32:16), relate
ח-ר-ת to ח-ר-ט, by the interchange of tav and tes. That root appears at least
twice: the *cheret* (stylus, engraver) with which the Golden Calf was fashioned
(Shemos 32:4), and the *cheret enosh* in which Yeshayahu was told to write a
prophecy (Yeshayahu 8:1), which seems to be a form of script (some scholars
identify it as cuneiform). Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 41:8, Shemos 7:11) and
others derive *chartumim*, the Egyptian or Chaldean priest-magicians, from the
same root as *cheret*, since they were experts in mysterious writings inscribed
with the *cheret*. Either way, Rabbi Klein concludes, the writing denoted by
ח-ר-ת or ח-ר-ט is a form of engraving, the letters pressed or cut deep into the
material to leave an imprint.

### charushah (חרושה)
Yirmiyahu, rebuking the kingdom of Yehudah, says that the sin of Yehudah is
engraved, *charushah*, on the tablet of their heart (Yirmiyahu 17:1). Menachem Ibn Saruk
gives the root ח-ר-ש five distinct senses: muteness, deafness, thinking,
craftsmanship and plowing. He ties *charushah* to plowing, implying that
engraving, which digs into the surface written on, resembles plowing, which digs
into the ground before sowing. Ibn Janach and Radak make similar points in their
*Sefer HaShorashim*.

Rabbi Klein thinks it quite plausible that ח-ר-ש is related to ח-ר-ת, shin and
tav being consecutive letters of the alphabet. Indeed Ibn Saruk essentially
defines *charushah* by *charut* and *charut* by *charushah*, and Targum (to
Yirmiyahu 17:1) renders *charushah* with an Aramaic cognate of *charut*, which
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) justifies by the interchange of Hebrew
shin with Aramaic tav.

Rabbi Shaul Goldman relates the root of *charushah* to the other roots that
begin ח-ר and whose third letter is one of the interchangeable zayin, shin,
tzadi, tes and tav. Besides *charut* and *cheret*, the group takes in other words
for making a hole: *charitz* (ditch) and *charuz* (stringing, threading, putting
something through a hole; in later Hebrew, rhyming and versification).

### chakuk (חקוק)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim explains *chakuk* too as writing by engraving: digging
small grooves with a metal stylus into a wooden or stone surface to represent
letters (or pictures, pictographs, hieroglyphs). This is for him the core
meaning of the biliteral ח-ק. He derives from it *chok*, the statute whose reason
is not widely known: because the rationale is not obvious, the exact rules must
be written down to preserve them, or they are likely to be forgotten. As a
corollary the legislator is a *mechokek* (Bereishis 49:10; Devarim 33:21;
Shoftim 5:9, 5:14; Tehillim 60:9), literally a digger or engraver (see Bamidbar
21:18 in that literal sense), because he is the legal innovator who creates the
need to write a law down and engrave it for posterity. Rabbi Shaul Goldman sees
a more abstract link: engraving draws a border around the contours of a letter,
as governing draws a legal line; so too *chok* in the sense of a quota
apportions set amounts and draws the limits of those quantities. Outside Tanach,
*chakuk* is the word Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi uses in his poem *Yom Shabbason* for the
Shabbos command inscribed on the Luchos.

### pasal (פסל)
Named among the words for the writing on the Luchos, but technically
**engraving** or **hewing**. The verb פ-ס-ל appears six times in Tanach, four of
them for Moshe carving the second Luchos (Shemos 34:1, 34:4; Devarim 10:1,
10:3), once for hewing stones for the Beis HaMikdash (Melachim I 5:32) and once
for hewing an idol (Chavakuk 2:18). The overwhelming majority of the root's
occurrences are the nouns *pesel* and *pesilim*, idols. The discarded shavings
of a hewn object are disqualified from the finished piece, which is why *pasul*
in the halachic sense means disqualified from a given purpose; and since the
Torah disqualifies idols from ritual use, the finished idol is itself called a
*pesel*, like the byproducts of carving. Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 4:16) connects
פ-ס-ל with פ-צ-ל (*pitzel*, peeling) and ב-צ-ל (*batzal*, onion): all three
involve cutting or otherwise removing outer layers.

### pituach (פיתוח) and galif (גליף)
*Pituach*, *pituchei chosam*, is the lettering of the names of the tribes on the
shoham stones of the ephod and on the stones of the choshen (Shemos 28:9, 28:11,
28:21; 39:6, 39:14) and of *Kodesh laHashem* on the *tzitz* (Shemos 28:36,
39:30). *Gittin* 20a makes clear that this is embossing: the letters protrude
from their background, the opposite of etching or engraving, since the
background is cut or pressed down to leave the letters standing out. It can be
seen as "opening" (*poseach*) the background to reveal the letters (see *HaKsav
VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 28:9 and *Yerios Shlomo*, *choveres* 11, *yeriah* 31).
In Modern Hebrew *pituach* has come to mean development in a broader sense,
technological, educational or photographic.

The root ג-ל-ף does not occur in Biblical Hebrew but is used in rabbinic
sources. The standard Targumic Aramaic for *pituchei chosam* is a form of
*galif*, and where the Torah forbids making a *pesel* (Shemos 20:4) the
*Mechilta* explains that one may not make a *gelufah*. The Zohar (*Yisro* 84b)
uses *galif* of the writing on the Luchos, as does Hebrew poetry (for example, a
*yotzer* for the second day of Pesach by Rabbi Meshullam bar Kalonymus of
Lucca). Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation suggests that
ג-ל-ף is related to the Biblical Hebrew ג-ל-ב, barber (only once in Tanach,
Yechezkel 5:1), by the interchange of peh and beis: a barber cuts hair as the
engraver cuts into the writing surface. On the other hand Rabbi Ernest Klein (no
relation to the columnist), in his etymological dictionary of Hebrew, takes the
word as a Hebraization of Greek *glyphein*, to hollow out, engrave or carve, the
source of English *glyph*. Linguists in turn derive the Greek from
Proto-Indo-European *gleubh*, the ultimate source of English *cleave*, *clove*,
*cleft* and *clever*.

### resham (רשם)
Another word in Tanach for writing or noting. The root ר-ש-ם appears seven
times in the Aramaic of Daniel (5:24 to 25; 6:9 to 14) and only once in its
Hebrew (10:21). Rabbi Klein notes that Targum (to Shemos 32:16) renders *charut*
as *reshom* or *rashum*, and that for the tattoo, *kesoves ka'aka* (Vayikra
19:28), itself a form of *ketivah*, Targum Onkelos and Targum pseudo-Jonathan
combine cognates of *resham* and *charut*. A whole family of words comes from
the root: *roshem* (marking, inscribing), *reshimah* (list, roll, register,
record), *mirsham* (prescription), *rishmi* (official, formal). Some appear in
the Mishnah (*Shabbos* 12:3 to 4; *Beitzah* 4:7; *Sotah* 2:4; *Makkos* 3:6;
*Chullin* 10:3; *Parah* 9:1) and elsewhere in rabbinic literature; others are
Modern Hebrew coinages. The Arabic *rasama* (drawing, describing) and *rashama*
(marking, designating) are said to be loanwords from the Biblical Aramaic.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (in both *Yerios Shlomo* and *Cheshek Shlomo*) traces
the word to the biliteral ר-ש, whose chief offshoot is *rosh*, head. With a
final mem it becomes *resham*: writing only the main points, the headlines, of a
discourse, notes rather than a full transcript.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *ketivah* "write"; *charut* "engraved" or "graven," and
  "cut through" only where a text follows Pappenheim's reading; *charushah*
  "engraved" (Yirmiyahu's tablet of the heart); *chakuk* "inscribed"; *pasal*
  "hew" or "carve"; *pituach* "emboss" or "engrave in relief"; *resham* "note
  down," "record."
- Trap: "engrave" will absorb four of these. Where a passage sets them against
  each other, or where a derashah turns on the choice (Rabbi Moshe Zacuto's
  argument that the Torah wrote *charut* and not *charushah*), keep them
  separate in English and say what the cutting does: through the stone, into
  the stone, around the letters.
- Second trap: *pituach* is not engraving. The letters of the *tzitz* stood out
  from their background (*Gittin* 20a). Rendering *pituchei chosam* as "engraved
  like a signet" reverses the operation; "in relief, as a signet is cut" holds.
- *Charut* and *cheirus* are one consonantal text. Where a text quotes *al
  tikri charus ela cheirus*, print both English words and keep the Hebrew:
  "graven (*charus*); read it rather as freedom (*cheirus*)."
- *Chok* is "statute." Pappenheim's derivation from engraving is worth a note
  where a text leans on it, since on his reading a *chok* is the kind of law
  whose reason is not obvious and which must therefore be written and fixed.
- *Pesel* is "graven image" in a pasuk and "idol" in running prose; do not
  render the verb *pasal* as "sculpt," which is too artistic for Shemos 34:1.
