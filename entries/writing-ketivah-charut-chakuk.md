# Writing: ketivah / charut / charushah / chakuk / pasal / pituach

## Bottom line

*Ketivah* is the general **writing**, ink on a surface, about 260 times in
Tanach. The rest are all ways of cutting: *charut* is writing that goes
**through** the material (Pappenheim: from *chor*, a hole); *charushah* is
**engraving** understood as plowing a furrow; *chakuk* is **incising** grooves
with a stylus; *pasal* is **hewing** out of stone; *pituach* is the reverse
operation, **embossing**, where the background is cut away and the letters stand
proud. The trap: English "engrave" will take *charut*, *charushah*, *chakuk* and
*pasal* all at once, and will also wrongly take *pituach*, which is its
opposite.

## Distinctions

### ketivah (כתיבה)
Root כ-ת-ב, the ordinary word, and the one used for the Luchos (Shemos 31:18,
32:16, 34:28; Devarim 9:10, 10:4) and for writing a Sefer Torah in ink on
parchment (Devarim 31:9, 31:22, 31:24). On the Maharal's principle that a word's
first appearance holds its essence, the first is *k'sov zos zikaron ba'sefer*
after Amalek (Shemos 17:14): writing is what gives speech duration. Rabbi
Yitzchak Ginsburgh notes that the last occurrence of כ-ת-ב in Nevi'im runs the
same way, *vayikasev sefer zikaron* (Malachi 3:16). Rabbi Shem Tov Gaguine goes
further and infers from the absence of any writing in Bereishis that Moshe
Rabbeinu was the first ever to write, and the Amalek account the first thing
written.

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 34:27) groups כ-ת-ב with כ-ת-ף (shoulder), ק-ט-ף
(plucking off) and ק-ט-ב (killing). His point is about the danger in the medium:
what is written down is only what was written, so committing an idea to writing
detaches a part from the whole and can kill the whole. Hence the Written Torah
was never given alone; only the Written and the Oral together carry the full
picture of Hashem's will.

Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) defines כ-ת-ב with the word
*mechokek*, but that cannot mean he read every *ketivah* as engraving, since
*ketivah* covers writing a Sefer Torah in ink.

### charut (חרות) and cheret (חרט)
Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak take *charut* (Shemos 32:16) from ח-ר-ת, a
*hapax*. Its uniqueness is what invited Chazal's reading of *charus* as *cheirus*,
freedom (*Shabbos* 54a; *Shemos Rabbah* 32:1; *Shir HaShirim Rabbah* 8:3).
Pappenheim ties *charut* to *chor*, a hole, from the biliteral ח-ר: this is
writing that bores clean through, which is why the letters on the Luchos could
be read from both sides (Rashi to Shemos 32:15; *Shabbos* 104a). Rav Hirsch (to
Shemos 32:15) says the same. Rabbi Moshe Zacuto (to *Zohar Korach* 177a) had
made the *chor* claim centuries earlier, adding the argument from word choice:
had the Torah meant plain engraving it would have written *charushah*, not
*charut*. Rashi and Rashbam (to Shemos 32:16) instead read ח-ר-ת as ח-ר-ט, tav
and tes interchanging: the *cheret* is the stylus that shaped the Golden Calf
(Shemos 32:4) and the *cheret enosh* in which Yeshayahu was told to write
(Yeshayahu 8:1; the article prints Yeshayahu 32:4 alongside it, which is a
different pasuk). Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 41:5, Shemos 7:11) derives
*chartumim* from *cheret*, men expert in inscriptions cut with the stylus.
Either way ח-ר-ת and ח-ר-ט are writing pressed or cut deep into the material.

### charushah (חרושה)
Yirmiyahu 17:1, the sin of Yehudah *charushah al luach libam*. Root ח-ר-ש,
which Ibn Saruk gives five senses: muteness, deafness, thinking, craftsmanship,
and plowing. He assigns *charushah* to plowing, since engraving digs into its
surface as the plow digs the ground; Ibn Janach and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
agree. Ibn Saruk in practice defines *charut* by *charushah* and *charushah* by
*charut*, and Targum (to Yirmiyahu 17:1) renders *charushah* with an Aramaic
cognate of *charut*, which Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) justifies from
the regular Hebrew shin to Aramaic tav correspondence. Rabbi Shaul Goldman
groups all the ח-ר roots whose third letter comes from zayin, shin, tzadi, tes
and tav as hole-making: *charut*, *cheret*, *charitz* (ditch), *charuz*
(threading through a hole, later rhyming).

### chakuk (חקוק)
Pappenheim: from the biliteral ח-ק, digging small grooves with a metal stylus
into wood or stone to form letters. From there he derives *chok*, the statute
whose reason is not known: precisely because the rationale is not evident, the
rule must be written down and fixed or it will be forgotten. The *mechokek*
(Bereishis 49:10; Devarim 33:21; Shoftim 5:9, 5:14; Tehillim 60:9) is literally
the digger or engraver, as the root plainly means in Bamidbar 21:18. Rabbi Shaul
Goldman reads the link more abstractly: engraving draws a boundary around the
contours of a letter, as legislating draws a line, and *chok* in the sense of a
quota is likewise a boundary set around an amount. In liturgical Hebrew,
*chakuk* is the word Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi uses in *Yom Shabbason* for the Shabbos
command inscribed on the Luchos.

### pasal (פסל)
Strictly hewing, not writing. The verb פ-ס-ל appears six times in Tanach, four
of them for Moshe carving the second Luchos (Shemos 34:1, 34:4; Devarim 10:1,
10:3), once for hewing stones for the Beis HaMikdash (Melachim I 5:32) and once
for hewing an idol (Chavakuk 2:18). Most occurrences of the root are the nouns
*pesel* and *pesilim*. The shavings struck off in hewing are *pasul*,
disqualified from the finished object, and since an idol is itself disqualified
from any ritual use, the idol too is a *pesel*. Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 4:16)
groups פ-ס-ל with פ-צ-ל (*pitzel*, peeling) and ב-צ-ל (*batzal*, onion), all
removal of outer layers.

### pituach (פיתוח) and galif (גליף)
*Pituchei chosam* on the shoham stones of the ephod and on the *tzitz* (Shemos
28:9, 28:11, 28:21, 28:36; 39:6, 39:14, 39:30). *Gittin* 20a makes clear that
this is embossing: the background is cut down so the letters protrude, the
reverse of engraving. *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* (to Shemos 28:9) and *Yerios
Shlomo* (*choveres* 11, *yeriah* 31) read it as "opening" (*poseach*) the
background to reveal the letters. The Aramaic ג-ל-ף is Targum's standard
rendering of *pituchei chosam*, and the *Mechilta* (to Shemos 20:3) glosses
*pesel* as *gelufah*; the Zohar (*Yisro* 84b) uses *galif* of the Luchos. Rabbi
Yehoshua Steinberg ties ג-ל-ף to ג-ל-ב, the barber of Yechezkel 5:1, peh and
beis interchanging: the barber cuts hair as the engraver cuts the writing
surface.

### resham (רשם)
Seven times in the Aramaic of Daniel (5:24 to 25; 6:9 to 14) and once in its
Hebrew (10:21). Targum renders *charut* of Shemos 32:16 as *reshom*, and the
Targumim render the tattoo of *kesoves ka'aka* (Vayikra 19:28) with a
combination of ר-ש-ם and ח-ר-ת. Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo* and *Cheshek
Shlomo*) traces it to the biliteral ר-ש, whose chief offshoot is *rosh*, head:
with a final mem it is writing down only the heads of a discussion, its
headlines, rather than the whole of it. The later family follows from that:
*roshem*, *reshimah* (a list), *rishmi* (official), and in the Mishnah
(*Shabbos* 12:3 to 4; *Beitzah* 4:7; *Sotah* 2:4; *Makkos* 3:6; *Chullin* 10:3;
*Parah* 9:1).

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *ketivah* "write"; *charut* "incised through" or
  "graven"; *charushah* "engraved" (Yirmiyahu's tablet of the heart);
  *chakuk* "inscribed"; *pasal* "hew" or "carve"; *pituach* "engrave in relief"
  or "emboss"; *resham* "note down," "record."
- Trap: "engrave" will absorb four of these. Where a passage sets them against
  each other, or where a derashah turns on the choice (Rabbi Moshe Zacuto's
  argument that the Torah wrote *charut* and not *charushah*), keep them
  separate in English and say what the cutting does: through the stone, into the
  stone, around the letters.
- Second trap: *pituach* is not engraving. The letters of the *tzitz* stood out
  from their background (*Gittin* 20a). Rendering *pituchei chosam* as "engraved
  like a signet" reverses the operation; "in relief, as a signet is cut" holds.
- *Charut* and *cheirus* are one consonantal text. Where a sefer quotes *al
  tikri charus ela cheirus*, print both English words and keep the Hebrew:
  "graven (*charus*), read it rather as freedom (*cheirus*)."
- *Chok* keeps its transliteration in a mussar or halachic context. Pappenheim's
  derivation from engraving is worth a note where a sefer leans on it, since it
  explains why a *chok* is the kind of law that must be written and fixed.
- *Pesel* is "graven image" in a pasuk and "idol" in running prose; do not
  render the verb *pasal* as "sculpt," which is too artistic for Shemos 34:1.
