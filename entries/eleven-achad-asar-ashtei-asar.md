# Eleven: achad-asar / ashtei-asar

## Bottom line

Both mean eleven, and both stand in the opening pesukim of Devarim: "eleven
(*achad asar*) days from Chorev... in the eleventh (*ashtei asar*) month"
(Devarim 1:2-3). On Rabbi Klein's tentative reading of *Peirush HaRokeach*,
*achad asar* is **eleven** and *ashtei asar* **eleventh**, though he concedes
that this does not pan out in every case in Tanach. Rabbi Meir Mintzberg's
distinction is that *achad asar* is eleven as a number of its own and *ashtei
asar* is eleven as an extension of ten; he points out that *ashtei asar* never
takes the definite article, while every other number does.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| אחד עשר | achad asar | eleven | an independent number; takes *ha-* (Rabbi Meir Mintzberg) |
| עשתי עשר | ashtei asar | eleventh | eleven as a swelling of ten; never takes *ha-* (Rabbi Meir Mintzberg) |
| עשתי | ashtei | the disputed element | "upon twelve" (Ibn Janach), "born of ten" (Ibn Ezra), "bloated" (Mintzberg), or unknown (Radak); from *eshes*, compounded of units and tens (Pappenheim); one, in the tongue of Shinar (Rabbi Aharon Marcus) |

Counting the feminine forms there are four words: *achad asar* and *achat
esreh*, *ashtei asar* and *ashtei esreh*. *Ashtei asar* and *ashtei esreh*
appear nineteen times in Tanach, *achad asar* and *achat esreh* thirteen.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What ashtei means.**

1. **Upon twelve.** Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach: *ashtei asar* contracts *al*,
   "upon," with *shteim asar*, twelve, eleven being, as he understands it, the
   number "on top" of twelve.
2. **Born of ten.** Ibn Ezra disagrees: *au contraire*, twelve is "on top" of
   eleven and not the reverse. Moreover, on Ibn Janach's account the masculine
   should be *ashnei asar*, since the masculine twelve is *shnei asar*/*shneim
   asar* and not *shteim esreh*, yet there is no such term, only *ashtei asar*
   and *ashtei esreh*, which casts a shadow of doubt over the proposal. Ibn
   Ezra's own reading takes
   *ashtei* as a cognate of *ashtanosav*, "his plans" (Tehillim 146:4): just as
   plans are born out of one's thoughts, eleven is born out of ten, so *ashtei
   asar* literally means that which is born out of ten. He admits that a great
   secret lies behind the connection of ten to eleven, and does not elaborate.
3. **A bloated ten.** Rabbi Meir Mintzberg: *eshes* denotes an overly distended
   body (Rashi to Shir HaShirim 5:14 and Yirmiyahu 5:28; Yoma 34b; Menachos
   28a), so *ashtei asar* is a swollen ten.
4. **Unknown.** Radak rejects both Ibn Janach and Ibn Ezra and concludes that
   the derivation is beyond us.

Ibn Janach ties eleven to twelve and Ibn Ezra ties it to ten, which may have
interesting implications for Jewish numerology.

**When each form is used.** *Peirush HaRokeach*, in what Rabbi Klein calls a
somewhat cryptic explanation: where eleven is invoked with all the numbers before
it included, the text says *achad asar*; where only the eleventh member of a set
is meant and the rest are disregarded, it says *ashtei asar*. Rabbi Klein is not
quite sure what this means, but suggests that perhaps the Rokeach is drawing the distinction
between the ordinal eleventh (*ashtei asar*) and the numeral eleven (*achad
asar*), on the model of *rishon* against *echad* and *revi'i* against *arba*.

In support of that suggestion he notes that archaeologists found the word
*ashtei* meaning "first" in Ugaritic, in a funerary inscription concerning
Niqmaddu, king of Ugarit; linguists hold Ugaritic and Hebrew to be somewhat
similar languages, both Semitic, so the parallel is not irrelevant. Nevertheless,
he concedes that if one tries to apply this approach to every case in
Tanach, it simply does not pan out.

Rabbi Meir Mintzberg points out a fact of grammar: *ashtei asar* never
carries the definite article *ha-*, though every other number does, *achad asar*
and *achat esreh* among them, because on his reading it names no number with
standing of its own, only ten carried one further. Rabbi Klein adds that this
might actually be what the Rokeach intended by an independent against a dependent
eleven.

## Distinctions

### achad asar (אחד עשר) / achat esreh (אחת עשרה)
Literally "one ten," built transparently from *echad*. Eleven counted as a
number: eleven days from Chorev (Devarim 1:2). Takes the definite article
normally.

### ashtei asar (עשתי עשר) / ashtei esreh (עשתי עשרה)
The opaque form, and the more common one in Tanach. Used of the eleventh month
(Devarim 1:3); for *Peirush HaRokeach*, of the eleventh in a series where the
others are not in view.
Never prefixed with *ha-*.

The Gemara (*Sanhedrin* 29a): Chizkiyah derives that whoever adds, subtracts,
from *lo sochlu mimenu velo sigu bo* (Bereishis 3:3); Rav Mesharshiya from
*amasayim vachetzi orko* (Shemos 25:10); and Rav Ashi from *ashtei esreh yerios*
(Shemos 26:7), where adding an ayin to *shtei esreh* makes eleven of twelve.

The Zohar (Terumah 164b) brings *ashtei asar* as a case of adding that
subtracts, *kol hamosif gorea*; and see HaKsav VeHaKabbalah to Devarim 33:16.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus (*Keses HaSofer* p. 358): *ashtei* means one in the language
of Shinar.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo* s.v. עש): *ashtei asar* (Shemos 26:7;
Bamidbar 7:72) is from *eshes*, matter compounded of different kinds. Eleven is
the first number compounded of two kinds, units and tens, and ten, the first of
the tens, is itself compound while one, the first of the units, is simple; so
the sum is called *ashtei asar*, *ashtei* hinting at the one added to ten, which
makes the whole like *eshes*, compounded of unlike things.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: אחד עשר = "eleven," עשתי עשר = "eleventh," and check the context
  before overriding, since the correlation is strong but Rabbi Klein concedes it
  does not hold everywhere.
- Trap: English "eleven" will absorb both, and in Devarim 1:2-3 that is exactly
  the loss, since the two forms stand two pesukim apart and the shift is the
  only signal the text gives. Render the first "eleven days" and the second "the
  eleventh month," and the Hebrew's own distinction comes through in English
  without a note.
- Where a derashah turns on *ashtei*, do not translate the element at all; give
  the Hebrew and the reading being used, since Ibn Janach's "upon twelve" and
  Ibn Ezra's "born of ten" point in opposite directions and the choice is the
  derashah.
- In a list of ordinals, keep *ashtei asar* in the ordinal series ("the tenth,
  the eleventh, the twelfth") rather than letting it fall back to a cardinal.
