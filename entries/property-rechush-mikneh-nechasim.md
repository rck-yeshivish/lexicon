# Property: rechush / mikneh / nechasim

## Bottom line

*Rechush* is **property** or **possessions**, the movable goods a man has
gathered. *Mikneh* is not property at large but **livestock**, the herds that
were the ancient world's measure of wealth. *Nechasim*, always plural, is
**assets**, the word the Mishnah uses where Tanach would say *rechush* or
*mikneh*. The trap: *mikneh* shares the root of *kinyan* and looks like a general
word for what one has acquired, but unless context forces otherwise it is herds.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| רכוש | rechush | property, possessions | movables, gold and silver; a hypernym for all assets (Radak), or inanimate movables only (Rav Hirsch) |
| מקנה | mikneh | livestock | specifically herds, a man's chief acquisition (Ramban, Radak) |
| נכסים | nechasim | assets | plural always; the Mishnah's standard word; from *kisui*, hidden (Bamidbar Rabbah 22:8) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**How *rechush* and *mikneh* relate.** Three views:

1. **Hypernym and hyponym.** Radak (to Bereishis 12:5, 14:11): *rechush*
   includes gold, silver, other movables, and *mikneh* too, so livestock are one
   kind of *rechush*. Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis 12:5) *seems* to explain that
   *rechush* means the same as *mikneh*, or at least can sometimes mean the
   same as *mikneh*.
2. **Mutually exclusive.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 12:5, 13:2, 31:18):
   *rechush* is inanimate movable belongings and *mikneh* is livestock, neither
   including the other. His reason: domesticated animals recognize the owner who
   acquired them (Yeshayahu 1:3) and will follow him from place to place of
   their own volition, whereas inanimate *rechush* the owner has to take and
   move himself.
3. **Animals against people.** Rabbi Menachem ben Shlomo (c. 1140), *Midrash
   Sechel Tov* (to Bereishis 31:18, 36:6-7), who *seems* to explain that
   *mikneh* is animal property and *rechush* human property, slaves and
   maidservants. The column offers this as an explanation that "can be gleaned"
   from him, not as his stated position.

The column does not adjudicate between the three. This guide's working default is
Radak's, which is the safe reading where a verse carries only one of the two;
where both stand together, Rav Hirsch's contrast is what makes the pair say
something.

**Where *nechasim* comes from.** Bamidbar Rabbah 22:8 and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
take it from KAF-SAMECH, whose core sense for Pappenheim is placing one thing on
top of another. Rabbi Aharon Marcus contends that *neches*, the singular, is
likewise derived from *reches*, ostensibly through the interchangeability of
REISH and NUN, making it a cousin of *rechush*. He notes that *nechasim* has
similar-sounding cognates in Phoenician (Canaanite) and Akkadian, languages whose
speakers were in close contact with Hebrew from an early time, and that
*nechasim* first appears in Sefer Yehoshua, written once the Jews had reached the
Holy Land and could theoretically have been influenced by its Canaanite
inhabitants. On this basis Marcus notes that the tie between *nechasim* and
*rechush* is especially poignant for Yehoshua himself, who as an Ephraimite
pronounced the *shin* like a *sin* (Shoftim 12:6), so he may have said *rechus*.
Marcus adds that in many ancient languages the letters equivalent to *shin* and
*lamed* are interchangeable, which leads him to claim that the root of *rechush*
is also the original source of ר-כ-ל, whence *rochel*, a merchant.

## Distinctions

### rechush (רכוש)
Thirty-three times in Tanach, only in Bereishis, Bamidbar, Daniel, Ezra, and
Divrei HaYamim; the verb *rachash*, to accrue property, is in Bereishis alone.
Seven instances fall in Lech Lecha (Bereishis 12:5, 13:6, 14:11, 14:12, 14:16,
14:21, 15:14), the highest concentration in any parashah. In Tanach *rechush*
and *mikneh* always appear in the singular. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 12:5), and
Solomon Rabinowitz (d. 1943) with him, argues that *rechush*, with a final SHIN,
is related to *reches* with a SIN, "tying" or "attaching" (see Rashi to Shemos
28:28): the term alludes to the pursuit of wealth, whereby a man seeks to accrue
as much as he can and tie all of it together into one net worth. The Hebrew
scholar Naftali Torczyner/Tur-Sinai (1886-1973) offers a slightly different
approach, taking *rechush* as ultimately derived from *rechesh*, a type of horse:
since livestock was the crux of a man's possessions in the ancient world,
*rechush* later expanded into a general term for everything he owned. *Ohalei
Yehudah* understands that *rechesh* itself relates back to *reches*, because a
harness or stirrup was tied to the horse for riding.

### mikneh (מקנה)
Over seventy-five times in Tanach, the commonest of the three. Root
KUF-NUN(-HEI), which also yields *miknah* and *kinyan*, acquisition. Ramban (to
Bereishis 14:18, 34:23) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the word names livestock
precisely because livestock were a person's chief acquisition, all the more so in
the ancient world, where how much livestock a man held was the measuring stick of
how rich he was. Dr. Gerald Leonard Cohen (Missouri University of Science and
Technology) notes in his *Comments on Etymology* that the importance of livestock
can be seen in the fact that of the first three letters of the alphabet, two
refer to domesticated animals: *aleph* means "ox," and *gimmel* is related to
*gamal*, "camel." Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
gives KUF-NUN the core sense of a minimal dwelling, whence *ken*, a bird's nest,
and *kinyan*, which shifts an item from one domain into another; *mikneh* is then
either the commercially central asset (a beast serves for wool, milk, hide,
offspring, meat, and field work, where produce is only eaten) or the animal kept
in a barn rather than a home.

### nechasim (נכסים)
Seven times in Tanach, always plural: once in Yehoshua (22:8), twice in Koheles
(5:18, 6:2), twice in Divrei HaYamim II (1:11 and 1:12), and twice in the Aramaic
of Ezra (6:8, 7:26). In rabbinic Hebrew it is the standard term for total assets:
*rechush* and *mikneh* never appear in the Mishnah, and *nechasim* runs through
it (Peah 3:6-8, Kesubos 8:1-2, Bava Basra 8:3-7, Avos 2:7, and dozens more).
Bamidbar Rabbah 22:8 assumes it is related to *kisui*, covered or hidden: assets
are *nechasim* "because they are hidden from this one and revealed to that one,"
that is, material possessions are held only for a time and do not inherently
become an inseparable part of the man, the way physical strength or wisdom might
be said to.
Rabbi Shmuel de Ucedah (*Midrash Shmuel* to Avos 2:8) sharpens it: a man often
spends much of his life accruing riches, then dies, and others end up reaping the
benefit of his labor, so there is always a hidden side to materialism, the one
who toils not always being the one who enjoys the fruit. He closes the discussion
cynically: "many people gather up money for the benefit of their wives' [future]
husbands." Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit, an early twentieth-century exegete,
offers two more ways of tying *nechasim* to *kisui*: a man's financial worth is
hidden from those who meet him, since net worth is not apparent to one who does
not know; or, where a man has all sorts of flaws but is wealthy, his wealth
covers up his shortcomings so that people relate to him as though he did not have
them (this second explanation is already found in *Shoresh Yesha*). Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim likewise traces *nechasim* to the biliteral KAF-SAMECH, whose core
meaning is placing something on top of something else: *kisui*; *kesus*,
clothing, which covers the skin; *kos*, a cup that has a cover; *kisei*, a chair
that has a cover; *kis*, a pocket, a covered container; *meches*, tax, monies
that fall into the government's *kis*; and *michsah*, the central pot of funds
into which the partners in a korban Pesach pool their monies (Shemos 12:4).
*Nechasim* is then what one puts into one's pocket, whether literally or
figuratively; or, alternatively, what one covers and hides in order to hold onto
it and protect it; or, finally, what covers one's needs and necessities so that
he does not need the aid of others to survive.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "property" absorbs all three at once. Keep "livestock" for
  *mikneh* wherever the passage lets you; a verse with both *rechush* and
  *mikneh* is drawing a distinction, and "property and property" is no
  translation. Bereishis 13:6, the herdsmen's quarrel, has *rechush* in the wide
  sense: render "possessions."
- *Nechasim* in a Mishnah or Gemara is "assets" or "estate," not "property"; the
  article's point that it is the standard rabbinic term for a man's *total*
  assets wants the financial word.
- Where a derashah turns on *nechasim* and *kisui*, transliterate and gloss.
