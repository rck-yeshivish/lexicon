# Laughter: tzchok / s'chok

## Bottom line

*Tzchok* and *s'chok* are the same word: **laughter**, with sin and tzadi
interchanging, and the lexicographers find no difference of meaning between
them. Both run the full range from laughing and playing to mocking and deriding,
and context, not the root, decides which is meant. The whole discussion of
*Yitzchak* against *Yischak* is therefore not lexical but homiletical and
kabbalistic.

## Distinctions

### tzchok (צחוק) and s'chok (שחוק): one word
Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim all hold that *s'chok*
and *tzchok* mean exactly the same thing. Sin and tzadi are interchangeable,
being formed at the same place in the mouth (see also *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to
Shemos 25:29). The Yerushalmi (*Berachos* 1:6) assumes as much: Yitzchak's name,
alone among the Avos, was never changed, because Hashem Himself gave it, which
takes *Yischak* and *Yitzchak* as one name.

### the core sense
Pappenheim: the root is laughter as an involuntary response to something from
outside, whether a physical tickle or something funny. Both words carry the
favorable senses (laughing, playing, enjoying) and the unfavorable ones
(deriding, mocking). Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 21:6) shows the two as one idea:
mockery is the use of sarcasm and irony to make someone laugh at a person or a
thing. See also Malbim to Shoftim 16:25, where Shimshon is brought out to
"play."

Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 17:17) further ties
*tzchok* and *s'chok* to *tzaakah* and *zaakah*, crying out, on the
interchange of ches and ayin. All four, he says, are man's reaction to a gap
between what he sees and what he expected: a child in an adult's wig makes us
laugh, an old man in a diaper makes us weep, and both are the same reflex
reconciling sight with expectation. Pappenheim makes a similar point, but
without comparing the etymologies of the words involved.

### the sense at Yitzchak's birth
When Avraham laughs at the news (Bereishis 17:17) and Sarah after him
(Bereishis 18:12), and when Sarah says *tzchok asah li Elokim, kol hashomea
yitzchak li* (Bereishis 21:6), Onkelos and Rashi take the laughter as an outward
expression of joy, not derision. Hence the name Yitzchak, "he will laugh"
(Bereishis 17:19).

### Yischak (ישחק): four places, and what is made of them
The name *Yitzchak* appears 108 times in Tanach; four times he is called
*Yischak*: Yirmiyahu 33:26 (the seed of Avraham, Yischak and Yaakov); Amos 7:9
(the high places of Yischak) and 7:16 (the house of Yischak); Tehillim 105:9
(Hashem's oath to Yischak). That last is customarily said at a bris, while its
parallel, Divrei HaYamim I 16:16, said in the daily tefillos, reads *Yitzchak*.
Since the words are one word, the column turns to more esoteric readings of the
two spellings:

Rabbeinu Bachaya (to Bereishis 17:19, Bamidbar 32:3): the name is really
*Yitzchak*, and the nevi'im sometimes wrote it with a sin to hint that Yitzchak
embodies *din*, likened to a raging fire, *aish*. The column then adds on its
own account that Pappenheim derives *aish* from the monoliteral shin, which it
takes as solidifying the connection. Rabbi Shmuel Feivish Kahane, a seventeenth
century sage, in *Leket Shmuel*, holds precisely the reverse: the name should
have been *Yischak*, but a sin would have pointed at a raging judgment from
which there would be no recovery, so the Torah writes a tzadi.

A further approach has it that the name, or at least its spelling, changed or
will change. Rabbi Yehuda Moscato (1530 to 1593): in the future the name will
change from *Yitzchak* to *Yischak*, since sin stands for *sasson* and
*simchah*, and it appears only four times in Tanach as a hint that it comes into
its own only once the four exiles are complete. Rabbi Shmuel Borenstein (1855 to
1926) uses a variation of that to settle an apparent contradiction: *Berachos*
61b and *Sefer Yetzirah* ch. 5 imply that the spleen answers to *s'chok*, while
*Tikkunei HaZohar* (*tikkun* 84) associates the spleen with sadness. There are
two laughters, he answers, the legitimate and holy against the illegitimate and
impure; the holy is reserved for the future, *az yimalei s'chok pinu* (Tehillim
126:2), while until then most laughter is an illusion masking sadness and
despondency, and it is that laughter of this world, itself a cover for sadness,
that the spleen answers to. He concludes that in the future, when *s'chok* will
mean laughter in its legitimate sense, the name will change from *Yitzchak*,
tied to the holy laughter alone, to *Yischak*, drawn from the form of laughter
that had been the unholy one. The Arizal (*Shaar HaPesukim* to Yeshayahu 52:3)
also offers an explanation of the switch.

In other sources it seems that the original name was *Yischak* and was later
changed to *Yitzchak*: when Yisrael cried out in Mitzrayim, Hashem asked the
Avos which of them would give up the numerical value of his name to save them,
and Yitzchak stepped forward and gave up the sin (300) for a tzadi (90), a
difference of 210, which is why they were redeemed after an exile of 210 years
(cited in numerous works, among them *Imrei Noam* to Shemos 6:2, which the
column notes is called the *Rosh* to Shemos 6:1; *Siddur Rokeach*; *Be'er Mayim
Chaim* to Bereishis 15:13; *Leket Shmuel* to Bereishis 25:19). The Vilna Gaon
(to *Sifra d'Tzniusa*, end of ch. 3) writes that each of the three Avos had a
lower name and a higher name; the column applies this to Yitzchak, alongside
Avram and Avraham and Yaakov and Yisrael or Yeshurun, giving him two names, or
at least two spellings of the one name, *Yitzchak* (lower) and *Yischak*
(higher). *Sefer HaShem*, ascribed to the Rokeach, notes that Avraham, Yischak
and Yeshurun together total 2448, the year of Matan Torah, taking final mem as
600 and final nun as 700.

## Rendering guidance

- Default rendering for both spellings: "laughter," "laugh." Where the context
  is derision (*metzachek* of Yishmael, Shimshon made to perform), render
  "mock," "make sport," "make sport of," and note the root is the same word as
  the laughter of joy. Rav Hirsch's point is that Hebrew does not have two
  words here, and an English rendering that supplies two must say so.
- Trap: English "laugh" and "mock" will pull apart what Hebrew holds together,
  and conversely a reader shown "laugh" twice will miss that Sarah's laughter
  (Bereishis 18:12) and her *tzchok* at the birth (21:6) are differently read
  by the mefarshim. Take Onkelos and Rashi at 21:6 as joy expressed outwardly.
- The names stay as they are printed: *Yitzchak* where the text has a tzadi,
  *Yischak* where it has a sin. Never normalize one to the other, and never
  translate the difference; the four *Yischak* pesukim are the whole basis of
  every derashah above.
- *S'chok* in Tehillim 126:2 is "laughter" and belongs to the future; in mussar
  prose *s'chok* often means frivolity, and "levity" serves better there than
  "laughter."
