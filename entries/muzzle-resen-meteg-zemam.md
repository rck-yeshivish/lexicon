# Muzzle: resen / meteg / zemam (prumbia, afsar)

## Bottom line

*Resen* and *meteg* both name the gear that controls a beast, and what separates
them is disputed. For Rabbi Avraham Bedersi the *meteg* is a metal bit and the
*resen* a rope or a leather bit. Dr. David J. A. Clines takes a similar
approach: the *meteg* is the whole bridle, and the *resen* the mouthpiece or the
reins. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi holds the exact opposite of Bedersi: the
*meteg* is the leather strap and the *resen* the metal bit. The Aramaic *zemam*,
which Targum uses for both, is a **muzzle**, the device that closes a mouth.
*Prumbia* is the rabbinic **muzzle/halter**; *afsar* is the **reins**, by which
one takes legal possession of an animal. The noun *zemam* is distinct from its
homograph *zomem* or *zimah*, a plot or scheme, though Shadal, among others,
ties them: the scheme stays locked in the mind as the muzzle locks the mouth.

## Distinctions

### resen (רסן)
Five occurrences in Tanach, once as the place-name Resen (Bereishis 10:12) and
four as the mouthpiece (Yeshayahu 30:28, Iyov 30:11, Iyov 41:5, Tehillim 32:9).
- Radak (to Yeshayahu 30:28): a *resen* is what steers an animal where its rider
  wants it to go; in *Sefer HaShorashim* he adds a second aspect, that it serves
  as a muzzle blocking the animal's mouth, presumably (so Rabbi Klein reads him)
  so that it will not bite people or eat from other people's fields.
- Rashi (to Yeshayahu 30:28) glosses it with the rabbinic *prumbia*.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk, Yonah Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon and Radak take the root as
  ר-ס-ן.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim treats the final nun as non-essential and derives it
  from biliteral ר-ס, "breaking something off into smaller pieces" (compare
  *la'ros*, to drip or sprinkle, Yechezkel 46:14): the bit makes the animal
  drool, and the dripping saliva is a small thing broken off a bigger one. Rabbi
  Klein adds that the same meaning accounts for the Modern Hebrew *tarsis*,
  spray. The nouns *resisim* (Amos 6:11) and *resisei* (Shir HaShirim 5:2)
  likewise refer to smaller pieces broken off something bigger.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus applies the same root slightly differently: the bit
  bifurcates the space inside the mouth.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 2:2) connects *resen* to *roznim*, nobles, by the
  samech/zayin interchange: the *roznim* steer the state as the *resen* steers
  the beast.
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*) reads it as a portmanteau
  of *ratz* and *ein*, "no running."

### meteg (מתג)
Also five occurrences (Shmuel II 8:1, Melachim II 19:28, Yeshayahu 37:29,
Mishlei 26:3, Tehillim 32:9).
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): a long piece of metal set in the mouth to control
  the animal, and he notes that it is not built like a *resen*, without fully
  explaining how they differ.
- *Ohalei Yehuda* offers two etymologies by letter-interchange: gimmel for kuf,
  linking *meteg* to *matok* (sweet), since control of one's beast is desirable
  and enjoyable to the rider (Rabbi Klein adds his own version: just as a man
  wants to keep something sweet in his mouth, so does a rider want to keep the
  *meteg* in his horse's mouth); or mem for nun with gimmel for kuf, linking it
  to *nituk* (untie), since the *meteg*'s virtue is a knot that cannot easily be
  undone.
- Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg suggests *tugah* / *yagon* (distress), since the
  *meteg* leads by causing pain.
- Rabbi Amitai Ben-David notes the later Masoretic *meteg*, the short vertical
  stress mark, and the Modern Hebrew "switch" that took its name from the shape,
  as did certain kinds of worm.

### The difference between them
- Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*, the first work on Hebrew synonyms): *meteg* is a
  **metal** bit; *resen* is either a rope at the neck or a **leather** bit.
- A similar approach is favored by Dr. David J. A. Clines in his dictionary of
  Classical Hebrew: *meteg* is the entire **bridle** by which one directs an
  animal, while *resen* is specifically the **mouthpiece** joining the bridle to
  the animal, or the **reins**, the long strap held by the rider and attached to
  the bridle.
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) holds the exact opposite of
  Bedersi, *meteg* the leather strap and *resen* the metal bit, tying *meteg* to
  *metach* (stretching out) via gimmel/ches.

That they are close in meaning is seen from their pairing in Tehillim 32:9, and
Targum's rendering both by *zemam* (Yeshayahu 30:28, Iyov 30:11; Melachim II
19:28, Yeshayahu 37:29) suggests the same. In the one pasuk where both stand
together, Targum to Tehillim 32:9 distinguishes them: *meteg* becomes *zemama*,
*resen* becomes *prumbia* (Targum to Iyov 41:5 likewise renders *resen* as
*prumbia*).

### zemam (זמם)
The Aramaic and rabbinic term for the muzzle. The Torah forbids muzzling an
animal at its work (Devarim 25:4), and the Mishnah (*Terumos* 9:3), ruling that
one threshing *terumah* should hang a basket of the same species from the
animal's neck so that it not be restrained from eating, uses the verb *zomem*
for muzzling. *Bereishis Rabbah* 41:5, Targum Yonasan and Targum Yerushalmi (to
Bereishis 13:7), relating the tradition Rashi cites there, all use cognates of
*zemam* for the muzzle Avraham was particular to put on his animals, while Lot
was not as careful. The homograph *zomem* means to plot (Tehillim 37:12;
ascribed even to Hashem, Yirmiyahu 51:12, Eichah 2:17, Zecharyah 1:6; the
conspiring witnesses of Devarim 19:19 and the *eidim zomemim* of the Mishnah,
Bava Kamma 7:3, Sanhedrin 6:2, Makkos 1:3 and 1:6), and *zimah* is the
licentious scheme of Vayikra 18:17 and 20:14.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the core of biliteral ז-ם is "intellectual decision,"
  yielding *zamam*, *yuzma*, *zimah* (the mind's decision to do wrong), *nezem*
  (the nose-ring that entices toward *zimah*) and even *zman*, time being
  graspable only by the intellect; he does not connect the muzzle.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg opens the way to a bridge: an intellectual
  decision **closes off** the other options, and so *nezem* is the ring that
  must form a complete circle to be affixed on the wearer. Mecklenburg himself
  stops there; it is Rabbi Klein who adds, in his own voice, that *zemam* is by
  that logic what closes an animal's mouth, or alternatively that the *zemam* is
  fixed on the animal's face just as the *nezem* is fixed on a human face.
- Shadal (to Vayikra 18:17) offers an all-inclusive explanation of the whole ז-ם
  family: what typifies a *zamam* is that it stays within the realm of the mind,
  so that *zemamah* might typically be evil thoughts though it could in theory
  be good ones; *zimah* is the secret thought a man holds in and never puts into
  words, and lying with a woman and her daughter or granddaughter is called
  *zimah* because society holds it so heinous that one would keep such fantasies
  secret. So too, Shadal writes, the muzzle locks the animal's mouth against
  eating and biting.
- *Bereishis Rabbah* 75:9 reads the *zemam* of Tehillim 140:9 as a muzzle over
  Esav's face.
- Yevamos 37b parses *zimah* otherwise, as a portmanteau of *zu mah hi*, "what
  is she," speaking of the after-effects of promiscuity, which leave people's
  lineage in question for want of clear parentage.

### prumbia (פרומביא) and afsar (אפסר)
*Prumbia* appears in the Mishnah (*Shabbos* 5:1, *Keilim* 11:5) and in Bava
Kamma 17b, where an animal damages by means of the *prumbia* on its mouth;
Rashi uses it to gloss *resen*. Dr. Alexander Kohut identifies *prumbia* as a permutation of the Greek
*phorbeia*, the leather strap worn by players of the aulos, a pipe-like wind
instrument, to spare their lips and cheeks the strain of continuous blowing; in
Latin and English the accessory is a *capistrum*. Being something like a muzzle
or halter, it came in rabbinic parlance to be associated with an animal's
headgear. The rabbinic form carries an extra mem vowel, what linguists call
epenthetic vowel insertion. *Bereishis Rabbah* 45:7 has the variant *probi* or
*prochi* (depending on the edition), seemingly cognate with *prumbia*/*phorbeia*, in the aphorism about the
donkey's ears. *Afsar* (from
Persian) is the bit or reins; because taking hold of an animal's *afsar*
acquires the animal, the term carries a legal sense in the Bavli for the
mechanism by which anything is acquired (Kiddushin 27a-b, Bava Basra 53b,
Targum Yonasan to Bamidbar 19:2).

## Rendering guidance

- Tehillim 32:9, where both appear: "with bit and bridle" keeps them apart,
  following Bedersi's metal/leather split. Note in a footnote that *Otzar
  Nirdafim* reverses it rather than silently picking a side.
- Trap: English "bridle" will happily swallow *resen*, *meteg*, *prumbia* and
  *afsar* at once. Where a passage turns on the difference (a Mishnah listing
  the gear, a derashah on Bamidbar 23:16), pick a distinct English word per Hebrew
  word and hold it for the whole passage.
- Bamidbar 23:16, *vayasem davar b'fiv*: with Rashi and *Bamidbar Rabbah* 20:20
  the *davar* is a *resen*, so render "a bit in his mouth" and gloss it as the
  mouthpiece by which a rider controls a beast; "word" alone loses the image.
- Do not render the *zemam* group's two senses with one English word. The
  muzzle is "muzzle"; *zomem* is "plot" or "scheme"; keep *eidim zomemim*
  transliterated, since "conspiring witnesses" is a halachic term of art.
- *Zimah* in Vayikra is "licentiousness" or "depravity," not "plot," even
  though Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives it from the plotting sense.
