# Dancing: rikud / kirkur / pizuz

## Bottom line

*Rikud* is the general word for **dancing**, and per Rabbi Moshe
Tedeschi-Ashkenazi it names the joy that is the occasion for it rather than any
particular step. *Kirkur* (*micharker*) is the footwork: **whirling, capering**,
which Rabbi Yosef Kara glosses as circular motion. *Pizuz* (*mifazez*) is the
wild, fast part: **prancing** or **leaping about**, the hands and fingers moving
with the feet. The one thing not to get wrong: at Shmuel II 6:14 David is only
*micharker*, and at 6:16, through Michal's eyes, he is *mifazez umecharker*. The
added verb is the point of the story, so the English must differ across the two
pesukim; never render both as "danced."

## Distinctions

### rikud (ריקוד)
Root ר-ק-ד, nine times in Tanach, of people and also of calves (Tehillim 29:6),
goats (Yeshayahu 13:21) and mountains that "danced like rams" (Tehillim 114:4
and 114:6). "A time to mourn and a time to dance (*rekod*)" (Koheles 3:4).
Mishnaic: the Shabbos ban on dancing (*Beitzah* 5:2), the pious men dancing at
the *Simchas Beis HaSho'evah* (*Sukkah* 5:4), and the melachah of sifting
(*Shabbos* 7:2; also *Shevi'is* 5:9, *Gittin* 5:9, *Menachos* 11:3), presumably
because the side-to-side shake of a sieve looks like the side-to-side motion of
a dancer. The Yerushalmi (*Beitzah* 5:2) defines *rikud* precisely: a jump that
leaves one foot on the ground, against *kefitzah*, where both feet leave it.
Rabbi Aharon Marcus reads the root as *reish*, movement, plus *kuf*, fixed or
entrenched, which fits the Yerushalmi exactly: one foot moves, one stays down.
Rabbi Tedeschi-Ashkenazi: *rekidah* is dancing out of happiness, and he traces
ר-ק-ד to the two-letter ק-ד, burning, since a dancer rises and falls back like
a flame reaching upward.

A near-variant is *tiratzdun*, "why do you dance, O peaks" (Tehillim 68:17),
root ר-צ-ד, a *hapax*. Menachem Ibn Saruk and Radak both define it with
*rekidah* (see also *Megillah* 29a); Tedeschi-Ashkenazi calls it a morphological
variant of ר-ק-ד with *tzadi* for *kuf*, as *tzalui* stands beside *kalui*.

The Gemara's uses of *rikud* are worth knowing: the Satan dances between the
horns of a black ox coming out of the marsh in Nisan (*Berachos* 33a, *Pesachim*
112b); the *yetzer hara* for idolatry is "still dancing among us" (*Yoma* 69b,
*Sanhedrin* 64a); the Angel of Death dances ahead of the women returning from a
funeral. Since the Satan, the *yetzer hara* and the Angel of Death are one
(*Bava Basra* 16a), the three may be one image: these forces exulting when they
succeed in their mission.

### kirkur (כרכור), the verb micharker (מכרכר)
Quadriliteral כ-ר-כ-ר, twice in Tanach, both of David before the Aron.
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim works from the two-letter כ-ר, digging: a *kar* is a
field whose boundary was dug out as a trench, *karim* are the fat animals that
graze on it, and *micharker* is the way such well-fed, free-ranging animals
appear to dance. Tedeschi-Ashkenazi reads the root as a compound of *kar* and
*rach* (soft, young) reversed: young *karim* have energy to burn and jump about.
Rabbi Yosef Kara (to Shmuel II 6:16) glosses *micharker* with a form of *sivuv*,
going around, so circular motion. Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (*HaMadrich
HaMaspik*) derives the dancing word from *kar*, a young camel, whose gait looks
like dancing; Ibn Janach and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) and Rabbeinu Avraham
ben Shlomo (to Shmuel II 6:16) reverse the direction: swift animals are called
*kar* or *kirkarim* because their movement resembles a dancer's.

### pizuz (פיזוז), the verb mifazez (מפזז)
Root פ-ז-(ז), which also yields *paz*, fine gold, and a sense of spreading or
strengthening. Targum (to Shmuel II 6:16) renders *mifazez* with the Aramaic
form of *miraked*. Rambam (*Hilchos Lulav* 8:13) describes the *Simchas Beis
HaSho'evah* with "*mefazezin* and *mecharkerin*," and then, of the sages and
great men joining in (8:14), switches to *merakedin*. Pappenheim (*Cheishek
Shlomo*) takes פ-ז as quick motion, covering both dancing and shining: gold
sparkles as though the light bouncing off it were dancing in every direction,
and *mifazez* is wild dancing in every direction. Rabbi Yehuda Aryeh of
Carpentras: *paz* is the gold that stands above the other metals, matching the
upward gestures of a dancer. *Metzudas David* (to Shmuel II 6:16): a fast dance.
Rabbi Zev Atlas: the Torah withholds *mifazez* at 6:14 and supplies it at 6:16
because the careless, wild element of the dance is exactly what galled Michal.
The sense of haste in the root is standard: the Gemara reads Yaakov's rebuke of
Reuven by it (*Shabbos* 55b on Bereishis 49:4), and a Sadducee sneered at Israel
as an *ama peziza*, a hasty nation, for saying *na'aseh* before *nishma*
(*Kesubos* 112a, *Shabbos* 88a, on Shemos 24:7). Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Ho'il
Moshe* to Shmuel II 6:16) links פ-ז-ז to פ-צ-צ, to burst or scatter: the dancer
seems to explode from place to place, and beaten gold sends fragments flying.

Radak (to Bereishis 49:24; the article prints 39:24) reads *vayafozu zero'ei
yadav* of Yosef as strengthening, and applies the same to David: dancers brace
their belt or garments when they dance with all their strength. Rabbi Tanchum
HaYerushalmi applies the Yosef pasuk to his strengthening himself against the
*yetzer hara* with the wife of Potifar.

Rashbam (to Bereishis 49:24) gives the cleanest division: *mifazez* is the
hands, *micharker* is the feet. Rabbi Tanchum adds that *mifazez* includes
moving the hands and fingers in time with the feet and snapping to the rhythm of
the song, and *Bamidbar Rabbah* 4:20 hears in David's *mifazez* the jingling of
his golden garments as he swayed.

## Rendering guidance

- Shmuel II 6:14 "David was whirling with all his might before Hashem"; 6:16
  "leaping and whirling before Hashem." The pair must be two English words, and
  the extra verb must appear only in the second pasuk.
- Trap: English "dance" absorbs all three. Keep "dance" for *rikud*, which is
  the neutral term, and force "whirl" or "caper" and "leap" or "prance" for the
  other two wherever they stand together (Rambam, *Hilchos Lulav* 8:13).
- Second trap: Rambam's 8:13 against 8:14 is a register distinction; if the
  sages "danced" while the crowd "pranced and whirled," the English carries what
  the Hebrew carried. Do not level the two halachos.
- *Rikud* in the halachos of Shabbos is a term of art. Where the Yerushalmi's
  definition is in play, render it "dancing (a step that keeps one foot on the
  ground)" and keep *kefitzah* as "jumping," both feet off.
- *Mifazez* in a mussar context (*ama peziza*, Reuven's *pachaz*) is "rash" or
  "impetuous," not "dancing." The root carries speed; the passage decides
  whether the speed is joy or recklessness.
