# Root צ-ב (splendor, desire, consent, army): tzvi / tzivyon / tzava

## Bottom line

*Tzvi* carries three senses: **splendor, glory, beauty**; **desire, what is
wanted**; and the animal, **deer** or **gazelle**. Rashi (to Yeshayahu 4:2)
reads beauty, Radak desire and glory. *Tzivyon* is
either **consent** or **ideal form**, depending on the commentator. *Tzava* is a
**legion**, a body joined by one shared want. *Eretz hatzvi* is "the Desired
Land" on the plain reading, "the Land of the Deer" in the derashos of Chazal.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |

| צבא | tzava | legion, army | a group united by one want (Rav Hirsch, Rav Friedlander) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What tzvi means at its core.**

- Rashi (to Yeshayahu 4:2): beauty.
- Radak (there, and to Yeshayahu 13:19): desire and glory, Bavel being the
  *tzvi* of kingdoms as the most desired place and the pride of the Chaldeans.

At Yechezkel 7:20, where the people took the "*tzvi* of Hashem's ornament," the
Beis HaMikdash, and turned it into an abominable place of idolatry: Targum
renders *tzvi* there as *chedvah*, "delight," and Radak as *pe'er* and *yofi*.
At Yirmiyahu 3:19, the "heritage of *tzvi*," Targum again has "delight" and
Radak an expression of desire.

**Why Eretz Yisrael is eretz hatzvi.** The Land is described as the most *tzvi*
of all lands (Yechezkel 20:6).
- The Ritva, Rabbi Yom Tov of Seville (1260 to 1330): the most beautiful of
  lands.
- Radak, on the other hand: the most splendid and desirable, because it has the
  most perfect, balanced climate.
- Ibn Ezra (to Daniel 8:9): simply "the *tzvi*," most desirable of all.
- Ralbag there: desired by *all* people; Midrash *Shocher Tov* (to Tehillim 5):
  all the nations of the world desire it and want it.
- Rav Saadia Gaon (to Daniel 11:16): Hashem wants, that is chooses, that land to
  rest His Presence there.
- Against these, Chazal read *tzvi* as the deer (Kesubos 112a; *Sifrei* to
  Devarim 11:10): as a deer's hide will not stretch back over it once flayed, so
  the Land's produce exceeds what it could hold; or, as the deer is swift, so
  its produce ripens quickly (*Shemos Rabbah* §32:2).

**What tzivyon means.** Rosh Hashanah 11a and Chullin 60a: Hashem created each
element of nature according to its *tzivyon*.

- Rashi and Rabbeinu Gershom: its will, having been asked and having consented.
- Alternatively, the Ritva follows Rabbi Nosson of Rome's *Sefer HeAruch*: an
  expression of beauty, each element of creation fashioned according to its most
  beautiful form.

(In Modern Hebrew *tzivyon* means a thing's "nature" or "character.")

## Distinctions

### tzvi (צבי)
The *haftarah* of Shemos has Yeshayahu mourning that the *tzvi tiferes* of the
Kingdom of Israel has withered (Yeshayahu 28:1, 28:4), while Hashem remains an
*ateres tzvi* for the righteous remnant (28:5). The Land bears three parallel
titles of desire: *eretz chefetz* (Malachi 3:12), *eretz chemdah* (Yirmiyahu
3:19; Zechariah 7:14; Tehillim 106:24), and *eretz hatzvi* (Daniel 11:16,
11:41). Since *chefetz* and *chemdah* are forms of desire, so is *tzvi*.

### tzvi as deer (צבי)
The kosher wild animal of Devarim 14:5.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim links it to desire: deer are ordinarily tame but among
  the most desirous of mammals, and can turn wild and dangerous in their mating
  season.
- Rabbi Klein offers his own suggestion: deer are known for running swiftly, so
  they are generally free to follow their heart's desires; and since *tzvi* is
  also related to beauty, the deer-comparison of Shir HaShirim (2:9, 2:17, 8:14)
  is a complimentary term of endearment, which likely refers to the deer's sleek
  physique along with its beautiful antlers, although usually only the males
  have antlers, except for reindeer.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) posits that the deer is called a *tzvi*,
  from the צ-ב root, because of its elegant, stately way of standing.

In the switch from Hebrew to Aramaic the *tzadi* commonly transforms into a
*tes*, so *tzvi* becomes *tvi*; and the Semitic ט-ב root in Hebrew and Aramaic
means "good," goodness being something both desirable and beautiful.

### the two-letter root צ-ב
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the root as "standing" (Bereishis 24:13; Shemos
  2:4; Devarim 29:9). *Yatziv* stands on established bases; *tzvi* as desire is
  what remains in a man's thoughts as though constantly standing before him. He
  derives with these: *matzeivah*, a monument erected to mark a tomb or a cultic
  place of idolatry; *tzava*, a standing army ready to take orders, as against a
  more haphazard militia that has to be assembled; *tzavah*, the act of bloating
  (Bamidbar 5:21-22), since something swollen and distended is less likely to
  move from its place and will rather remain standing there; *tzav*, the turtle
  of Vayikra 11:29, whose large shell makes it appear bloated; and *tzav*, the
  covered wagon of Bamidbar 7:3 and Yeshayahu 66:20, whose shape resembles the
  turtle (see also *Bamidbar Rabbah* §12:17 on why the wagons are called
  *tzav*).
- The Aramaic *tzva* appears in Daniel in the sense of "wishes" or "wants";
  Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549), also known as Elias Levita, writes in
  *Meturgeman* that *tzva* means *emes v'yatziv*, "true and well-grounded"; he
  also explains *tamrukei*, "cosmetics" (Esther 2:3-12), which Aramaic renders
  *tzavhata*, as an expression of beauty and glory.
- *Tzava* itself, colloquially "army," literally means "legion," a gathering of
  people. Rabbi Chaim Friedlander (1923 to 1986) and Rav Shimshon Raphael Hirsch
  (1808 to 1888) connect it to wanting with the same idea: any body of people
  can be said to be joined together if and only if they share one want; unite
  for a common goal and they become one army, otherwise they are a hodgepodge of
  unrelated individuals.

### the Arabic cognate and sababa
The modern Hebrew slang *sababa*, which Rabbi Klein calls untranslatable, is
used to express enthusiasm, satisfaction or agreement, along the lines of
"awesome" or "cool." It comes from Arabic *tzababa*, "great" or "excellent" in
spoken Arabic, whose technical meaning is "yearning" or "strong love"; he
holds this Arabic word to be related to the Hebrew and Aramaic words
under discussion, so that *sababa* should really be pronounced *tzababa*, the
way Baba Tzali became Baba Sali. Rabbi Klein concludes that *tzvi* carries a
plethora of meanings: desire or wanting, deer or gazelle, beauty or glory, good,
and delightful or awesome.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "glory" will absorb *tzvi*, *tiferes* and *pe'er* alike. In
  *tzvi tiferes* the two are already stacked: "splendor of its glory."
- *Eretz hatzvi*: "the Desired Land" on the plain reading, "the Land of the
  Deer" where a derashah runs on the deer's hide or its swiftness; pick by the
  passage and gloss the other in a note.
- *Ateres tzvi* (Yeshayahu 28:5) is "crown of splendor," not "crown of beauty."
- *Tzivyon*: "consent" if the source follows Rashi and Rabbeinu Gershom, "ideal
  form" if it follows the Ritva; the meforshim decide, not the Gemara's phrase.
