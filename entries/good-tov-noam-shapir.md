# Good: tov / noam / shapir (shefer)

## Bottom line

*Tov* is **good**, the plain and general word. *Noam* (adjective *naim*) is
**pleasant**: goodness felt as beauty that draws a person in; Rabbi Bedersi
(*Chosam Tochnis*) defines it as *yofi*. *Shapir* / *shefer* is **fine** or
**fitting**: goodness in the sense of well done, proper, meeting the standard;
Bedersi defines it as *tov*, and in the Gemara *shapir* is primarily the adverb
"well". Two readings of Bedersi's split, neither of them his own words: Rabbi
Klein, who freely admits he is probably reading too much into Bedersi, takes
*noam* as subjective, personally felt goodness and *shapir* as objective
goodness; Rabbi Shaul Goldman holds that even if both are subjective they name
different qualities, *noam* attraction and desirability (whatever its basis) and
*shapir* plain positivity with no pull in it. *Tov* and *naim* sit together in
one pasuk (Bereishis 49:14).

## Distinctions

### tov (טוב)
Root ט-ו-ב. The hypernym. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*): Targum often
renders *tov* as *shapir* in two kinds of place, approval, something "good in
his eyes" (Vayikra 10:20; Bamidbar 24:1; some Targum texts read *takin*), and
physical beauty (Bereishis 6:2; Shmuel I 8:16, 9:2; Melachim I 20:3), where it
also tends to render *yafeh* (Bereishis 12:11, 29:17, 41:2) as *shapir*.

### noam (נעם), naim (נעים)
Root נ-ע-ם.
- Rabbi Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*): from the biliteral ע-ם, which also gives
  *im*, "with": a person tends to bond with what he finds pleasant, so *noam* is
  the pull toward a thing one wishes to join; in *Cheshek Shlomo* he reduces it
  further to ע, movement.
- The Vilna Gaon: *naim*/*noam* is visual appeal, as against *matok*, sweet,
  which is gustatory appeal. Rabbi Pappenheim argues against him that these words
  name no physical property at all but the transcendental pleasure one derives
  from enjoying something sweet, so that *matok* and *areiv* are the physical and
  *naim* the sublime.
- Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 5:30): the pleasant thing arouses and moves a person
  toward a goal.
- Ibn Janach and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) define both *noam* and *shapir* as
  *yofi*.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) defines *shapir* as *noam* and
  *noam* as *hadar*, beauty.
- Bedersi, in *Chosam Tochnis*, the first known book that tries to distinguish
  Hebrew synonyms: *noam* is *yofi*, beauty. Rabbi Klein, saying it seems to him
  that Bedersi means this and admitting he is probably reading too much in:
  subjective goodness, felt personally, as beauty is in the eye of the beholder,
  the way the *yefas toar* need be beautiful only in her captor's eyes
  (Kiddushin 22a; Sifrei Ki Seitzei 211; Ibn Ezra to Devarim 21:11).
- Rabbi Goldman, in personal correspondence with Rabbi Klein: *noam* is
  attraction and desirability, whether grounded in objective features such as
  symmetry, color and form or in wholly subjective ones.

Personal names carry the root: Naomi, wife of Elimelech; Naamah, daughter of
Lemech (Bereishis 4:22) and also a wife of Shlomo (Melachim I 14:21, 14:31;
Divrei HaYamim II 12:13); Naaman the Aramean general (Melachim II 5:2-27); the
Arabic Naim and the Modern Hebrew Noam.

### shapir (שפיר), shefer (שפר)
Root ש-פ-ר: beauty, improvement, goodness. Iyov 26:13, "by His breath the
heavens were made *shifrah*", suggesting an act of perfecting, making them
splendid and orderly. Biblical Aramaic *shefar* (Daniel 3:32, 4:24, 6:2) is a
king's approval, "it seemed good". Talmudic *shapir* is primarily "well",
"properly" (*ati shapir*, *shapir ka'amar*), affirming that an argument is
correct or that a thing meets the standard.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: a *shiphal* form of פ-א-ר, the shin marking an action
  that produces *pe'er*, glory. (A popular folk etymology ties the old Jewish
  surname Shapiro or Shapira to *shapir*, though historians presume it more
  plausibly comes from the German town Speyer.)
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 5:1, 49:11), on the interchange of shin and sin and
  of beis and peh, connects *shefer*/*shapir* with *sefer*, a book, a composite
  of sub-parts brought together coherently, *mispar* and *sofer*, number and
  counting, and *shover*, breaking. He surmises that *shefer* is beauty as the
  overall property of a thing of many parts brought together in an orderly and
  pleasing way, and so differs from *yofi*: *shefer* is inward beauty, the
  harmonious working together of the parts, *yofi* the outward, superficial beauty
  others perceive.
- Bedersi: *shefer* is *tov*. Rabbi Klein, on the same admittedly strained
  reading of Bedersi: objective goodness, a value recognized regardless of the
  beholder.
- Rabbi Goldman: plain positivity, "good" or "fitting", with no necessary appeal
  or elegance; a strong logical argument is not always elegant, an elegant
  insight is not always practical, and good advice is *shapir* even when it does
  not resonate and is hard to accept.

Naftali's *imrei shefer* (Bereishis 49:21) is read by *Midrash Shocher Tov*
(Tehillim 81:4) as spreading Torah, which Rabbi Klein compares to the English
*gospel*, from Old English *god spell*, "good message"; by Sotah 13a through
*sefer*, "book", on the interchange of shin or sin with samech, to which
*Midrash Aggadah* adds that the blessing is for Naftali's Torah students to be
especially clever. The Targumim and Midrashim variously give good tidings,
thanksgiving and berachos to Hashem, agricultural fecundity, song, verbal
cleverness, and even the shofar, which together suggest to Rabbi Klein that
Naftali is the quick and persuasive communicator.

### Why Torah mastery went to Yissachar and not Naftali
Yaakov gave Yissachar and Naftali essentially the same berachah, that they excel
in Torah, and the only difference is the word: *na'eimah* for Yissachar
(Bereishis 49:14, read of Torah by Bereishis Rabbah 98:12, with the Zohar
Vayechi 242b making "rest" the Written Torah and "the land" the Oral Torah) and
*imrei shefer* for Naftali (49:21). Yet in practice it is Yissachar who is more
associated with mastery of Torah: Klal Yisrael consulted the children of
Yissachar for their depth in Torah (Divrei HaYamim I 12:33), Targum Yonasan (to
Bereishis 46:13) has his descendants wise men and masters of calculation, and
the poskim generally came from Levi or from Yissachar (Yoma 26a). Rabbi Klein's
answer, on his own reading of Bedersi: Yissachar experienced Torah as
*subjectively* good, felt as beautiful and pleasant on a personal level, rather
than as an objective value held at arm's length; he frames the lesson in terms
of a "growth mindset," intrinsic motivation over extrinsic validation. Rabbi
Goldman carries the contrast back to the tribes' origins: Yissachar came of
Leah's deliberate transaction with Rachel over the *dudaim* (Bereishis
30:14-18), earned reward through effort, while Naftali came of Rachel's prayers
and struggles and of her giving Bilhah to Yaakov (Bereishis 30:3-9), following
the precedent of Sarah and Hagar.

## Rendering guidance

- *tov*: "good". Keep it plain; it is the word the others are measured against.
- *noam* / *naim*: "pleasant", "pleasantness"; "sweet" only in the gastronomic
  sense (see Rabbi Klein's "Sweet and Pleasant", Jan. 2018). *Darkei noam*:
  "ways of pleasantness".
- *shapir* / *shefer*: "fine", "fitting", "well" (Aramaic adverb), "becoming".
  *Imrei shefer*: "fine sayings" or "goodly words"; not "beautiful words", which
  hands *shefer* the English that belongs to *yofi* and *noam*.
- Together, Bereishis 49:14: "he saw that rest was good and the land was
  pleasant." Tehillim 16:6: "portions have fallen to me in pleasant places; yes,
  a fine inheritance is mine." Two words, never "good" twice.
- Trap: "good" absorbs all three, and "beautiful" absorbs *noam* and *shapir*
  (both lexica define both as *yofi*). If the passage turns on the difference,
  give *noam* the felt, attractive side and *shapir* the proper, well-made side;
  that split is common to both readings of Bedersi.
- Aramaic *shapir* in a Gemara quotation: "well", "rightly", "correctly".
