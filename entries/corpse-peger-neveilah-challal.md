# Corpse: peger / neveilah / challal

## Bottom line

*Peger* is a human **corpse**, and by Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur the rotting body
of a rasha. *Neveilah* is a **carcass**, an animal that died without shechitah,
and by the same authority it can name a human body too, but only one killed by
an outside force. *Challal* literally means **empty** or **cavity**: a body
emptied of its soul is like an empty vessel. *Neveilah* in a halachic passage
is a technical term.

## Distinctions

### peger (פגר)
Some twenty-two appearances in Tanach, among them the sentence on the wilderness
generation, where it falls three times: "in this wilderness your *pegarim* shall
drop... you are *pegarim* who shall fall in the wilderness... until your
*pegarim* are finished in the wilderness" (Bamidbar 14:29-33).

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*, and his glosses to Radak's *Sefer
  HaShorashim*): *peger* is a human body, and specifically the rotting body of a
  wicked man. He allows one exception, Bereishis 15:11, where *peger* names the
  animal carcasses of the Bris bein HaBesarim.
- Rashi there writes that *peger* is related to *pigul*, disgusting, and other
  commentators note that reish and lamed are sometimes interchanged.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim maintains that *peger* is a portmanteau of *po gar*,
  "here he lives," but does not elaborate; Rabbi Klein suggests this possibly
  refers to the dead body being immobile, unable to move elsewhere, so that it
  lives in its place for eternity.

In Aramaic the root פ-ג-ר carries inactivity or at least lagging behind: a
festival is a *yoma d'pagra*, a day of no work, and the Modern Hebrew cognate
*mifager* means "retard."

### neveilah (נבלה)
Close to fifty appearances. Its root sense is *balah*, worn out or decayed,
which is what any carcass left in the open becomes. In halachah *neveilah* is
the fixed term for a kosher animal that died without proper shechitah, and its
meat is forbidden. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur holds that *neveilah* can name human
remains as well, but only where the person was killed by an outside force, not
where he died naturally. He raises the difficulty of Yeshayahu 26:19, "may Your
dead live, my *neveilah* shall arise": why would the navi call himself that?

- One answer is that Yeshayahu was killed by his grandson Menashe (Yevamos 49b),
  so his own body fits the definition.
- Another is that "my *neveilah*" is not his own body at all but the bodies of
  future martyrs whose devotion resembled his, and they too were killed by
  outside forces.

### challal (חלל)
Close to a hundred appearances. The root means empty or hollow: a human body
emptied of its soul is like an emptied vessel. In Modern Hebrew *challal* also
means outer space. The same root gives *chalil*, a flute, obviously hollow
inside, and *chalulim*, the body's inner parts, cavities holding the important
organs. On the same principle, things one would expect to be holy and which are
not are reckoned hollow, something being missing: *chullin*, the Gemara's term
for non-sacred meat, and *Chol HaMoed*, the days of the festival that are not as
holy as Yom Tov in the activities they forbid. And the child born to a kohen
from a marriage forbidden to him is a *chalal* or *chalalah*, desecrated or
emptied of the kehunah he or she would otherwise have received through the
father. The word *chalus* (which yeshiva students commonly pronounce *chaloys*),
the effectuation of a halachic status, may be linked here too, on the pattern of
a Hebrew word carrying its own opposite: a *chalus* is really what *fills* the
void.

### Why the siddur says pegarim
*Elokai Neshamah* thanks Hashem for returning souls *lifgarim meisim*, to dead
bodies. Rabbi Yosef Teomim (*Pri Megadim*), building on Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur,
explains the choice: since *peger* connotes the body of a wicked man, the
berachah is saying that Hashem returns the soul even to the most wicked, who
have done nothing to deserve it. That is the measure of His kindness.

## Rendering guidance

- *Peger*: **corpse**, and in a passage of judgment or contempt **carcass** is
  right for a human body too. Bamidbar 14 is deliberately harsh; do not soften
  it to "bodies."
- *Neveilah* in halachah: leave it as ***neveilah***, or render **an
  unslaughtered carcass**. Never "roadkill" or a merely descriptive "dead
  animal"; the word is a status.
- *Challal*: **the slain** where a person is meant, **hollow** or **cavity**
  where the root's plain sense is in play. *Chalal* of a kohen's child:
  **disqualified**, with the emptying sense glossed once.
- Trap: English "corpse" absorbs all three, and "carcass" absorbs *peger* and
  *neveilah*. Where a source contrasts them, or where *neveilah* is a halachic
  category and *peger* a description in the same sentence, hold two English
  words apart.
- Second trap: *challal* is a family, not a word. In one paragraph it may run
  from a dead body to *chullin* to *Chol HaMoed*; keep the thread of
  "emptiness" visible in English rather than translating each occurrence
  independently.
