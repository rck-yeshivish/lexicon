# Time: eit / zman / moed / shaah

## Bottom line

*Zman* is **time** in general, the span or flow (Wertheimer: the most general
of the three; Rav Hirsch: time in general; Maharal: the flow of time as an
existing element of creation). *Eit* is **a time**, a point or portion within
the span (anonymous "others": a point; Rav Hirsch: a portion); for Malbim it is
a natural unit of time that nature itself shows, "rain in its *eit*." *Moed* is
**appointed time**, the most specific preset point (Wertheimer), set by man or
by Hashem, hence the *moadim* (Malbim). The scheme is not agreed: Malbim puts
*zman* with *moed* as time set by man, and Rabbi Yaakov Emden makes *moed* the
beginning of an appointed time, *zman* its whole duration and *eit* any point
within it, while conceding that in practical usage all these words may be used
interchangeably. *Shaah* beside *eit* is **hour**, the hours of the day against
the seasons of the year (Rama). The thing not to get wrong: בכל עת ובכל שעה is
not a redundancy to be collapsed into "at all times"; the Rama rejects those who
say only *bechol eit* and reads two measures, "at every season and every hour,"
though Rabbi Yaakov Emden takes issue with his limiting *eit* to the seasons.

## Distinctions

### zman (זמן)
Root ז-מ-ן. Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: related to *mizuman*, "ready, prepared" (and
used colloquially of someone "invited"); Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the root of
*zman* is *zamem*, to "plot" or "think ahead." The column draws the upshot: in
essence time is a means of preparation, for the future. (In Modern Hebrew, the
column adds, cash is *mizuman* because, unlike checks or credit cards, it makes
for a fast and easy transaction and can readily be used.) Maharal (*Netzach
Yisrael* 26): *zman* is the flow of time as an existing element of creation,
*eit* time as an abstract concept. Malbim: *zman*, like *moed*, is a
predetermined time set by man. The column observes that this does not seem to
account for the difference between *moed* and *zman*, and suggests ("one could
argue") that *moed* is Hebrew and *zman* Aramaic: the Targumim commonly render
Biblical *moed* as *zman*, although HaBachur points out that *zman* itself
appears in some of the post-Exilic books of the Bible and in Koheles.
Wertheimer: *zman* is the most general of the three. Rav Hirsch: *zman* is
time in general. Rabbi Yaakov Emden: *zman* is the entire duration of an
appointed time.

### eit (עת)
Root ע-ת. HaBachur disagrees with those who call *zman* a synonym of *eit*,
and cites anonymous sages who mention a minute, philosophy-based distinction,
which he does not spell out, writing that he lacks the energy to go into
details; the column says Maharal's abstract-versus-existent line (above) is
that sort of distinction. Malbim: *eit* is a natural unit of time whose
existence nature itself shows (*geshamim b'ittam*). Pappenheim: *eit* shares
some meaning with *zman*, because it too refers to preparation and designation,
as the *ish itti* of Vayikra 16:21 is the man designated to send the goat to
Azazel; the article adds that *atid*, "future," is derived from *eit* and is
also a form of a verb meaning "to prepare." Others: *zman* is a span, *eit* a
point within it. Wertheimer: *eit* is more specific than *zman*, less than
*moed*. Rav Hirsch: *eit* is a portion of time, related to *eeteit* or
*ettim*, a spade that breaks up soil into smaller units. Rabbi Yaakov Emden:
*eit* is any given point within an appointed span; and he takes issue with the
Rama's assertion that *eit* means the seasons of the year, citing examples in
which *eit* refers to other (natural) phenomena as well.

### moed (מועד)
Root י-ע-ד, "appoint." Malbim: a predetermined time, set by man, or by Hashem
for the festivals. Wertheimer: the most specific of the three, a preset point
like a meeting scheduled down to the hour and minute. Rabbi Yaakov Emden:
*moed* is the beginning of an appointed time (its *zman* is the whole duration,
its *eit* any point within). Emden concedes that these are only the technical
differences; in practical usage all these words may be used interchangeably.

### shaah (שעה)
Root ש-ע-ה. The article treats *shaah* only through the Rama (Rabbi Moshe
Isserles). He heard that some consider *bechol eit u'vechol shaah*, in the
second berachah of Birkas HaMazon and the last of Shemoneh Esrei, redundant,
and say only *bechol eit*. He rejects that: *eit* is the annual seasons and
*shaah* the hours of the day, two different measures and not a doubling. Rabbi
Yaakov Emden takes issue with the Rama's claim that *eit* refers to the
seasons, citing other (natural) phenomena that *eit* names; the article does
not say how Emden then reads the pair. So *shaah* here is the unit of the day:
**hour**. The article gives no ruling on *shaah* standing alone in the sense "a
while, a moment, temporarily" (*lefi shaah*, *shaah achas*); the guide has no
source for that yet, and a rendering of it is a judgment call to be labeled as
such.

## Rendering guidance

- בכל עת ובכל שעה: "at every season and at every hour" (Rama), or "at every
  time and every hour," which does not commit to the Rama's reading of *eit*
  that Emden contests. Keep two nouns; do not write "always."
- עת alone: "a time," "the time [for]," "season" (עת לכל חפץ, "a time for
  every purpose"); בעתו "in its season," "at its proper time."
- זמן alone: "time" (the span, the flow); זמן קבוע "a set time"; בזמן הזה "in
  this time / at this season" (the berachah: "at this season" is customary).
- מועד: "appointed time," "set time"; the festivals are "the *moadim*" or
  "the appointed festivals," never bare "holidays" in a sefer.
- שעה beside עת: "hour." Standalone שעה in the "moment" sense: unsettled here;
  see above.
- Trap: English "time" absorbs עת, זמן, and מועד, and English "moment" would
  absorb עת and שעה. Where two stand together (עת וזמן, זמן ומועד), use the
  Wertheimer ladder: general "time," then "a time," then "appointed time."
  Emden's concession (the three interchange in practical usage) means the
  ladder is a tool for doubled phrases, not a claim that every occurrence is
  technical.
- When the passage turns on preparation (Rabbi Moshe Shapiro, Pappenheim),
  a gloss such as "time (*zman*, the time made ready)" may be earned; do not
  build the etymology into the running English.
