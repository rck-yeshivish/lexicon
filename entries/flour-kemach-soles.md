# Flour: kemach / soles

## Bottom line

*Kemach* is ordinary **flour**, ground with the bran and germ still in it:
**whole wheat flour**, and by extension anything of the coarser grade.
*Soles* is the sifted, refined grade: **fine flour**. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis
18:6) makes the point sharply: the difference is not how fine or coarse the
grind is per se, but the difference between whole wheat flour and white flour,
*soles* being made of the endosperm alone. So *kemach* has everything in it and
*soles* has been sifted clean. Rashi (Bava Metzia 87a)
states that *soles* is better than *kemach*.

## Distinctions

### kemach (קמח)
Fourteen appearances in Tanach.

- Donash Ibn Labrat, arguing against Menachem Ibn Saruk, pointed to the
  ostensible Arabic cognate, which means wheat; an anonymous student of Menachem
  answered that the Arabic word means wheat generally while the Hebrew means
  specifically wheat that has been ground, that is, flour.
- Donash's own student Ibn Sheshes conceded that in the Sotah offering, where
  the Torah writes *kemach se'orim*, flour of barley, the word cannot mean wheat.
- Rashi (Sotah 14a): everything is mixed into *kemach*. It can be finely ground
  and still be *kemach*, since husk and bran remain within the granules (Rashi
  to Kesubos 112b).
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: *kemach* is adulterated flour carrying subpar
  material.

As a metaphor it is the lesser stock: "he does not
care about his *kemach*" (Yevamos 42b, Pesachim 84a, Sukkah 54a) of one who
speaks imprecisely, since he grinds *kemach* and not *soles* (Rashi there);
and of Kimchis, the pious woman who merited that each of her seven sons serve as
Kohen Gadol because she was careful always to cover her hair, even indoors, of
whom Chazal said "all *kemach* yields *kemach*, but the *kemach* of Kimchis is
*soles*" (Yerushalmi Yoma 1:1 and Bamidbar Rabbah 2:26; the story is at Yoma
47a). Just as wheat yields flour, so people yield offspring.

Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehudah*) reads *kemach* as a
compound of ק-מ-ה, standing erect, and מ-ח-ה, erasing, since grinding takes the
stalk that stood tall in the field and cuts it down; *kamah* is standing,
uncut grain (Shemos 22:5; Peah 2:7, 4:7, 5:2; Menachos 10:9). Alternatively he
derives it straight from י-מ-ח, erasure, on the interchange of kuf and yud.
The Kimchi family name (Rabbi Yosef Kimchi and his sons Radak and Rabbi Moshe
Kimchi) appears to come from this word. Some have proposed the pronunciation
Kamchi, but Rabbi Klein holds Kimchi to be certainly the more popular one, and
Rabbi Immanuel Frances rhymes it with *simchi*, ostensibly attesting to it.

### soles (סולת)
Fifty-two appearances in Tanach, more than half of them in Bamidbar, mostly
clustered in Nasso and Pinchas. Every menachah in the Torah is brought of
*soles* except the Omer (Vayikra 23:10-11) and the Sotah offering (Bamidbar
5:15), both of which are barley; the Sotah offering the Torah calls *kemach*.

- On that basis Rashi (Vayikra 2:1) and Rashbam (Bereishis 18:6) hold that
  *soles* always means wheat flour.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim objects from Shemos 29:2, which says *soles chittim*,
  flour of wheat, implying that *soles* alone does not necessarily specify the
  species.
- Rabbi Klein adds that either way, since the Sotah offering is explicitly said
  to come from *kemach*, this implies a different type or grade of flour rather
  than a different species of grain.

Menachos
6:7 describes sifting the flour for menachos over and over to yield the finest
*soles* (see Rashi to Menachos 76b); Rashi to Taanis 9b explains that the finer
white flour passes the sifter first and the coarser after (see also Tosafos
there), and Avos 5:15 uses the image: the sieve lets out the *kemach* and keeps
the *soles*. Rashi (Bava Metzia 87a) states plainly that *soles* is better than
*kemach*; Kiddushin 69b calls the Babylonian community pure *soles* against the
mixed dough left in Eretz Yisrael.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *soles* to the biliteral ס-ל, repeated action:
*mesilah* is a road much trodden, *misalsel* (Rosh Hashanah 26b) is one who
twists his hair over and over, and *sal* is the bread basket (Bereishis 40:16,
Vayikra 8:2), in daily use. So, he proposes, *soles* is either the flour precious enough to
be kept in a *sal* rather than dumped in a sack like *kemach*, or the flour
named for the repeated pressing and grinding that produces it.

### geres (גרש) and semida (סמידא)
- Rashi (Menachos 27a): *geres* said of barley (Vayikra 2:14) is to barley what
  *soles* is to wheat.
- Ibn Janach defines *geres* with the word *soles* outright.
- Menachem, Ibn Janach and Radak all treat ג-ר-ש and ג-ר-ס as one root meaning
  breaking or grinding.
- The Aramaic *semida* Rashi glosses as *soles* (Shabbos 110b, Pesachim 74b,
  Taanis 24b, Gittin 56a), and Ibn Ezra (Vayikra 2:1) says *soles* is white
  flour, known in Arabic and Aramaic as *semida*.
- There is a counter-usage: the Targum to Bereishis 18:6 often attributed to
  Rabbi Yonasan ben Uziel renders *kemach* as *semida* and *soles* as *solta*.

This Aramaic
and Arabic word is the etymological ancestor of the English
*semolina*, by way of the Greek *semidalis* and the interchange of dalet and
lammed.

## Rendering guidance

- Default pair: *kemach* = **flour** (or "whole wheat flour" where the contrast
  is live), *soles* = **fine flour**. "Fine flour" is the safe rendering in a
  korbanos context and matches the sifting the Mishnah describes.
- Do not render *soles* as "semolina." The English word does descend from the
  Aramaic *semida*, as Rabbi Klein notes; but
  semolina in current English is a coarse durum meal, the opposite of what
  *soles* means.
- Trap: English "flour" alone will swallow both words. In any passage that
  grades them (Avos 5:15, Bava Metzia 87a, the Kimchis maamar) keep the two
  renderings visibly different, and in a metaphor render the sense, "coarse
  stock yields coarse stock, but Kimchis's yields the finest."
- The two together: Bereishis 18:6, *kemach soles*. Bava Metzia 87a reads the
  doubling as Avraham offering the better grade and Sarah the lesser, so a
  translation following the Gemara must keep both words distinct. Radak reads
  the phrase as one thing, *kemach* to be made into *soles*; on his reading
  render "flour for fine meal." Say which you follow.
- Where the Torah writes *kemach se'orim* (Bamidbar 5:15), render "barley
  flour" and let a note carry the point that the coarse word is deliberate.
