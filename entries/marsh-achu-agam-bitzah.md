# Marsh: achu / agam / bitzah (suf)

## Bottom line

*Agam* is a **marsh**, and Rashi also glosses it as a
standing **pond**. *Bitzah* is a **mire**, ground held in mud, from *botz*, mud.
*Achu* divides the meforshim: Rashi makes it a **marsh**, plain and simple;
Ramban and Rav Hirsch make it the **riverbank growth**, the reeds and grasses,
not the place. *Suf* means the **reeds** that grow in a marsh, which is why
*Yam Suf* is the Sea of Reeds. Iyov 8:11 sets *bitzah* and *achu* in a single
pasuk, with *gome* beside them.

## Distinctions

### achu (אחו)
Pharaoh's cows graze *ba'achu* (Bereishis 41:2, 41:18).

- Rashi (there): *achu* means *agam*, and he supports it from Iyov 8:11, "can
  the *gome* shoot up without a *bitzah*, or the *achu* grow without water?"; he
  renders it in Old French *maresc*, marsh.
- Ramban (to Bereishis 41:2) objects: *achu* is not the marsh but a kind of
  grass or growth that comes up on a riverbank, and he suggests that the word
  relates to *ach*, brother, for the fellowship of the various plants that grow
  together along the water. Rav Hirsch says the same.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives two turns on the same root: the brotherhood of
  the animals that gather in that fertile place to feed, or the fact that a
  stretch of ground lying beside the river looks like the river's brother.
- Rabbi Moshe ben Shem Tov Gabbai defends Rashi: *achu* names the marsh in which
  Ramban's vegetation often grows, and since that growth tends to appear in
  swampy ground, the word for the plant came to serve for the place, so that in
  practice *achu* and *agam* mean the same.
- Ibn Janach and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) had already proposed that *achu*
  covers both, the grass (possibly papyrus) and the ground where it grows.

### agam (אגם)
The word in the makkos: "over their rivers, over their canals, over their
*agamim*, and over every gathering of their water" (Shemos 7:19, and again at
8:1).

- Rashi (to Shemos 7:19) defines it as a standing body of water and renders it
  Old French *estanc*, a pond, which is not quite a marsh; elsewhere (to
  *Taanis* 22a, *Yevamos* 121a, *Sanhedrin* 5b) he renders *agam* and Aramaic
  *agama* as *maresc*, marsh.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *agam* to the two-letter ג-ם, spongy or
  absorbent matter: ground soaked and partly flooded, which must be sponge-like
  to hold that much water and stay wet. From the same root comes *gome*, the
  spongy reed of wetlands.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer even says a marsh is called *agam* for the
  *gome* that grows in it.
- Shadal (to Shemos 2:3): *gome* is papyrus.

### bitzah (ביצה)
Three times in Tanach: Iyov 8:11 and 40:21, Yechezkel 47:11. Muddy, swampy
ground, from *botz*, mud (Yirmiyahu 38:22).

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes both from the two-letter ב-ץ, matter of a
  mucus-like consistency, whose commonest derivative is *beitzah*, an egg,
  typically gooey inside (the singular *beitzah* never appears in Tanach, only
  the plural *beitzim*, as at Devarim 22:6). Mud fits: not pourable like water,
  not solid either. He also puts [*butz*](linen-sheish-bahd-butz-pishtim.html#%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A6) here (Esther 1:6, 8:15, Yechezkel
  27:16), linen from flax grown in wet ground like a *bitzah*'s.
- Rashi renders *bitzah* as *maresc* (to Yechezkel 47:11, Iyov 8:11, *Bava
  Metzia* 74a), and the Talmudic *bitzaim* / *bitzaei mayim*, spelled with an
  ayin, the same way (to *Shevuos* 16a, *Sanhedrin* 96a).

### suf (סוף)
Rashi (to Shemos 2:3 and 13:18) associates *suf* with *agam*. Rabbi Avraham Meir
HaKohen Glanzer of Antwerp (*Maayanei Agam*) reads Rashi precisely: he does not
say *suf* means *agam*, only that it is somehow associated with the *agam*; and Rashi's own laaz
bears this out, since he renders *suf* as Old French *roisel*, the common reed.
*Suf* is therefore the reed growing in the marsh, not the marsh. Hence *Yam
Suf* is the Sea of Reeds.

## Rendering guidance

- Iyov 8:11, the pasuk that carries three of these words: "Can the *gome* shoot
  up without a mire, can the *achu* grow tall without water?" Render *bitzah*
  "mire" and leave *achu* transliterated or as "marsh-grass," since Rashi and
  Ramban disagree on whether it is the place or the plant. Do not render *bitzah*
  and *achu* both "marsh"; Rashi's proof depends on their being two words.
- אגם in the makkos: "marshes" (Shemos 7:19). "Ponds" is defensible on Rashi's
  Old French but sits oddly in a list that already has rivers and canals.
- ביצה: "mire" or "swampy ground," not "swamp" flat, so that *agam* keeps
  "marsh." The English pair marsh/swamp is too close to carry the distinction.
- ים סוף: "Sea of Reeds," never "Red Sea." *Suf* is the reed, and by Rabbi
  Avraham Meir HaKohen Glanzer's reading of Rashi it is not a word for marsh at
  all, so "Swamp Sea" is wrong too.
- Trap: Rashi glosses *achu*, *agam* and *bitzah* all three with the same Old
  French *maresc*. That is Rashi telling a reader what the word points at, not a
  claim that Hebrew has three names for one thing. Do not use his laaz to
  license one English word for all three.
- אחו in Bereishis 41: if the translation follows Ramban, "in the reed grass";
  if Rashi, "in the marsh." Say which; the pesukim about Pharaoh's cows read
  differently under each.
- On "marsh" against "swamp" in English, Rabbi Klein uses the two
  interchangeably, and notes from Google Books' Ngram Viewer that
  *marsh* was the more common word in print from 1800 to 1850, *swamp* from 1850
  to 1970, *marsh* again from 1970 to 2000, and *swamp* once more since.
