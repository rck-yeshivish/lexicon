# Dust: afar / eifer / avak / deshen

## Bottom line

*Afar* is **dirt** or **soil**, the general word, and the one that can grow
things. *Eifer* is **ashes**, what fire leaves, and it grows nothing. *Avak* is
**dust**, fine powder produced by pulverizing rather than by burning. *Deshen* is
a narrower word for ashes, tied by Rabbi Pappenheim to fat and by Rabbi Yehuda
Leib Shapira-Frankfurter to the Mikdash. The difference between *afar* and
*eifer* is fertility, which is what makes it significant that the Torah calls
the ashes of the Parah Adumah *afar* and not *eifer*.

## Distinctions

### afar (עפר)
Dirt, soil. The word of Hashem's promise to Avraham that his descendants would be
as the *afar* of the earth (Bereishis 13:16). Whether *afar* is narrowly "soil"
or broadly "dirt" is a machlokes at *Chullin* 88b over covering the blood of a
slaughtered chayah or bird (Vayikra 17:13): Beis Shammai holds only literal soil
may be used, Beis Hillel that ashes serve as well, and the halachah follows Beis
Hillel, which treats *afar* as the general term. *Shabbos* 18a and Rashi to
*Taanis* 15a likewise imply that *afar* and *eifer* are synonyms, or at least
interchangeable in some contexts.

The Zohar (*Vayishlach* 170a) sets the real line: *afar* is the crucible from
which growth comes, while *eifer* and *avak*, what fire leaves behind, grow
nothing. The
Zohar's own formulation treats *eifer* and *avak* as though they were perfect
synonyms for burnt remnants; Rabbi Klein notes that this is a bit of an
oversimplification, since although both are formed by a violent destructive
process they are not the same thing, *eifer* being produced by burning and *avak*
by pulverizing.
Rabbi Moshe Shapiro draws the lesson of Bamidbar 19:17, where the Torah calls the
ashes of the Parah Adumah *afar*: what looks like the residue of destruction can
sometimes become the seed of renewal, which is exactly the point for one purified from
tumas meis.

On the root, in Rabbi Pappenheim's system *afar* cannot be related to the
biliteral פ-ר, because for him a two-letter root joins only HEY, ALEPH, MEM, NUN, TAV,
YOD or VAV to become triliteral, and *afar* opens with an AYIN. Others who work
with two-letter roots do connect them: Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg, since
*afar* is indispensable to growing *pri*; and Rabbi Aharon Marcus (*Barzilai*),
who traces both *afar* and *eifer* to פ-ר, and elsewhere (*Keses HaSofer*) reads
*afar* as a fusion of ע-פ, "flying," with פ-ר.

### eifer (אפר)
Ashes, the product of burning. Rabbi Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral פ-ר,
"breaking a thing down into smaller components," so *eifer* is matter reduced to
particles by fire. From the same root he derives *peirurim* (crumbs), *parur* (a
pot for crumbs and small grains), *pur* (the box for drawing lots, shaped like a
*parur*), *pri* (fruit, a microcosm of the tree it comes from), *par* and *parah*
(cattle kept for breeding), *pe'er* and *tiferes* (a beauty that resolves into
many aspects), *hafarah* (dissolving a vow), *primah* (tearing a garment into
shreds), and *tefirah* (sewing, which repairs what *primah* did).

The Netziv reads Avraham's *afar va'eifer* (Bereishis 18:27) as a double disclaimer before tefillah: some tefillos are answered in the
merit of righteous forebears (Rashi to Bereishis 25:21) and some in the merit of
righteous descendants (*Bereishis Rabbah* 29:5). Having neither, Avraham prefaced
his tefillah by appealing directly to Hashem's mercy. He called himself *afar*
because dirt has no useful past, as his own father Terach was an idolater, and
*eifer* because ashes have no fruitful future, since, as far as Avraham could
tell, he was not destined to father righteous descendants, Yitzchak not yet
having been born.

### avak (אבק)
Fine dust, produced by pulverizing.
- Rabbi Pappenheim: *afar* is run-of-the-mill dirt, *avak* is finer powder.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: *avak* is matter rubbed down to its core particles, light
  enough to be carried on the wind, and it is always the product of a struggle;
  the *avak* of Yaakov's wrestling with the angel rose to the Kisei HaKavod
  (*Chullin* 91a). Chazal's *avak* compounds work in this spirit, brushing the
  edge of a prohibition without technically crossing it: *avak lashon hara*
  (*Bava Basra* 165a), *avak ribbis* (*Temurah* 6b), *avak sheviis* (*Succah*
  40b), *avak avodah zarah* (*Tosefta Avodah Zarah* 1:3).
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel proposes that since ALEPH and CHES are sometimes
  interchangeable, *avak* may be related to *chavak*/*chibbuk*, a hug, since
  such particles are so small and insignificant on their own that one meets them
  only clinging to, or "hugging," something else; and Rashi (to Bereishis 32:25)
  describes that same wrestling as *chovek*, a hugging.

### deshen (דשן)
A second word for ashes.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer cites Rabbi Eliezer Lipman Lichtenstein (*Shem
  Olam*): *deshen* is what is burnt while fire still glows inside it, like an
  ember, while *eifer* is burnt through with no fire left.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter: *eifer* is ordinary ash and *deshen* is
  reserved for the ashes of the avodah in the Mikdash.
- Rabbi Pappenheim: "ashes" is only the secondary sense of *deshen*, whose
  primary sense is fat (Devarim 31:20; Tehillim 63:6; Mishlei 15:30); ashes of
  burnt animals are called *deshen* for the residual fat in them, so on his view
  *deshen* is only animal ash, while burnt flora yields *eifer* alone; fruit and
  vegetable oils, on his account, are *deshe* (Bereishis 1:11). He connects
  *deshe* and both senses of *deshen* to the biliteral ד-ש.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *afar* "dirt" or "soil," *eifer* "ashes," *avak* "dust,"
  *deshen* "ashes" with the qualifier the context supplies (the Mikdash's ashes,
  or "fatty ash").
- The pair *afar va'eifer* (Bereishis 18:27): "dirt and ashes." Do not soften it
  to "dust and ashes," which is the King James phrase and quietly swaps *afar*
  for *avak*, losing the Netziv's past-and-future reading entirely.
- Trap: English "dust" absorbs *afar*, *eifer* and *avak* at once. Reserve "dust"
  for *avak*, and where the passage turns on growth against sterility, make the
  contrast explicit rather than trusting one English word to carry it.
- Bamidbar 19:17: render *afar* there as "dirt" or "earth," not "ashes," even
  though the referent is ash. The unexpected word is the whole point.
- *Avak lashon hara* and its siblings: keep *avak* transliterated, or render "the
  dust of," never "a trace of," which loses the image of brushing the boundary.
- Rabbi Pappenheim concludes that despite the distinctions, these words are close
  enough that sources often use them interchangeably; do not force a distinction
  onto a passage that is not making one.
