# Place: makom / atar / duchta

## Bottom line

*Makom* is the only native Hebrew word for "place." *Atar* is the
standard Aramaic word for the same thing, the Targum's regular rendering of
*makom*; *duchta* is the Bavli's Aramaic word for "place," which the column
suggests, if the derivation holds, may itself be an Old Persian loanword rather
than native Aramaic. These are not shades of meaning but
shades of *language*. Where a passage sets *makom* against *atar*, the contrast
is Hebrew against Aramaic, not one kind of place against another.

## Distinctions

### makom (מקום)
Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach and Radak (each in *Sefer HaShorashim*) derive *makom*
from the triliteral root ק-ו-ם, taking the opening MEM as non-radical; Menachem
Ibn Saruk has no entry for the word at all, so his root for it is unknown.
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, working from the biliteral ק-ם, supplies the sense that
ties the family together: the core idea is "height," the up-and-down dimension of
a thing. From it come *komah* (physical stature), *kamah* (standing grain),
*kumah* (standing or rising, and figuratively holding one's stature under
pressure), *kiyum* (established, enduring), and *makom*, the spot in which a
thing has been planted and thereby firmly established, then borrowed outward to
mean any location at all. Rabbi Pappenheim adds *nekamah* (revenge) to the
family: the debt owed a wrongdoer is kept "established" until it can be repaid.
*Makom* appears roughly four hundred times in Tanach. For why Hashem is
sometimes called *HaMakom*, literally "the place," in rabbinic literature and
liturgy, the column refers the reader to *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 33:21.

### atar (אתר)
An Aramaic word. Outside the phrase *derech ha'atarim* (Bamidbar 21:1) it never
appears in the Hebrew of Tanach, nor in the Mishnah, but it appears five times in
the Aramaic sections (Daniel 2:35; Ezra 5:15, 6:3, 6:5, 6:7). Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur (*Meturgaman*): *atar* is the Targumim's standard rendering of *makom*
(so Targum to Bereishis 1:9, 13:4, 20:11; Devarim 12:2; Shoftim 19:13; Shmuel I
7:16; Shmuel II 17:12; Melachim I 13:16; Yirmiyahu 22:3), which settles its
Aramaic bona fides. The cognate *atra* stands in *Kaddish D'Rabbanan*, also of a
place or locale. In Modern Hebrew *atar* has been reappropriated to parallel the
English "site": a tourist site or attraction, a construction site, geo-location,
and a website.

On *derech ha'atarim* itself the commentators split. Most (*Eichah Rabbah* 1:21,
Targum Onkelos, Menachem Ibn Saruk, Rashi, Rashbam, Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor,
Ibn Ezra, Radak) treat the ALEPH as extraneous to the core root and read the word
from *la'tur*, "to spy," so the phrase names the route the Ten Spies had taken
close to four decades earlier, entering the land from the south. A second group
(Rabbi Toviah ben Eliezer in *Pesikta Zutrata / Midrash Lekach Tov*, Rabbeinu
Meyuchas, and Rabbi Menachem Ricanati, all to Bamidbar 21:1) glosses *atar* with
the word *makom* outright, giving "the way of the sites"; on that reading, as the
column puts it with a pun, the king of Arad heard the Jews were taking the scenic
route to go site-seeing. Rashi himself glosses *atar* with *makom* elsewhere (to
Daniel 2:38; Ezra 6:3, 6:8, 8:15; *Kesuvos* 68a; *Bava Metzia* 67a; *Bava Basra*
29a, 103a; *Avodah Zarah* 7b, 31b). Those same exegetes, the column stresses, make
a point of noting that *atar* is an Aramaic word while *makom* is Hebrew, so that
the two are not quite synonyms even where they mean the same thing.

Rabbi Yaakov Yehudah (Zilberberg) de Kassif (1914-2003) seems, rather, to explain
*atar* as a Hebrew word, connecting by metathesis three roots built of the
consonants aleph, tav and reish around the ideas of boundary and clear
delineation: ת-א-ר (*toar*), the aesthetic form a body's contours and curves
outline; ת-ר-א (*hasraah*), the warning that establishes the border of what is
acceptable and tells a would-be sinner what awaits him if he crosses it; and
א-ת-ר (*atar*), a place as a geographical unit defined by the borders that mark it
off from the rest of the world.

### duchta (דוכתא)
The Bavli's usual Aramaic word for "place," with the shortened *duch* at
*Berachos* 42b. Rashi defines both with the word *makom* (to *Moed Katan* 16b,
*Kesuvos* 77a, *Sanhedrin* 96b). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur's *Meturgaman* has no
entry for *duchta* or *duch*, though, so far as the column knows, *duchta* does
render *makom* at least once in Targum (Mishlei 27:8), and *ducha* in Targum to
Melachim I 14:28 and Divrei HaYamim II 12:11.

That raises the column's own question: why should Aramaic have two words for the
same thing, *atar* and *duchta*, when Hebrew has only *makom*? Two answers are
offered. Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein (1899-1983) and Rabbi Dr. Yitzchak (Jared)
Greenblatt see the Aramaic *atar* as related to verb cognates in Arabic and
Ethiopic meaning "tracing" or "tracking" (Modern Hebrew *l'ater* is used the same
way), on the strength of which, the column suggests, the "place" sense of
*duchta*/*duch* may be an expansion of a word that at core is about finding
something's location rather than about the location itself. Alternatively, Rabbi
Joseph Perles (1835-1894), in his German *Etymologische Studien zur Kunde der
rabbinischen Sprache und Alterthümer* (pp. 57, 81-82), argues that *duchta*/*duch*
derives from the Old Persian *daqyua* and *dahi*. Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894)
cites this approvingly in his *Aruch HaShaleim*, which the column says does not
mean much to it, since Kohut has a general propensity to source rabbinic words in
Old Persian; but Rabbi Shaul Goldman clarified that the Avestan or Old Persian
word Perles and Kohut meant is *dahyu*, "country" or "province." On that showing
*duchta*/*duch* would not be native Aramaic at all but borrowed Old Persian, and
Aramaic too would have only one native word for place, namely *atar*.

If that connection can be established, the column continues, it is not too
farfetched to see the Old Persian *dahyu* as a derivative of the
Proto-Indo-European root *deuk-*, "lead," a territory united under one
leadership. Linguists take that same PIE root as the etymon of the Latin
*dux*/*ducis*, at first a military commander and later the ruler of a region,
whose counterpart *duchus* appears in rabbinic idiom (*Berachos* 32b, *Sanhedrin*
39a, *Sotah* 14a, *Avodah Zarah* 11a), and behind the Latin stand English duke,
duce, duchess, duchy, and ducat, duct, conduce, deduce, induce, produce, seduce
and educate. Asked about a link between the Aramaic *duchta* and the Latin *dux*,
ChatGPT told the columnist the proposal has some merit on shared conceptual
grounds (he adds a possible allusion in *Bereishis Rabbah* 5:3 expounding Tehillim
93:3), but he closes by saying it is difficult to confirm any actual etymological
link between the two conclusively.

### asher (אשר)
The common Biblical conjunction, appearing thousands of times and meaning
"that/which/of/because/so," but in at least two places Targum renders it *atar*
(Targum to Shmuel I 23:13 and Yechezkel 23:40; see Rashi there and to *Kiddushin*
76b). On a simple level this may be interpretation rather than translation,
reading *asher* in context as "that [place where]"; but there is ample room, the
column holds, to see Targum as saying the Hebrew *asher* is genuinely cognate with
the Aramaic *atar*, since Hebrew shin often turns into tav in Aramaic, and there is
an Akkadian word *ashru* that means "place." Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) takes
it a step further: the Akkadian and Sumerian *ashartu*, "temple," literally means
"[holy] place" and is cognate with the Hebrew *asher* and the Aramaic *atar*.

The column applies that to a find at Kuntillet Ajrud in the northeastern Sinai,
where archaeologists uncovered a Hebrew inscription reading "I blessed you to
Hashem of Samaria and His Asherah (*u'l'asherto*)," which on its face makes the
writer no pure monotheist but a believer in the Canaanite goddess Asherah
alongside Hashem. If *asher*/*atar* can mean a temple, shrine or holy site, the
inscription may instead be read "and His Temple," which the column says makes
better sense; it presents this as a partial solution to the puzzle, not a settled
one.

## Rendering guidance

- *Makom* standing alone: "place." Reach for "site," "location," or "spot" only
  where the English sentence needs the variety; none of them is a different word
  in the Hebrew.
- Where a Rishon glosses *atar* or *duchta* with *makom*, he is translating.
  Render both "place" and do not manufacture a distinction the gloss denies.
- Trap: English "place" absorbs all three, so a passage whose point *is* the
  difference (an Aramaic word explained by a Hebrew one, a Targum's choice) needs
  the Hebrew or Aramaic kept in the rendering, at least once.
- *Derech ha'atarim* (Bamidbar 21:1): follow the majority and render "the way of
  the spies," noting the minority "the way of the sites" if the passage turns on
  it. Do not present the minority as the plain sense.
- *Atra* in *Kaddish D'Rabbanan*: "place," in the sense of a locale or community.
- The Rabbinic *le'alter* ("immediately") is plausibly *al atar*, "on the spot";
  render it as an expression of time, not of place.
