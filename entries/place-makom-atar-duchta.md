# Place: makom / atar / duchta

## Bottom line

*Makom* is the only native Hebrew word for "place." *Atar* is the
standard Aramaic word for the same thing, the Targum's regular rendering of
*makom*; *duchta* is an Aramaic word for "place," quite common in the Bavli,
which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests, if the derivation holds, may itself be
an Old Persian loanword rather than native Aramaic. These are not shades of
meaning but shades of *language*.

## Distinctions

### makom (מקום)

- Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach and Radak (each in *Sefer HaShorashim*) derive *makom*
  from the triliteral root ק-ו-ם, taking the opening MEM as non-radical.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk has no entry for the word at all, so his root for it is
  unknown.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, working from the biliteral ק-ם, supplies the sense
  that ties the family together: the core idea is "height," the up-and-down
  dimension of a thing. From it come *komah* (physical stature), *kamah*
  (standing grain), *kumah* (standing or rising, and figuratively holding one's
  stature under pressure), *kiyum* (established, enduring), and *makom*, the
  spot in which a thing has been planted and thereby firmly established, then
  borrowed outward to mean any location at all. Rabbi Pappenheim adds *nekamah*
  (revenge) to the family: the debt owed a wrongdoer is kept "established" until
  it can be repaid.

*Makom* appears roughly four hundred times in Tanach. *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* (to
Shemos 33:21) explains why Hashem is sometimes called *HaMakom*, literally "the
place," in rabbinic literature and liturgy.

### atar (אתר)
An Aramaic word. Outside the phrase *derech ha'atarim* (Bamidbar 21:1) it never
appears in the Hebrew of Tanach, nor in the Mishnah, but it appears five times in
the Aramaic sections (Daniel 2:35; Ezra 5:15, 6:3, 6:5, 6:7). Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur (*Meturgaman*): *atar* is the Targumim's standard rendering of *makom*
(so Targum to Bereishis 1:9, 13:4, 20:11; Devarim 12:2; Shoftim 19:13; Shmuel I
7:16; Shmuel II 17:12; Melachim I 13:16; Yirmiyahu 22:3), which solidifies its
Aramaic bona fides. The cognate *atra* stands in *Kaddish D'Rabbanan*, also of a
place or locale. In Modern Hebrew *atar* has been reappropriated to parallel the
English "site": a tourist site or attraction, a construction site, geo-location,
and a website.

On *derech ha'atarim* itself the commentators split.

- Most (*Eichah Rabbah* 1:21, Targum Onkelos, Menachem Ibn Saruk, Rashi,
  Rashbam, Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor, Ibn Ezra, Radak) treat the ALEPH as
  extraneous to the core root and read the word from *la'tur*, "to spy," so the
  phrase names the route the Ten Spies had taken close to four decades earlier,
  entering the land from the south.
- A second group (Rabbi Toviah ben Eliezer in *Pesikta Zutrata / Midrash Lekach
  Tov*, Rabbeinu Meyuchas, and Rabbi Menachem Ricanati, all to Bamidbar 21:1)
  glosses *atar* with the word *makom* outright, giving "the way of the sites";
  on that reading, as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein puts it with a pun, the king of
  Arad would seem to have heard the Jews were taking the scenic route to go
  site-seeing.

Rashi himself glosses *atar* with *makom* elsewhere (to Daniel 2:38; Ezra 6:3,
6:8, 8:15; *Kesubos* 68a; *Bava Metzia* 67a; *Bava Basra* 29a, 103a; *Avodah
Zarah* 7b, 31b). Those same exegetes make a point of noting that *atar* is an
Aramaic word while *makom* is Hebrew; so, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein infers, the
two are not quite synonyms even where they might mean the same thing.

Rabbi Yaakov Yehudah (Zilberberg) de Kassif (1914-2003) seems, rather, to explain
*atar* as a Hebrew word, connecting by metathesis three roots built of the
consonants aleph, tav and reish around the ideas of boundary and clear
delineation: ת-א-ר (*toar*), the aesthetic form a body's contours and curves
outline; ת-ר-א (*hasraah*), the warning that establishes the border of what is
acceptable and tells a would-be sinner what awaits him if he crosses it; and
א-ת-ר (*atar*), a place as a geographical unit defined by the borders that mark it
off from the rest of the world.

### duchta (דוכתא)
An Aramaic word for "place," quite common in the Bavli, with the shortened *duch* at
*Berachos* 42b. Rashi defines both with the word *makom* (to *Moed Katan* 16b,
*Kesubos* 77a, *Sanhedrin* 96b). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur's *Meturgaman* has no
entry for *duchta* or *duch*, though, so far as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein knows, *duchta* does
render *makom* at least once in Targum (Mishlei 27:8), and *ducha* in Targum to
Melachim I 14:28 and Divrei HaYamim II 12:11.

Why should Aramaic have two words for the same thing, *atar* and *duchta*, when
Hebrew has only *makom*? Two answers are offered.

- Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein (1899-1983) and Rabbi Dr. Yitzchak (Jared) Greenblatt
  see the Aramaic *atar* as related to verb cognates in Arabic and Ethiopic
  meaning "tracing" or "tracking" (Modern Hebrew *l'ater* is used the same way),
  on the strength of which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests that the "place"
  sense of *atar* may be an expansion of a word that at core is about finding
  something's location rather than about the location itself.
- Alternatively, Rabbi Joseph Perles (1835-1894), in his German *Etymologische
  Studien zur Kunde der rabbinischen Sprache und Alterthümer* (pp. 57, 81-82),
  argues that *duchta*/*duch* derives from the Old Persian *daqyua* and *dahi*.
  Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894) cites this approvingly in his *Aruch
  HaShaleim*, which, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says, does not mean much to him,
  since Kohut has a general propensity to source rabbinic words in Old Persian;
  but Rabbi Shaul Goldman clarified that the Avestan or Old Persian word Perles
  and Kohut meant is *dahyu*, "country" or "province." On that showing
  *duchta*/*duch* would not be native Aramaic at all but borrowed Old Persian,
  and Aramaic too would have only one native word for place, namely *atar*.

If that connection can be established, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds, it is not
too farfetched to see the Old Persian *dahyu* as a derivative of the
Proto-Indo-European root *deuk-*, "lead," since *dahyu* may refer to a territory
united under one leadership. Linguists take that same PIE root as the etymon of the Latin
*dux*/*ducis*, at first a military commander and later the ruler of a region,
whose counterpart *duchus* appears in rabbinic idiom (*Berachos* 32b, *Sanhedrin*
39a, *Sotah* 14a, *Avodah Zarah* 11a), and behind the Latin stand English duke,
duce, duchess, duchy, and ducat, duct, conduce, deduce, induce, produce, seduce
and educate. Asked by Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein about a link between the Aramaic
*duchta* and the Latin *dux*, ChatGPT replied that the proposal has some merit on
shared conceptual grounds; Rabbi Klein points to *Bereishis Rabbah* 5:3, expounding
Tehillim 93:3, as a possible allusion to such a connection. Nonetheless, it is
difficult to confirm any actual etymological link between the two conclusively.

### asher (אשר)
The common Biblical conjunction, appearing thousands of times and meaning
"that/which/of/because/so," but in at least two places Targum renders it *atar*
(Targum to Shmuel I 23:13 and Yechezkel 23:40; see Rashi there and to *Kiddushin*
76b). On a simple level this may be interpretation rather than translation,
reading *asher* in context as "that [place where]"; but there is ample room,
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds, to see Targum as saying the Hebrew *asher* is genuinely cognate with
the Aramaic *atar*, since Hebrew shin often turns into tav in Aramaic, and there is
an Akkadian word *ashru* that means "place." Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) takes
it a step further: the Akkadian and Sumerian *ashartu*, "temple," literally means
"[holy] place" and is cognate with the Hebrew *asher* and the Aramaic *atar*.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein applies that to a find at Kuntillet Ajrud in the northeastern Sinai,
where archaeologists uncovered a Hebrew inscription reading "I blessed you to
Hashem of Samaria and His Asherah (*u'l'asherto*)," which on its face makes the
writer no pure monotheist but a believer in the Canaanite goddess Asherah
alongside Hashem. If *asher*/*atar* can mean a temple, shrine or holy site, the
inscription may instead be read "and His Temple," which in Rabbi Reuven Chaim
Klein's view makes better sense; he presents this as a partial solution to the
puzzle, not a settled one.

## Rendering guidance

- *Makom* standing alone: "place." Reach for "site," "location," or "spot" only
  where the English sentence needs the variety; none of them is a different word
  in the Hebrew.
- Where a Rishon glosses *atar* or *duchta* with *makom*, he is translating.
  Render both "place" and do not manufacture a distinction the gloss denies.
- Trap: English "place" absorbs all three, so a passage whose point *is* the
  difference (an Aramaic word explained by a Hebrew one, a Targum's choice) needs
  the Hebrew or Aramaic kept in the rendering, at least once.
- *Derech ha'atarim* (Bamidbar 21:1): follow the majority and render "the way of
  the spies," noting the minority "the way of the sites" if the passage turns on
  it. Do not present the minority as the plain sense.
- *Atra* in *Kaddish D'Rabbanan*: "place," in the sense of a locale or community.
- The Rabbinic *le'alter* ("immediately") is plausibly *al atar*, "on the spot";
  render it as an expression of time, not of place.
