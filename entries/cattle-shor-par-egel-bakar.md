# Cattle: shor / par / egel / aleph / bakar

## Bottom line

*Shor* is the **ox**, and it bears that name from the day it is born. *Par* is
the **bull**, *parah* the **cow** or **heifer**, and the name attaches only once
the animal is grown. *Egel* and *eglah* are the male and female **calf**. *Aleph*
is the strong working **ox** of Tanach's poetry, eight appearances. *Bakar* is
the collective, **cattle**, and its *boker* is the **herdsman**. *Shor* is not
a synonym for *par* at any age.

## Distinctions

### the English words first
Rabbi Klein holds that English has neat parallels to the Hebrew terms, only
without a traceable etymology behind them. A
*heifer* is a young female bovine, especially one that has not yet calved; a
*cow* is a mature female that has calved and so gives milk, though colloquially
"cow" covers any bovine at all. On the male side English marks the gelded off
from the fertile, the young from the old, and the beast bred for food from the
beast bred for work: a *bull* keeps its testicles and is typically used for breeding; one
castrated young and raised for beef is a *steer* or *bullock*, one castrated
older a *stag*; an older castrated beast used for draft work is an *ox*, though
"ox" too is sometimes used loosely for any bovine trained to draw.

### the five names
Rabbi Eliezer HaKallir's *piyut* for the second day of Pesach uses five Hebrew
words for an ox: *par*, *shor*, *egel*, *aleph*, *bakar*. *Peirush HaRokeach* and
Rabbeinu Efrayim find the five alluded to in the law that a thief who steals an
ox and then slaughters or sells it pays five times its value (Shemos 21:37).

### egel (עגל) and eglah (עגלה)
The calf, still nursing. English "calf" serves both sexes; Hebrew has *egel* for
the male and *eglah* for the female. Rabbi Zalman Hanau (*Tzohar HaTeivah*):
*egel* from *igul*, a circle, since young calves tend to be round and pudgy. The Mishnah
(Parah 1:1) fixes when the name changes: Rabbi Eliezer holds it is an *eglah*
through its first year and a *parah* thereafter; the Chachamim hold it stays an
*eglah* through its second year as well.

### par (פר) and parah (פרה)
The classical pair, male and female.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives them from the biliteral peh-reish, breaking a
  thing into smaller parts, whence *peirurim* (crumbs), *eifer* (ash), *parur*
  (a pot for crumbs and small grains), *pri* (a fruit, a microcosm of the tree
  that comes off it), *pe'er* and *tiferes* (an all-encompassing beauty made of
  many aspects), *hafarah* (dissolving a vow), *primah* (tearing a garment into
  shreds), and *tefirah* (sewing, which repairs *primah*). Since *pri* is
  reproduction, the *par* is the fertile male, unlike the *shor*, and the
  *parah* is almost always kept for exactly what her name says, calving and
  milk, only a small minority of females ever being made impotent.
- Rashi (to Tehillim 69:32; Chullin 60a; Avodah Zarah 8a): a *shor* is called
  that from the day it is born (see Vayikra 22:27), while *par* is a name it
  takes on only later.
- Radak (to Tehillim 69:32): a *shor* is big and a *par* is small, though he
  does not say whether he means in age or in build.

### shor (שור)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: from the biliteral shin-reish, borrowed from *shar* or
*sharir*, the umbilical cord (Yechezkel 16:4; Mishlei 3:8; Iyov 40:16). As the
cord at the child's navel joins him to his mother and carries all his growth, so
the ox holds the core of its strength in its belly. In Aramaic the shin becomes a
tav, giving *tor*; some linguists maintain that the Latin *taurus* derives from
that Aramaic *tor*. Whether *shor* means specifically a castrated ox is disputed:
many assume it does, but Rabbi Yonah Merzbach argues it cannot, since castrating
an animal is forbidden (Vayikra 22:24), so *shor* and *par* must stand alike on
that question.

### aleph (אלף)
Eight appearances in Tanach for cattle (Devarim 7:13, 28:4, 28:18, 28:51;
Tehillim 8:8, 50:10; Mishlei 14:4; Yeshayahu 30:24). The letter aleph in the
original paleo-Hebrew script (*ksav ivri*) looked like an ox; that script was
borrowed by the Phoenicians and then by the Greeks, and became the standard
alphabet. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
connects it to the biliteral lamed-peh, a strong hold: the chieftain with a firm
hold over those under him is an *aluf*, and the number one must command to earn
that title is an *elef*, a thousand; an *aleph* is on that reckoning the
strongest sort of ox. The Vilna Gaon (to Mishlei 14:4) instead connects *aleph*
to the Aramaic *yalif*, to learn, though his account of how *aleph* differs from
*shor* is involved. Malbim likewise ties the word to learning: an *aleph* is a
*trained* ox, one that has already learned how to work the land.

### bakar (בקר)
The collective, cattle. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the root ב-ק-ר means
investigating, seeking, probing. *Boker*, morning, is from it, the hour light
comes and one can begin to discern; *bikur cholim* likewise is not merely
visiting but finding out how the sick man is and what can be done for him. Rabbi
Yehoshua Steinberg: *bakar* is domesticated cattle precisely because such animals
need watching, supervision and defense from marauders, and that is the work of
the *boker* or *voker*, the "cowboy" (Amos 7:14), who must
also seek out greener pasture for them. Some have claimed the English slang
*buckaroo*, a cowboy, comes from that same *boker*/*voker*; but most linguists
agree it derives from the Spanish *vaquero*, from *vaca*,
"cow." Dr. Julian Mason, of the University of North Carolina, argued instead
that *buckaroo* comes from Gullah, the Creole spoken by African-Americans in
coastal South Carolina and Georgia, in which *buckra* means "white man."

## Rendering guidance

- שור: "ox." פר: "bull," and in a korban context keep the technical *par* where
  the halachah names it (*par he'elem davar*, *par kohen mashiach*).
- פרה: "cow," and "heifer" for *Parah Adumah* by long usage. עגל / עגלה: "calf,"
  with the sex supplied where it matters (*Eglah Arufah* is a female calf, and
  that is load-bearing).
- אלף in a pasuk: "ox" or "oxen," in a poetic register; "cattle" flattens it.
  בקר: "cattle"; בוקר of a person: "herdsman." "Cowboy" is accurate
  but too modern a register for most prose.
- Trap: English "ox" will swallow *shor*, *par* and *aleph* at once. Where
  HaKallir or a Midrash is counting the five names (as with the fivefold payment
  of Shemos 21:37), the translation has to keep five distinct English words, or
  the count collapses.
- Second trap: בקר is cattle and בוקר is morning, from one root. Check the
  vocalization before rendering.
- Do not let "cow" stand for *par*. The Hebrew pair is sexed, and *par* is the
  fertile male on Rabbi Pappenheim's whole reading.
