# Curse: arur / kelalah / allah / kavah

## Bottom line

*Arur* is the strong, unrestricted curse: **cursed, a curse** with nothing
specified, calling down whatever may come (the Vilna Gaon and Rabbi Mecklenburg;
Malbim runs *arur* the other way, see below). *Kelalah* is the weaker word, rooted
in *kal*, light: **disparagement**, or a **curse** that must name what it wishes
on its target. *Allah* is the curse that invokes Hashem by name (Radak) or, for Rabbi
Mecklenburg, one laid down conditionally: **oath-curse, imprecation**. *Kavah* is the curse pronounced
in public with the victim named outright. *Arur* and *kelalah* are not one
word; Shemos 22:27 uses both of them in a single pasuk.

## Distinctions

### arur (ארור)
- The Vilna Gaon: *arur* is the true and intense curse, which is why the Torah
  uses it for cursing a king. A king's powers are more overreaching than a
  judge's, so he can potentially hurt a man more, the more so as kingship is
  commonly an inherited position and the king's moral standing is generally
  beside the point; a man gravely mistreated by him may be tempted to curse him
  in earnest and not merely to disparage him.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: *arur* is broad and all-inclusive, wishing
  every misfortune on an adversary, and it can stand alone (one simply declares
  that an *arur* shall come upon him); it can also fall on something abstract,
  not only on what physically exists.
- Malbim reads *arur* narrowly the other way: it names the practical working-out
  of a curse in one specific thing of the victim's, his body or his property,
  and he links it to *me'erah*, decrease (Devarim 28:20), the shrinking of a
  man's yield.

### kelalah (קללה)
- The Vilna Gaon: *kelalah* is colloquially "curse" but literally a diminutive,
  something one invokes to belittle another and not truly a curse at all, from
  *kal*, light or easy; one who utters a *kelalah* about another dismisses him
  as unimportant. The Torah uses it for cursing a judge, since the harm a judge
  can do a man is not usually so damaging, the more so as society always strives
  to appoint upright judges, and the wronged party will make do with disparaging
  him rather than actually cursing him.
- Rabbi Mecklenburg: *kelalah* is the specific curse and cannot stand alone; the
  one who curses must say in what way the curse should take effect. On his
  reading it applies only to something that physically exists. This is how he
  reads Bereishis 12:3, that whoever curses Avraham with a specific *kelalah*
  receives an all-encompassing *arur* from Above.

### allah (אלה)
- Radak: an *allah* is the curse in which one expressly invokes Hashem to bring
  the misfortune about.
- Rabbi Mecklenburg disagrees: an *allah* is a conditional curse, laid down with
  a stipulation, as in "whoever does such and such shall be cursed."
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 14:3) relates א-ל-ה to א-ל-ח, the root of *elach*,
  becoming soiled, through the interchange of *hei* and *ches*: *allah*
  describes a conflict in which one person wishes ill on another, much as
  *elach* describes the conflict or incompatibility created when one element is
  introduced into another.

### kavah (קבה)
Malbim: a general curse declaring a man and everything of his cursed, uttered in
public with his name stated explicitly (compare the naming of Bamidbar 1:17).
*Arur* carries no such connotation of public naming.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 22:27: do not use "curse" twice. "A judge you shall not revile, and a
  prince among your people you shall not curse" keeps the Vilna Gaon's
  distinction; a note can carry Rabbi Mecklenburg's reading instead.
- Bereishis 12:3: "whoever curses you, I will curse" loses the pair. Render the
  first "whoever calls down a curse on you" and the second "him will I curse,"
  or gloss the Hebrew.
- *Arur* standing alone as a declaration: "Cursed be he." *Kelalah* alone:
  "curse" is acceptable, but "disparagement" is right wherever
  the passage plays on *kal*.
- *Allah*: "oath-curse" or "imprecation," never plain "curse," since the whole
  distinction is that Hashem's Name or a condition is attached. In a shevuah
  context, "the curse of the oath."
- Trap: English "curse" absorbs all four. Where two appear in one pasuk or one
  derashah, differentiate; where only one appears and nothing turns on it,
  "curse" is fine.
