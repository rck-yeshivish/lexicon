# Curse: arur / kelalah / allah / kavah

## Bottom line

*Arur* is the strong, unrestricted curse: **cursed, a curse** with nothing
specified, calling down whatever may come. *Kelalah* is the weaker word, rooted
in *kal*, light: **disparagement**, or a **curse** that must name what it wishes
on its target. *Allah* is the curse that either invokes Hashem by name or is laid
down conditionally: **oath-curse, imprecation**. *Kavah* is the curse pronounced
in public with the victim named outright. The trap is treating *arur* and
*kelalah* as one word; Shemos 22:27 uses both of them in a single pasuk, and the
switch is the whole point.

## Distinctions

### arur (ארור)
The Vilna Gaon: *arur* is the true and intense curse, which is why the Torah uses
it for cursing a king. A king's power is far-reaching and his office is
inherited, so a man wronged by him may be tempted to curse him in earnest.
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: *arur* is broad and all-inclusive, wishing every
misfortune on an adversary, and it can stand alone (one simply declares that an
*arur* shall come upon him); it can also fall on something abstract, not only on
what physically exists. Malbim reads *arur* narrowly the other way: it names the
practical working-out of a curse in one specific thing of the victim's, his body
or his property, and he links it to *me'erah*, decrease (Devarim 28:20), the
shrinking of a man's yield.

### kelalah (קללה)
The Vilna Gaon: *kelalah* is colloquially "curse" but literally a belittling,
from *kal*, light or slight; one who utters a *kelalah* about another writes him
off as of no account. The Torah uses it for cursing a judge, since a judge does
less harm than a king and the wronged party will settle for disparaging him.
Rabbi Mecklenburg: *kelalah* is the specific curse and cannot stand alone; the
one who curses must say in what way the curse should take effect. On his reading
it applies only to something that physically exists. This is how he reads
Bereishis 12:3, that whoever curses Avraham with a specific *kelalah* receives an
all-encompassing *arur* from Above.

### allah (אלה)
Radak: an *allah* is the curse in which one expressly invokes Hashem to bring the
misfortune about. Rabbi Mecklenburg disagrees: an *allah* is a conditional curse,
laid down with a stipulation, as in "whoever does such and such shall be cursed."

### kavah (קבה)
Malbim: a general curse declaring a man and everything of his cursed, uttered in
public with his name stated explicitly (compare the naming of Bamidbar 1:17).
*Arur* carries no such connotation of public naming.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 22:27: do not use "curse" twice. "A judge you shall not revile, and a
  prince among your people you shall not curse" keeps the Vilna Gaon's
  distinction; a note can carry Rabbi Mecklenburg's reading instead.
- Bereishis 12:3: "whoever curses you, I will curse" loses the pair. Render the
  first "whoever calls down a curse on you" and the second "him will I curse,"
  or gloss the Hebrew.
- *Arur* standing alone as a declaration: "Cursed be he." *Kelalah* alone in a
  mussar context: "curse" is acceptable, but "disparagement" is right wherever
  the passage plays on *kal*.
- *Allah*: "oath-curse" or "imprecation," never plain "curse," since the whole
  distinction is that Hashem's Name or a condition is attached. In a shevuah
  context, "the curse of the oath."
- Trap: English "curse" absorbs all four. Where two appear in one pasuk or one
  derashah, differentiate; where only one appears and nothing turns on it,
  "curse" is fine.
