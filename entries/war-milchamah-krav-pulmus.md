# War: milchamah / krav / pulmus (tarunya, kenigia)

## Bottom line

*Milchamah* is the common Biblical word for **war**, over three hundred times in
Tanach, with the verb *lochem*, **to wage war** (also around three hundred).
*Krav* also means **war, battle, combat**, in such phrases as *yom krav* and
*klei krav*. *Pulmus* is Mishnaic, from Greek *polemos*, war, found three times
in one Mishnah (*Sotah* 9:14). The article presents these, with *b'tarunya* and
*kenigia* (the last three Greek), as apparent synonyms and draws no line between
*milchamah* and *krav*; rendering *krav* "battle" and *milchamah* "war" where
both appear is our judgment. The disputes it records are over the root of
*milchamah* (bread and war from ל-ח-ם, or from ח-ם, heat, or "coming
together"), over *b'tarunya* (battle, or Greek tyranny), and over *kenigia*
(war, or hunting, which the Greek *kynigia* supports).

## Distinctions

### milchamah (מלחמה), lochem (לוחם)
Almost unanimously traced to the root ל-ח-ם, which also yields *lechem*
(bread) and meat. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) lists the three
senses as separate declensions without insinuating any link between them. Ibn
Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) collapses the food senses into one broad "staple
food," most instances of *lechem* being bread and some meat; the column adds
that cognate languages confirm this, Arabic *lahm* usually meaning meat and, in
the South Arabic dialect of Soqotra, fish. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) closes
the gap further: war can be figuratively characterized as the sword of the
combatants "eating" the slain. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 3:19, 10:9, 14:2) reads
the root as struggle, especially for survival: man struggles against nature for
his bread and against his fellow who would take it, and *milchamah* is that
second struggle between parties; by his phonetic etymology he links ל-ח-ם to
ל-א-ם (*leom*, nation), the existential fight of a polity to survive.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) takes a totally different approach,
deriving *lechem* and *milchamah* from the two-letter core ח-ם. The article
calls this noteworthy, since Pappenheim generally holds that a biliteral core
joins only with ה, א, מ, נ, ת, י, or ו to form a three-letter root, and here he
has it join an initial lamed. The core is heat: *cham*, hot; *cheimah*, anger
heated up; *chamah*, the sun; *chaman*, an idol in the shape of the sun; *chum*,
brown, the color of what looks sunburnt; *chemah*, butter, made by agitating and
so heating milk; *cheimas*, a flask for butter or like a butter vat; and
*chami*/*chamos*, parents-in-law, who shower a daughter-in-law with warm love.
Bread is baked by heat, and two sides go to war only once matters have heated to
the point of fighting.

The *Theological Dictionary of the Old Testament* (vol. 7) suggests another
approach: the basic idea of ל-ח-ם is coming together, people joining for a meal
or eaters in contact with food, and combatants in close contact on the
battlefield; its scholars adduce the Arabic *lahhama*, soldering or welding. The
column observes that the sense is already in Rabbinic Hebrew, where *halchamah*
is soldering (Yerushalmi Beitzah 1:5, Vayikra Rabbah 3:3, Shir HaShirim Rabbah
4:30, 5:10), as in Modern Hebrew, and that the Netziv (*Haamek Davar* to Bamidbar
28:2) already partially makes the point: *lechem* relates to *halchamah* since
food solders body to soul, and korbanos are called *lechem* because they cement
the bond between Klal Yisrael and Hashem. The Netziv does not explicitly connect
this to *milchamah*, as the scholars of the TDOT did.

As an aside the article notes Lachmi, brother of Golias (Divrei HaYamim I
20:5), and Lachman ben Ristak, a gentile named in the Gemara (Eruvin 63b).

### krav (קרב)
Root ק-ר-ב, meaning approaching, innards, and war, which the classical
lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak) present as three distinct senses.
The column says the truth is blurrier: to approach is to come nearer to the
inside of a perimeter and reach its innards, and in battle the attackers
approach while the defenders protect what lies within their line. Some verbs of
ק-ר-ב literally mean approaching but contextually refer to potential battle
(Shemos 14:10, Vayikra 20:10, Devarim 2:19 and 20:10, as the article gives them;
Vayikra 20:10 has no form of ק-ר-ב, by our check). The same root gives *korban*,
brought near to the mizbeach and, in a metaphysical sense, bringing the one who
offers it near to Hashem, and it is used of relations, two people coming
together (Bereishis 20:4, Vayikra 18:6, Devarim 22:14, Yeshayahu 8:3). Rav
Hirsch (to Tehillim 140:2) relates *akrav*, the scorpion, with an added initial
ayin, since it is always poised to attack. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras
(*Aholei Yehudah*) links *garav*, a kind of boil, to *krav* by the interchange
of gimmel and kuf: it itches so fiercely that a man feels impelled to wage war
on himself scratching it, even to bleeding. In Tanach *krav* appears in such
phrases as *yom krav*, day of battle (Zecharyah 14:3, Tehillim 78:9, Iyov
38:23), and *klei krav*, weapons (Koheles 9:18).

### pulmus (פולמוס)
Mishnaic, in the sense of war or battle, a loanword from Greek *polemos*, war. It
appears three times in one Mishnah (*Sotah* 9:14), listing takanos enacted at
the time of the *pulmus* of Vespasian, the *pulmus* of Titus, and the last
*pulmus*. The Greek is also the etymon of English *polemic*, whose sense has
shifted to a war of words while keeping the core idea of combat; so too Modern
Hebrew *pulmus* is often a controversy or debate, not an actual war.

### b'tarunya (טרוניא)
*Avodah Zarah* 2b-3a: in the future the nations will claim they would have kept
the Torah had it been given them, and Hashem will test them with the mitzvah of
sukkah; the Gemara asks how mitzvos can be given then, and answers that Hashem
does not come *b'tarunya* with His creations. The columnist says he always
understood the word as *batrunya*, "battle," Hashem not fighting against His
creations, but on further investigation it appears it should be read
*b'tarunya*, the beis a preposition. Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Musaf HeAruch*)
writes that *tarunya* is a Hebraization of the Greek *tyronia*, tyranny. On this
reading, the column explains, Hashem does not impose His will like a dictator,
but in His benevolence always gives people another chance. The same Greek word
is in the Targumim, which render *sarnei Plishtim* (Yehoshua 13:3, Shoftim 3:3
and 16:5-27, Shmuel I 5:8-11, 6:4, 6:16, 7:7, 29:7, Divrei HaYamim I 12:20) as
*turnaei Plishtaei* (in Modern Hebrew a *seren* is roughly a captain). Bereishis
Rabbah 42:4 says Rome imposed a *tironya* on the nations; *Matnos Kehunah* there
reads it as tyranny, that is, sovereignty or lordship, while Rabbi Natan of Rome
(*Sefer HeAruch*) reads it as a tax for military funding, and Dr. Alexander
Kohut (*HeAruch HaShaleim*) writes at great length to defend that reading.

### kenigia (קניגיא)
A hapax in the Bavli, so its precise meaning is not readily apparent: Gavriel is
destined to do *kenigia* with the Leviasan (*Bava Basra* 74b-75a). The Mainz
commentary ascribed to Rabbeinu Gershom (to *Bava Basra* 75a), and the Rashba,
define it as *milchamah*. The Rashbam (there) explains it as hunting, and
buttresses this from *Chullin* 60b, which asks whether Moshe was a *kenigi* or a
*balistiri*, that is a hunter or an archer (so Rashi and Rabbeinu Gershom there),
the point being that such detail about kosher and non-kosher animals could only
have come from Above.

The article adds that *kenigia* is indeed a loanword from Greek *kynigia*, which
in fact means hunting or pursuing predatory beasts, and which linguists derive
from *kyon*, dog (whence English *canine* and *cynicism*). By the shift of an
original k to h in the Germanic languages (Greek *kardia*, *keras*, Latin
*centum*; English *heart*, *horn*, *hundred*), *kyon* is related to English
*hound* and Yiddish and German *hunt*, and *kynigia* to English *hunt*. The
columnist once thought *kenigia* related to English *king*, German *Koenig*, and
Yiddish *kenig*; Rabbi Shaul Goodman assured him that those belong with *kin*,
*kind*, and *genus*, for the regent's rightful parentage.

## Rendering guidance

These are our rendering judgments, built on the article's meanings.

- *Milchamah*: "war." *Krav*: "battle" or "combat." Where both stand in one
  passage, keeping English "war" off *krav* keeps the two Hebrew words visible;
  the article itself treats them as apparent synonyms.
- *Yom krav* is "day of battle," not "day of war," and *klei krav* is "weapons"
  or literally "instruments of battle."
- *Pulmus* with a Roman name attached: "the war of Vespasian," or "the Vespasian
  campaign." Never "the polemic," and never "the controversy."
- *B'tarunya* in *Avodah Zarah* 3a: follow the *Musaf HeAruch*, as the article
  does, and render "Hashem does not act tyrannically toward His creatures,"
  noting the reading *batrunya*, "does not enter into battle with them," which
  the columnist says he once held, if the passage turns on it.
- *Kenigia*: "do battle with" follows Rabbeinu Gershom and the Rashba; "hunt"
  follows the Rashbam, and the article adds that the Greek *kynigia* means
  hunting. Pick one and say which; the Leviasan aggadah reads differently under
  each.
- Trap: a passage that plays on *lechem* and *milchamah* together (Radak, Rav
  Hirsch, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, and the TDOT all connect them) cannot be
  rendered without keeping the Hebrew visible; translate and gloss rather than
  substitute.
