# War: milchamah / krav / pulmus (tarunya, kenigia)

## Bottom line

*Milchamah* is the general Biblical word: **war**, the whole enterprise, over
three hundred times in Tanach, with the verb *lochem*, **to wage war**. *Krav*
is the closing of the two sides: **battle, combat**, the engagement itself
(*yom krav*, *klei krav*). *Pulmus* is Mishnaic and names a particular
**campaign** or war of a named enemy (the *pulmus* of Vespasian, of Titus). Do
not render *krav* "war" in a pasuk where *milchamah* also appears; *krav* is the
fighting, *milchamah* the conflict.

## Distinctions

### milchamah (מלחמה), lochem (לוחם)
Root ל-ח-ם, which also yields *lechem* (bread) and, in some pesukim, meat.
Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) lists the senses as separate
declensions and draws no link between them. Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
merges the food senses into one broad "staple food." Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*)
goes further and ties war to food: the sword of the combatants "eats" the slain.
Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 3:19, 10:9, 14:2) reads the root as struggle for
survival: man struggles against nature for his bread and against his fellow who
would take it, and *milchamah* is that second struggle between parties; he links
ל-ח-ם to ל-א-ם (*leom*, nation), the existential fight of a polity to survive.
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) derives *lechem* and *milchamah*
instead from the two-letter core ח-ם, heat, alongside *cham*, *cheimah* (anger
heated up), *chamah* (the sun), *chum*, *chemah* (butter), *cheimas* (flask), and
*chami/chamos* (parents-in-law, who warm a daughter-in-law with love). Bread is
baked by heat, and two sides go to war only once matters have heated to the point
of fighting. The Netziv (*Haamek Davar* to Bamidbar 28:2) connects *lechem* to
*halchamah*, soldering, since food solders body to soul, and korbanos are called
*lechem* because they cement the bond between Klal Yisrael and Hashem; he does
not extend this to *milchamah*.

### krav (קרב)
Root ק-ר-ב, which the classical lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak)
present as three senses: approaching, innards, and war. The senses shade into one
another: to approach is to come within a perimeter and reach its innards, and in
battle the attackers approach while the defenders guard what lies within their
line. Verbs of ק-ר-ב that plainly mean "approach" carry the sense of engaging in
battle (Shemos 14:10, Vayikra 20:10, Devarim 2:19 and 20:10). The same root gives
*korban*, brought near to the mizbeach and bringing the one who offers it near to
Hashem, and it is the Torah's idiom for marital relations (Bereishis 20:4,
Vayikra 18:6, Devarim 22:14, Yeshayahu 8:3). Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 140:2) adds
*akrav*, the scorpion, always poised to strike. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras
(*Aholei Yehudah*) links *garav*, a kind of boil, to *krav* by the interchange of
gimmel and kuf: it itches so fiercely that a man wages war on himself scratching
it. In Tanach *krav* appears in set phrases: *yom krav*, day of battle (Zecharyah
14:3, Tehillim 78:9, Iyov 38:23), and *klei krav*, weapons (Koheles 9:18).

### pulmus (פולמוס)
Mishnaic, a Greek loanword (*polemos*, war). It appears three times in one
Mishnah (*Sotah* 9:14), listing takanos enacted at the time of the *pulmus* of
Vespasian, the *pulmus* of Titus, and the last *pulmus*. It names a particular
war or campaign, not warfare in the abstract. In Modern Hebrew it has drifted to
"controversy" or "debate," as the English *polemic* has drifted to a war of
words; do not let the later sense color a Mishnaic *pulmus*.

### tarunya (תרוניא)
*Avodah Zarah* 3a: Hashem does not deal with His creations *b'tarunya*. The
common reading takes it as one word, *batrunya*, a fight. Rabbi Binyamin
Mussafia (*Musaf HeAruch*) instead reads the beis as the preposition and
*tarunya* as a Hebraization of Greek *tyronia*, tyranny, so the Gemara means that
Hashem does not impose His will by coercion but always gives another chance. The
same word is in the Targumim, which render *sarnei Plishtim* (Yehoshua 13:3,
Shoftim 3:3 and 16:5-27, Shmuel I 5:8-11, 6:4, 6:16, 7:7, 29:7, Divrei HaYamim I
12:20) as *turnaei Plishtaei*. Bereishis Rabbah 42:4 says Rome imposed a
*tironya* on the nations; *Matnos Kehunah* there reads it as tyranny, that is
lordship, while Rabbi Natan of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) reads it as a tax levied
for military funding.

### kenigia (קניגיא)
A hapax in the Bavli: Gavriel is destined to make *kenigia* with the Leviasan
(*Bava Basra* 74b-75a). The Mainz commentary ascribed to Rabbeinu Gershom (to
*Bava Basra* 75a), and the Rashba, define it simply as *milchamah*. The Rashbam
(there) reads it as hunting, and proves it from *Chullin* 60b, which asks whether
Moshe was a *kenigi* or a *balistiri*, that is a hunter or an archer (so Rashi and
Rabbeinu Gershom there), the point being that such detail about kosher and
non-kosher animals could only have come from Above.

## Rendering guidance

- *Milchamah*: "war." *Krav*: "battle" or "combat." Where both stand in one
  passage, English "war" must not swallow *krav*; the Hebrew separates the
  conflict from the engagement.
- *Yom krav* is "day of battle," not "day of war," and *klei krav* is "weapons"
  or literally "instruments of battle."
- *Pulmus* with a Roman name attached: "the war of Vespasian," or "the Vespasian
  campaign." Never "the polemic," and never "the controversy."
- *B'tarunya* in *Avodah Zarah* 3a: follow the Musaf HeAruch and render "Hashem
  does not act tyrannically toward His creatures," noting the older reading
  "does not enter into battle with them" if the passage turns on it.
- *Kenigia*: "do battle with" follows Rabbeinu Gershom and the Rashba; "hunt"
  follows the Rashbam. Pick one and say which; the Leviasan aggadah reads
  differently under each.
- Trap: a passage that plays on *lechem* and *milchamah* together (Radak, Rav
  Hirsch, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim all build on the shared root) cannot be
  rendered without keeping the Hebrew visible; translate and gloss rather than
  substitute.
