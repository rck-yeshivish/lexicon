# River: nahar / yeor / nachal

## Bottom line

*Nahar* is the default **river**, a natural watercourse fed by tributaries.
*Yeor* is the Nile and rivers that behave like it, a river that floods past its
banks; Rashi (to Shemos 7:19) glosses it **an irrigation canal**, and standing
alone it is **the River**, the Nile. *Nachal* is a **brook** or **wadi**, variously
explained as the river that cuts a valley, the seasonal runoff, or the river that
runs to the sea, and the same word
names the valley itself. Rabbi Malkiel Tzvi Tannenbaum *proposes* that *nahar* and *yeor* are only sharply distinguished
when they appear together, so that Rashi's canal is what *yeor* means beside a
*nahar*; apart, on his proposal, either word can mean a river of any kind.

## Distinctions

### nahar (נהר)
- Rashi (to Shemos 7:19): a river in the ordinary sense, as against the *yeor*
  that Aharon was also to stretch his staff over.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) derives it from the two-letter ה-ר,
  which he reads as protruding or standing out, the core also behind *har*, a
  mountain, *herayon*, pregnancy, and *yuharah*, haughtiness: a *nahar* is the
  river into which smaller streams flow, which makes the bigger river stick out
  vis-a-vis those tributaries.
- Malbim (to Yirmiyahu 46:7): a *nahar* and a *nachal* alike keep their water
  within their banks, simply pushing it along a forward current and not past the
  banks, as against the *yeor*, which tends to overflow. In another discussion
  Malbim (to Yeshayahu 19:5) sizes the three: a *nahar* is shallow, short and
  narrow, a *nachal* is wider but also not deep, and a *yeor* denotes an even
  smaller stream. There Malbim *again* notes that the *yeor* differs from the
  other two in tending to overflow, so the two passages agree on that point.
- Some also explain that a *nachal* is a seasonal river caused by rainwater
  flowing down from the mountains, while a *nahar* is a river that continues to
  flow the entire year.

### yeor (יאור)
Before the plague of blood Hashem tells Aharon to stretch his staff over the
different bodies of water in Egypt, among them the *neharos* and the *ye'orim*
(Shemos 7:19).
- Rashi there: a *nahar* is the sort of river we are familiar with, while a
  *yeor* is a man-made irrigation canal that brings water to faraway fields.
  This *implies*, Rabbi Klein reasons, that a *yeor* is man-made and a *nahar*
  naturally occurring. But that assumption is belied by the fact that the Nile
  is called a *yeor* and is one of the four rivers that flowed from Gan Eden
  (Bereishis 2:11), which cannot possibly be man-made. Rabbi Malkiel Tzvi
  Tannenbaum of Lomzha (1847-1910), author of the responsa *Divrei Malkiel*,
  *proposes* a resolution by context: on their own, *nahar* and *yeor* can each
  mean a man-made river or a natural one; used together, as at the plague of
  blood, each takes one specific meaning the other does not, *nahar* the natural
  river and *yeor* the artificial one.
- Malbim: the mark of a *yeor* is that it tends to overflow and flood the
  surrounding area, and most instances of *yeor* in Tanach refer specifically to
  the Nile, which behaved that way, though he concedes that in two or three
  places *yeor* refers to a different river.
- Rabbi Pappenheim takes *yeor* from the letter *reish* itself, which he reads
  as throwing or shooting: the waters of a *yeor* shoot downstream as though
  thrown by the forces of nature, so a *yeor* is a river whose waters flow with
  especially violent force, and in Tanach it is used only of the Nile and the
  Tigris. Pappenheim *admits* that a borrowed sense of *yeor*, which also
  appears in Tanach, is a man-made irrigation duct, which is not a river as such
  but the river's artificial tributaries.

Rabbi Klein then bridges the two: in light of what Malbim wrote, he explains
that *yeor* recalls the fact that its waters tend to overflow past the banks,
giving the illusion of the river itself shooting its waters outwards.

Chasam Sofer (*Toras Moshe* to Bereishis 41:1): a *yeor* is a river whose end
can be seen, a *nahar* one whose ends cannot.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 41:1): *yeor* first meant
the Nile specifically and later any river, so that in Daniel (10:4, 12:5) it can
mean the Chidekel.

### nachal (נחל)
Means both a river and a valley.

- There are two anonymous accounts of the connection: some explain that a
  *nachal* is the type of river that causes erosion, thereby creating a valley
  in the manner of the Grand Canyon, which in English we call a *wadi*, a word
  borrowed from Arabic; others explain that a *nachal* is the seasonal runoff
  from the mountains after rain, as against the year-round *nahar*.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866-1935) restricts the word further:
  *nachal* applies only to a river that flows into a sea, as it says, "All the
  *nechalim* go to the sea" (Koheles 1:7), and not to any type of river. Indeed,
  Rabbi Pappenheim in *Yerios Shlomo* gives the root as ח-ל, circular motion,
  which aptly describes that same cycle: the *nachal* empties into the ocean,
  and the waters of the ocean in turn flow back into the river.

## Rendering guidance

- *Nahar* standing alone: "river." *Nahar* beside *yeor* (Shemos 7:19): "rivers
  and canals," following Rashi, and note that on Tannenbaum's proposal the
  pairing is what forces the split.
- *Yeor* of Egypt: "the Nile," or "the River" where the pasuk is being quoted and
  the name would be an intrusion. Only render "canal" where a second word for
  river is present in the same phrase.
- *Nachal*: "brook" for a small flowing stream, "wadi" where the dryness matters,
  "valley" where the topography is the subject (and say which you chose if the
  passage puns on the two).
- Trap: English "river" will absorb all three and erase the pesukim that list
  them together. A verse that names *neharos* and *ye'orim* and *agamim* is
  cataloguing kinds of water; give each a different English word.
- Second trap: "stream" is a poor default for *nachal*, because Malbim at
  Yeshayahu 19:5 makes *nachal* the widest of the three and the *yeor* the
  smallest stream of the three. Where you follow that passage, do not also call
  *yeor* a "great river."
- Where a source builds on Rabbi Pappenheim's *nahar*/*har* link (the river that
  stands out above its tributaries), the English has to keep "river" and let the
  note carry the derivation; no English word does both.
