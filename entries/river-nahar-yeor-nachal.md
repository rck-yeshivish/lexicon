# River: nahar / yeor / nachal

## Bottom line

*Nahar* is the default **river**, a natural watercourse fed by tributaries.
*Yeor* is the Nile and rivers that behave like it, a river that floods past its
banks; where it stands beside *nahar* it means **an irrigation canal** (Rashi to
Shemos 7:19), and standing alone it is **the River**, the Nile. *Nachal* is a
**brook** or **wadi**, the seasonal watercourse that cuts a valley and runs to
the sea, and the same word names the valley itself. The one thing not to get
wrong: *nahar* and *yeor* are only sharply distinguished when they appear
together (Rabbi Malkiel Tzvi Tannenbaum); apart, either can mean a river of any
kind.

## Distinctions

### nahar (נהר)
Rashi (to Shemos 7:19): a river in the ordinary sense, as against the *yeor*
that Aharon was also to stretch his staff over. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
(*Cheshek Shlomo*) derives it from the two-letter ה-ר, which he reads as
protruding or standing out, the core also behind *har*, a mountain, *herayon*,
pregnancy, and *yuharah*, haughtiness: a *nahar* is the greater river into which
smaller streams empty, so that it stands out against them. Malbim (to Yirmiyahu
46:10): a *nahar* keeps its water within its banks and drives it forward in a
current rather than flooding out. Elsewhere Malbim (to Yeshayahu 19:5) makes it
the shallow, short, narrow one of the three, with *nachal* wider but likewise
not deep. One further reading the article records: a *nahar* flows year round,
while a *nachal* is seasonal.

### yeor (יאור)
Rashi: the man-made channel that carries water out to distant fields. Against
reading that as the word's meaning stands the fact that the Nile is called a
*yeor* and is one of the four rivers of Gan Eden (Bereishis 2:11), which cannot
be man-made. Rabbi Malkiel Tzvi Tannenbaum (*Divrei Malkiel*) resolves it by
context: alone, *nahar* and *yeor* each cover any river, natural or dug; side by
side, as at the plague of blood, they divide, *nahar* taking the natural river
and *yeor* the artificial one. Malbim: the mark of a *yeor* is that it overflows
and floods the land around it, which is exactly how the Nile behaves; he grants
that in two or three places *yeor* names some other river. Rabbi Pappenheim
takes *yeor* from the letter *reish* itself, which he reads as throwing or
shooting, so a *yeor* is a river whose water is driven with unusual force; for
him it is used in Tanach only of the Nile and the Tigris, with the irrigation
duct a borrowed sense naming the river's artificial tributaries rather than a
river proper.

### nachal (נחל)
Means both the watercourse and the valley it runs in. Two accounts of that pair
are given: a *nachal* is the river that erodes its bed into a valley, what
English calls a wadi (a borrowing from Arabic), or a *nachal* is the seasonal
runoff from the mountains after rain, as against the year-round *nahar*. Rabbi
Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer restricts the word further: a *nachal* is specifically a
watercourse that empties into a sea, from *kol hanechalim holchim el hayam*
(Koheles 1:7). Rabbi Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) gives its core as ח-ל, circular
motion, which fits that same cycle, the *nachal* draining to the sea and the sea
returning its water to the *nachal*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Nahar* standing alone: "river." *Nahar* beside *yeor* (Shemos 7:19): "rivers
  and canals," following Rashi, and note that the pairing is what forces the
  split.
- *Yeor* of Egypt: "the Nile," or "the River" where the pasuk is being quoted and
  the name would be an intrusion. Only render "canal" where a second word for
  river is present in the same phrase.
- *Nachal*: "brook" for a small flowing stream, "wadi" where the dryness matters,
  "valley" where the topography is the subject (and say which you chose if the
  passage puns on the two).
- Trap: English "river" will absorb all three and erase the pesukim that list
  them together. A verse that names *neharos* and *ye'orim* and *agamim* is
  cataloguing kinds of water; give each a different English word.
- Second trap: "stream" is a poor default for *nachal*, because Malbim at
  Yeshayahu 19:5 makes *nachal* the *widest* of the three. Where you follow that
  passage, do not also call *yeor* a "great river."
- Where a source builds on Rabbi Pappenheim's *nahar*/*har* link (the river that
  stands out above its tributaries), the English has to keep "river" and let the
  note carry the derivation; no English word does both.
