# Remember: zachor / pakad

## Bottom line

*Zachor* is remembering in the mind: **remember, call to mind**. *Pakad* is
remembering that issues in action: **attend to, take note of** (and in the
right context "count," "appoint," "punish," "deposit"). Rabbi Shlomo Aharon
Wertheimer, capsulizing Malbim's stance: *zechirah* is in thought, *pekidah* is
in action.

## Distinctions

The Torah gives six commandments to remember: the Shabbos (Shemos
20:8), the Exodus (Devarim 16:3), that
Amalek attacked after the Exodus (Devarim 25:17), the giving of the Torah at Har
Sinai (Devarim 4:9), how the Jews angered Hashem in the wilderness (Devarim
9:7), and what Hashem did to Miriam when she spoke slander (Devarim 24:9). In
all but one of those commandments the Torah uses *zachor*. The Gemara (Rosh Hashanah 32b)
essentially recognizes *pakad* as a synonym of *zachor*, asserting that
*pikdonos* equals *zichronos*, so that pesukim which speak of Hashem
remembering with a cognate of *pakad* may also be included in the *zichronos* of
Mussaf on Rosh Hashanah.

### zachor (זכור / זכר)
Root ז-כ-ר.

- The most basic definition is Rabbi Moshe Ibn Ezra's (1055-1138): *zachor* is
  to remember something one once knew and forgot, as against *shamor*, to keep
  in mind something one currently knows.
- Malbim points to two differences between the two kinds of remembering. First,
  *zachor* simply refers to remembering in one's mind, mentally recalling a fact
  or idea and doing nothing else. Alternatively, Malbim explains that *zachor*
  denotes constantly remembering something over a long span of time.

Rabbi Naftali Hertz Treves of Frankfurt (1493-1540) *postulates* in *Sefer
HaGur* that *zachor* itself implies remembering as an act of mercy, while
*pakad* does not, since *pakad* sometimes refers to remembering in order to
punish. He offers this to resolve a difficulty: Rashi enigmatically explains
that Hashem's remembering Noach (Bereishis 8:1) means He switched His trait of
strict judgment for His trait of mercy, but, Rabbi Klein observes, there is no
textual basis for that explanation, especially since that very pasuk uses the
name Elokim, which implies strict judgment, rather than the Tetragrammaton,
which implies mercy.

The noun *zecher* is not always "memory." The Italian scholar Rabbi Shlomo of
Urbino points out in *Ohel Moed* that *zecher* sometimes refers to the **scent**
of a thing (Vayikra 6:8, Hoshea 14:8, Tehillim 20:4), in the same sense that a
memory of something is a whiff of that idea and is not it itself.

Shadal (to Shemos 20:8): *zachor* said of Shabbos does not mean remembering
something past but keeping something present in mind: to remember each week that
it is Shabbos and not ignore the day's holiness by forgetting it.

Shadal (to Vayikra 2:2): the original, concrete meaning of *zachor* is smelling.
Smell fixes a thing in the mind so it can be recalled without being present, and
in a later, more abstract sense *zachor* came to mean remembering any fact or
idea after it is no longer before one.

### pakad (פקד)
Root פ-ק-ד. Its range in Tanach is so wide that its core meaning is quite
difficult to pin down: to count (Bamidbar 1:21, 2:32, Shmuel I 15:4), to be
missing (Bamidbar 31:49, Yeshayahu 38:10), to command (Tehillim 19:9, 119:56,
Ezra 1:2), to appoint (Bamidbar 1:50, 31:14, Esther 2:3, Tehillim 109:6), to
punish (Shemos 20:5, Hoshea 2:15), to bring about death (Bamidbar 16:29,
Yirmiyahu 46:21), to visit (Rashi to Nedarim 39b; Kli Yakar to Bamidbar 16:29),
and to deposit (Vayikra 5:23).

- Malbim's first difference accounts for the whole spread: *pakad* denotes
  remembering something in order to take some sort of action, for better or for
  worse, so that it primarily means remembering something or someone in order to
  attend to it, deal with it, or otherwise pay attention to it. The most basic
  way of paying attention to a thing is to determine whether it is actually
  present, hence "to count" and "to be absent"; another way of dealing with
  someone is to give him instructions about what to do, hence "command" and
  "appoint"; a third is to give him what he deserves, whether to punish him, in
  some cases even to bring about his death, or simply to visit him; and a way to
  deal with an item is to deposit it in someone else's hands to take care of.
  Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866-1935) capsulizes this stance: *zechirah*
  is in thought, *pekidah* is in action.
- In Malbim's second approach *pakad* simply denotes one fleeting act of
  remembering, enough that a man can take the actions related to that memory,
  after which he can forget about it.

(Rabbi Dr. Asher Weiser writes that *kapdanus*, meticulousness or strictness,
relates to פ-ק-ד by way of metathesis, in the sense that one who is *makpid*
pays attention to something to the utmost degree and is particular about all the
details.)

Rabbi Saadia ben David Al-Dhamari, a fifteenth-century Yemenite scholar, reads
the pair by elapsed time instead: *pakad* is remembering after a long time,
*zachor* after a shorter one. Hence *pakad* when Hashem said He remembered the
Jews and would redeem them from Mitzrayim (Shemos 3:16), after centuries of
exile, and when He remembered Sarah and allowed her to conceive (Bereishis
21:1), after decades of barrenness; but *zachor* for Noach, who had been in the
teivah only about a year (Bereishis 8:1), and for Rachel, barren only a few
years (Bereishis 30:22).

### zachar (זכר, "male")
*Zachor* *seems* to be related to *zachar*, "male." The interplay shows up in a
Talmudic anecdote (Bava Basra 21b) in which Dovid HaMelech's general Yoav
explains that he did not kill the female Amalekites, because his teacher had
vowelized the *zecher* of "erase the remembrance of Amalek" (Devarim 25:19) as
*zachar*.

Rabbi Shimon ben Tzemach Duran (1361-1444), the Rashbatz (also Tashbatz), writes
in his commentary to Avos 5:12 that men have better memory, or better potential
for memory, than women, and buttresses the claim with the observation that
*zachar* and *zachor* *seem* related. On the flip side, he adds, the Hebrew word
for women, *nashim*, is related to the word for forgetfulness (see *nashani* in
Bereishis 41:51 and *teshi* in Devarim 32:18).

Rabbi Moshe de Leon (1240-1305), the Kabbalist who first published the *Zohar*,
writes in *Sefer HaRimmon* that *zachor* is indeed related to *zachar*: in the
relationship between a man and a woman, the man is the initiator who provides
the nourishing kernel that the woman receives and develops into something
greater. On that basis he accounts for a curious phrase in *Yaaleh V'Yavo*,
where we ask Hashem to remember us with both forms of remembering
(*v'yizacher*/*zichroneinu* and *v'yipakeid*/*pikdoneinu*) and then beseech Him
in two ways, *zachreinu* and *ufakdeinu*, for two kinds of bounty: **tovah** is
His direct bestowal of good, paralleling the male's role in giving the kernel,
so it is no wonder we say *zachreinu bo l'tovah*; **berachah** is His blessing
what we already have by letting it grow and multiply, mirroring the female's
role in incubating and gestating what the male provided, so we say *ufakdeinu bo
livrachah*.

Rabbi Klein ties Treves back to this: the explanation dovetails with the
Kabbalistic terminology, since Hashem's trait of mercy reflects unfettered
Divine influence with the focus on the Giver, associating *zachor* and mercy
with the male paradigm, while His trait of judgment reflects a limited Divine
influence focused on the recipient and on whether he deserves to receive, so
that *pakad* and judgment follow the female model.

Rabbi Levi Yitzchok of Berditchev (1740-1810) in *Kedushas Levi* (to Bereishis
21:1) explains that *pakad* alludes to the female element because it implies
that the one remembered has already received his due. When Hashem promises to
give something, the gift is already considered to have come into existence at
the moment of the promise, since vis-a-vis Hashem there is no difference between
past, present and future; any difference in time is only according to our
perception. So if in the present it looks as though the promise has not yet been
granted, that only means its fulfillment is hidden from us until He decides to
reveal it. Hence *pakad* at Yitzchak's birth: from Hashem's timeless vantage
Yitzchak already existed before he was born, but from the receiver's viewpoint,
Sarah's, it had been hidden away until He remembered to reveal the gift's
existence to her.

Finally, Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865) explains that *zachar* is
related to *zachor* in that a man's male descendants are typically his legacy,
by which he is remembered in This World: daughters generally marry into other
families and become part of the husband's household, while sons carry on the
name of their father's family.

Shadal (to Bereishis 12:2): *zachar* is related to *zachor*, because a man's
male offspring are his remembrance in this world after he passes away.

## Rendering guidance

- *Zachor* in the six mitzvos of remembrance: **remember**, plain.
- *Pakad* of Hashem toward a person: **took note of**, or **remembered** with
  the consequence named, since in the cases cited the pasuk supplies one
  (Sarah conceives, the redemption begins). "Visited" is the old rendering and
  reads archaic.
- *Pakad* in a census or a list: **counted**, **mustered**; *nifkad*:
  **missing**, not "unremembered."
- Trap: English "remember" will absorb both words. In a passage that contrasts
  them, or in *Yaaleh V'Yavo* where both appear in one breath, hold the
  distinction: *zachreinu* "remember us," *ufakdeinu* "and take account of us."
- *Zecher* is not automatically "memory." Check whether the pasuk wants
  **trace** or **scent** (Hoshea 14:8) before defaulting.
- Of the six commandments, the one that does not use *zachor* is Devarim 4:9
  (*hishamer lecha... pen tishkach*); Devarim 9:7 does use *zechor*, alongside
  *al tishkach*.
