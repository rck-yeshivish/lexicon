# Jerusalem: Tziyon / Yerushalayim (Ir David, tziyun)

## Bottom line

*Tziyon* and *Yerushalayim* do not necessarily name the same place. *Tziyon* is
the **City of David**, a separate walled city that only later came inside the
walls of **Yerushalayim**; the nechamah formula, "the mourners of Tziyon and
Yerushalayim," already implies that they are two. Where *tziyon* stands for the
Beis HaMikdash or for Klal Yisrael, it is, on Rabbi Tukachinsky's reading, the
common noun *tziyun*, "outstanding", not the place. Where the Hebrew uses
*Tziyon* for the whole city, some sources suggest it serves as a synonym, though
Rabbi Klein proposes that synecdoche, the part naming the whole, may be the more
accurate term.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether Tziyon is a separate city.**
- Rabbi Ashtori HaParchi, the Radbaz, and Rabbi Elazar Azkiri all prove from
  Melachim I 8:1 (and Divrei HaYamim II 5:2) that they are two: Shlomo gathered
  the elders *in Yerushalayim* in order to bring the Aron up *from the City of
  David, which is Tziyon*.
- Rashi (to Sotah 9a, Yoma 77b) says flatly that Tziyon is outside Yerushalayim.
- Rabbi Yechiel Michel Tukachinsky disagrees with some of this: *Tziyon* in
  Tanach is not always an adjoining city but at times a neighborhood inside
  Yerushalayim.
- The Zohar (*Idra Zuta* 296b) says the two are two spiritual levels, one the
  aspect of *rachamim* and the other the aspect of *din*, which Rabbi Klein
  takes as suggesting that both names denote one physical place.

**Where Har Tziyon is.** Tehillim 48:3 places it *yarkesei tzafon*, in the
north.

1. **North of the city.** Ibn Ezra and Radak: the northern part of
   Yerushalayim.
2. **Har HaZeisim.** Rashi cites Dunash ibn Labrat that Har Tziyon is another
   name for the Mount of Olives.
3. **South, and "north" is not geography.** *Midrash Socher Tov* asks the
   question outright, that Har Tziyon lies to the south, and answers that
   "north" here points to the intense elation felt at a *chatas* slaughtered
   north of the mizbeach (Vayikra 1:11). The thirteenth-century Rabbi Yosef
   Tuv-Elem (Bonfils), in his super-commentary *Tzafnas Panei'ach* to Vayikra
   1:11, argues for the south against Ibn Ezra, on the grounds that the
   Midrash's author, Rabbi Yochanan, actually lived in Eretz Yisrael, and that
   he himself lived in Yerushalayim and saw that Har Tziyon lies south of the
   old city. He grants that Ibn Ezra visited Yerushalayim as well, but assumes
   Ibn Ezra came only after he had already written, mistakenly, that Har Tziyon
   lies to the north.

Rabbi Klein's own comment is that none of the first three sources agree with what
we today call Har Tziyon, which is southwest of the Old City, and that the
Midrash makes this very point.

**How wide "the mountain of Tziyon" is.**

- HaParchi, a prominent rabbinic topographer, concedes that "mountains of
  Tziyon" or "mountain of Tziyon" can mean not Har Tziyon itself but the
  mountains of that general vicinity, taking in Har Tziyon, Har HaMoriah, and
  Har HaZeisim, so that when Tziyon appears alongside the Mikdash it can include
  the Temple Mount as well.
- Tukachinsky reaches the same place from the other side: where *tziyon* means
  the Mikdash, Har Tziyon sometimes refers to Har HaMoriah.

## Distinctions

### Tziyon (ציון)
Sometimes called *Ir David* and *Metzudas David*, and walled on its own (Rashi to Shmuel II
8:7). Yerushalayim's outer walls were only later extended to enclose it, which
may have happened in the late first Beis HaMikdash period or at the beginning of
the second. Because it was added later, its halachic status for eating
*kodashim* and *maaser* may not have matched the rest of the city, and the inner
wall called *chomas beis pagi* went on marking Yerushalayim proper off from
Tziyon even in the late second Beis HaMikdash period, by which time the two had
merged; that wall marked out where within greater Yerushalayim one might eat
from the korbanos. The Vilna Gaon (to Yeshayahu 1:9): the populations differed
too, the noblemen living in Tziyon and the common people in Yerushalayim.

The Rogatchover Gaon, an anti-Zionist rabbinic figure, was once asked his
opinion of Zionism. He did not answer directly but replied playfully that Tziyon is an area outside Yerushalayim
proper where gentile heretics historically gathered: the Mishnah rules that
spittle found in the Upper Marketplace is presumed to come from a non-Jew
(*Shekalim* 8:1), and he explained that this is the area called Tziyon. By
pointing out that Tziyon in Mishnaic times was essentially a slum he registered
his disapproval of the secular Zionists, whom he held to be like those
historical heretics.

### Yerushalayim (ירושלים)
Written *Yerushalem* in Tanach and in Aramaic, a singular form, from which the
English "Jerusalem" comes; the traditional pronunciation *Yerushalayim* is a
dual, which is heard as alluding to two cities joined into one. The Targum
reads *shevet achim gam yachad* (Tehillim 133:1) as the unity of the twin
cities, and Rabbi Klein's illustration is Buda and Pest becoming Budapest.

### tziyon as a common noun
Rabbi Tukachinsky: at Yoel 4:17 and Tehillim 2:6 *tziyon* names the Beis
HaMikdash, and at Yeshayahu 51:3 it names Klal Yisrael. In those places it is
not a proper name at all but a common noun meaning "outstanding," derived from
*tziyun*.

## Rendering guidance

- Never translate *Tziyon* as "Jerusalem" or fold the two into one name. In
  *HaMakom yenachem eschem b'soch she'ar aveilei Tziyon v'Yerushalayim*, both
  names stand.
- Where the tefillos say *v'sechezenah eineinu b'shuvcha l'Tziyon* or
  *u'mizbeach chadash b'Tziyon tachin*, they name the Mikdash by Tziyon. Keep
  "Tziyon" in the English and note the synecdoche; writing "Jerusalem" erases
  the figure. The same holds at Yirmiyahu 31:5 and Tehillim 132:14.
- Trap: an English text that uses "Zion" loosely for the Jewish people, the
  city, and the Temple in successive lines makes all three sound like one
  referent. Follow Tukachinsky and distinguish: the place, the Mikdash, and
  Klal Yisrael.
