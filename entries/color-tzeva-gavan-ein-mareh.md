# Color: tzeva / gavan / ein / mareh

## Bottom line

*Tzeva* is **color** in the sense of dye: the colorant, and the color a thing
has been given (from *tzovea*, dyeing; for Shadal, an artificial color). *Ein*,
construct of *ayin*, is a thing's own single **color**; Rambam sometimes uses
*tzeva* for colors, but in other cases consistently uses *ein*. *Mareh* is the
**appearance**, the whole picture of a thing, which could include its color
(Malbim); for Shadal it is a sight that may include more than one color. *Gavan*
is Aramaic, **color** or **shade**, and belongs to Talmudic and Targumic
language, not to Tanach. Between *tzeva* and *gavan* Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein would
say the difference is simply one of origin: *tzeva* is the native Hebrew word, *gavan*
an Aramaic borrowing of an Indo-European word.

## Where the authorities disagree

**ein against mareh.** Malbim (to Vayikra 13:5): not synonyms. *Ein* is the
color proper; *mareh* is the full picture of the thing, which may take its color
in. Shadal draws a similar line (*mareh* a sight that might include more than
one color, *ein* one specific color) and adds the third term: *ein* and *mareh*
both name a thing's natural look, while *tzeva* is an artificial color produced by painting
or dyeing.

**How wide ein reaches.** Raavad I (*Emunah HaRamah* 1:6) explains why it means
color: the eye is the organ that detects color, exactly as *lashon* (tongue) and
*safah* (lip) came to mean language. The Maharal (*Gur Aryeh* to Vayikra 13:5)
likewise takes *ein* to mean color primarily, and adds that it can name any
property by which one thing is compared to another, even where nothing
visual is involved, which is how *k'ein* and *m'ein* come to mean resembling or
being a microcosm of something.

**tzeva against gavan.** Three views:

1. **Concrete against abstract.** Rabbi Meir Hellman (*Levush HeAron*): *tzeva*
   is the colorant, the substance used for dyeing; *gavan* is the abstract color
   a thing is perceived to have. Rabbi Shalom Pushinsky refuted this over a
   century ago from Yirmiyahu 12:9, where *tzavua* describes a colorful bird of
   prey with no dyeing in sight, though that usage occurs only once.
2. **Natural against man-made.** The botanist Aharon Aaronsohn: *gavan* is a
   thing's own natural color, *tzeva* a man-made color imposed on it.
3. **Not a semantic difference at all.** Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own conclusion
   ("I would say"): *tzeva* is the native Hebrew word and *gavan* an
   Aramaic borrowing of an Indo-European word, so no distinction of meaning need
   be sought. Rabbi Shalom Pushinsky argues that *gavan* is native neither to
   Hebrew nor to Aramaic and does not come from a Semitic language at all,
   tracing it to Old Persian *gün/gune* ("color"). He supports this from its
   absence in the earlier Aramaic works (the Targumim, save *sasgavana* above
   and the relatively late Targum to Esther) and its occurrence only in later
   Aramaic heavy with Persian influence, such as the Bavli; he adds that on this
   showing the vav of ג-ו-ן was originally sounded as the vowel *u* and only
   later as a consonant. Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionary of
   Hebrew, likewise derives *gavan* from Old Persian, and Dr. Alexander Kohut
   connects it to the Sanskrit *gaona/gauna*, which apparently also means
   "color"; Sanskrit and Old Persian both belong to the
   Indo-Iranian branch of Indo-European and share a common ancestor.

**Whether the Aramaic root ג-ו-ן has one meaning or three.** Rabbi Eliyahu
Bachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*, *Meturgeman*) gives three separate entries: form or
likeness (Targum of *k'demus*, Tehillim 58:5), color, and topic or subject
(Targum of *inyan*, Koheles 1:13, 2:23). Rabbi Aharon Fuld, in his glosses to
*Sefer HaTishbi*, holds them to be one idea in three shades: color distinguishes
things to the eye, likeness distinguishes them in form, and topic distinguishes
them thematically. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur adds that the ordinary *k'gon* ("for
example," *k'gavna* in Zoharic Aramaic) comes from this root.

## Distinctions

### tzeva (צבע)
Root צ-ב-ע. The word does not occur in Chumash and appears only four times in
Tanach, three of them in one pasuk: Sisera's mother imagining *tzeva'im* among
the spoils (Shoftim 5:30), where Radak explains *tzeva'im* as a single garment
made of many colors, and Yirmiyahu 12:9, the *tzavua* bird of prey hated by the
other birds. Beyond these, the given name Tziv'on (Bereishis 36) and the
place-name Tzevo'im (Nechemiah 11:34, Shmuel I 13:18) are perhaps from the root
as well. In the Mishnah the root appears in many different contexts: wool given to be dyed one
color and dyed another (*Bava Kamma* 9:4), dyeing with *orlah* peels (*Orlah*
3:1), telling a red spot of blood from paint (*Niddah* 9:6). The peacock's three
hundred and sixty-five colors are its *tzivonim* (*Tanchuma Tazria* 2; see
*Bereishis Rabbah* 7:4). A man who is superficial or two-faced is sometimes
called *tzavua*, literally colored, because, as Rashi explains (to *Sotah* 22b), the
color of the outside does not match the inside. Rambam uses the root for dyeing
the hair black (*Avodah Zarah* 12:10), the coloring of *tzitzis* (*Tzitzis*
2:3), dyeing on Shabbos (*Shabbos* 9:13), red spotting (*Issurei Biah* 9:36 and
*Metamei Mishkav U'Moshav* 4:11), and *shemittah* fruit used for dyeing
(*Shemittah* 5:1).

Gershom Scholem ("Colours and Their Symbolism in Jewish Tradition and
Mysticism," pp. 85-86) doubts that Tanach has a general concept of colour at
all: *tzeva*, the word later Rabbinic literature uses, appears only in the Song
of Devorah (Shoftim 5:30) and may mean a many-coloured garment; where a word for
colour is expected, Tanach instead says *ayin*, eye, in the sense of appearance,
that something looks like a given colour.

In Aramaic the same root means immersing or soaking (Daniel 4:12, 4:20, 4:22,
4:30, 5:21; Targum to Vayikra 4:6, 11:25, 15:17). Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg: the
core is still dyeing, and since dyeing is done by soaking the material in a
solution, the root came to cover soaking of every kind. Menachem Ibn Saruk
(*Machberes Menachem*) and Rabbi Natan of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) already imply
this, listing both senses under one root without their usual separation.

Dr. Alexander Kohut (*Aruch HaShalem*) ties צ-ב-ע to ט-ב-ע, which
means sinking or drowning, and also stamping, and also nature, the link seemingly
resting on the interchangeability of tzadi and tes. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein supplies the
reasoning Kohut presumably had: dyeing immerses an object in liquid much as a sinking thing is
immersed; dyeing imprints a lasting color as stamping leaves a permanent mark;
and *teva*, nature, is what is ingrained in a thing, as a dyed fabric absorbs
its color until it is inseparable from it.

- On the deeper sense of the root, Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav
  VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 8:15, 31:18, Vayikra 4:25) takes it as gripping or
  holding fast: dyeing forges a bond that holds the color to the material, and
  color is the derived sense. That also explains *etzba*, finger, which is said
  to come from this root and whose aleph Radak holds is not part of it (whence
  *matzbia*, using one's finger to enter a raffle, *Mishnah Yoma* 2:1, later
  pointing, and in Modern Hebrew voting): the finger is what lets a man hold and
  control what he grasps, which is why the plagues are "the finger of Hashem"
  (Shemos 8:15) and the Luchos are written with His Finger (Shemos 31:18).
- Rabbi Shmuel Yehudah Steiger (*Avnei Shayish*) reads the two-letter string צ-ב
  throughout as binding and gathering: *tzava* (an army gathered), *tzover*
  (collecting), *tzibbur* (a public), and here the colorant bound to the cloth.

### ein (עין)
The construct of *ayin*, eye. In many cases it names the picture the eye holds,
and in some cases one aspect of it, the thing's color: the manna whose color was like the color of
*bedolach*, *eino k'ein habedolach* (Bamidbar 11:7), and the *tzaraas* spot that
"stands in its color" without lightening or darkening (Vayikra 13:5). Rambam,
who sometimes uses *tzeva* for colors and coloring, in other cases consistently
uses *ein*: the *galgalim* have no physical
color (*Yesodei HaTorah* 3:3), the garment whose color changes with *tzaraas*
(*Tumas Tzaraas* 16:10), and the abstraction one cannot sell, the taste of honey
or the color of a crystal (*Mechirah* 22:14). He uses both together for making
*ein hatzeva*, a colorant, on Shabbos (*Shabbos* 9:14).

### mareh (מראה)
From ר-א-ה, to see, so its plain sense is the content of what one sees: the
*mareh* of Hashem's glory that appeared as fire (Shemos 24:17, similarly
Bamidbar 9:15, Yechezkel 1:27, 8:2), and the *mareh* of a *tzaraas* spot,
as Vayikra 13 often calls it. Hence Malbim's distinction above: the whole appearance,
not the single color.

### gavan (גוון)
Aramaic, not in Tanach, and seemingly not in the Mishnah or Yerushalmi, but common in the
Bavli and Targum: the man selling a *tallis* asked what *gavan* it is (*Eruvin*
53b), and the egg yolk that gives mustard a stronger yellow, *l'gaven* (*Shabbos*
140a). Targum renders *tachash* as *sasgevana* (to Bamidbar 4:6), which Rashi
reads as *sas* (glad) with *gavan* (color), the creature rejoicing in its many
colors, and renders *chur* as *gavan chivar*, white color (Targum to Esther 1:6,
cited by Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur in *Sefer HaTishbi*). The one possible Mishnaic case
is *Niddah* 3:3, the sac full of water, blood, or *genunim*; the Gemara (*Niddah*
24b) concludes with Rabbi Ada bar Ahavah that it be read *gavanim*, tissue of
varied colors, as Rashi there defines it and as *Sefer HeAruch* seems to do as
well. In Modern Hebrew
*gavan* has narrowed to a shade within a color.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *tzeva* "dye" or "color" (as a dyer's word), *ein* "color," *mareh*
  "appearance," *gavan* "color" or "shade" in an Aramaic quotation.
- Trap: in Vayikra 13, *ein* and *mareh* stand within a few pesukim of each
  other. Rendering both "appearance," or both "color," destroys Malbim's
  distinction, on which the halachah of the *nega* turns. Give *ein* "color" and
  *mareh* "appearance" and keep them apart throughout the perek.
- Second trap: *tzeva* in a Shabbos context is the melachah of *tzovea*, dyeing;
  leave it transliterated where the melachah is named. *Tzavua* of a person is
  "two-faced," not "colorful" (Rashi to *Sotah* 22b). *Ein hatzeva* (Rambam,
  *Shabbos* 9:14) is a colorant, not "the color of the color."
