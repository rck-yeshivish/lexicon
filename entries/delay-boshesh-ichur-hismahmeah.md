# Delay: boshesh / ichur / hismahmeah

## Bottom line

*Ichur* is the neutral word: **being late**, simply past the appointed time.
*Boshesh* is **long overdue**, a delay so far past the hour that those waiting
give up, and the word carries the sting of *bushah*, shame. *Hismahmeah* is
**tarrying** or **dawdling**, going slower than one should, though Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim sees in it the one delay that is at times the right thing to do.
Shemos 32:1 says *boshesh* where the *Eitz Yosef* would expect *ichur*.

## Distinctions

### ichur (איחור)
From *achar*, after.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *ichur* is neutral, meaning only that a point in time
  has passed, with no verdict on whether the tardiness was excusable.
- Malbim: *ichur* is anything past a pre-set time.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (*HaRechasim LeVik'ah*): *ichur* is an
  acceptable lateness, what we would call fashionably late.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg quotes an elaborate discussion of these words from
the writings of R' Naftali Hertz (Wessely) Weisel, a figure often maligned as a
maskil but whose rabbinic bona fides are also attested to. Weisel: *ichur* is
being late willingly and deliberately,
while *boshesh* is being held up by forces beyond one's control. On that reading
Shemos 32:1 says
*boshesh* because the people assumed Moshe had been prevented from coming down,
by illness or death, which is why they were ready to accept another leader; in
reality his delay is better characterized as an *ichur*, since he was
deliberately staying to bask in Torah.

### boshesh (בושש)
- Rashi (to Shemos 32:1) glosses *boshesh* as an expression of *ichur* without
  saying what separates them.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter: *boshesh* is an excessive delay, hours
  upon hours.
- Malbim similarly, a lateness beyond the lateness.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *boshesh* is the delay one ought to be ashamed of,
  tying it directly to *bushah*.

The Midrash (*Bereishis Rabbah* 18:6) parses *boshesh* as *ba'u shesh sha'os*,
"six hours have come," the people reckoning that midday had passed and Moshe was
still not there.

- Rabbi Chanoch Zundel of Bialystok (*Eitz Yosef*) explains the derashah's
  warrant: the Torah's use of *boshesh* where *ichur* was expected screams for
  further interpretation, and the Midrash fills that lacuna.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter, on his own supposition that *boshesh*
  denotes excessive lateness, suggests that perhaps the appearance of this word
  is what led Chazal to assume the people thought Moshe was late by many hours.

Rabbi Avraham Saba (*Tzror HaMor*) reads *boshesh* against Aramaic *bshash*,
food without salt or seasoning, and hears in the word choice the Erev Rav's
argument that Moshe's teachings had no taste and could be discarded. His two
attestations: Targum renders the bland food of Iyov 6:6 as *bshash*, and
Berachos 40a describes a bread so good it "requires no *bshash*." The
Rishonim read the Gemara's *bshash* as delay, that is, tasty bread need not
wait for a relish; *Piskei HaRosh* and Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah prove it from
Shemos 32:1, while Rashi proves it from Shoftim 5:28, and *Maadanei Yom Tov*
asks why Rashi passed over the pasuk in Shemos.

### hismahmeah (התמהמה)
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter: a delay caused by moving slower than
  one should, dawdling where speed was called for. His cases are Lot dragging
  his feet leaving Sedom (Bereishis 19:16) and David holding back his flight
  from Avshalom to hear from Achimaatz (Shmuel II 15:28).
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim looks at it differently: *hismahmeah* is a delay that
  is positive and at times the right thing to do. He ties it to the biliteral
  מ-ה, *mah*, "what," an expression of uncertainty: one who is *hismahmeah* is
  standing about doing nothing in particular because he is still asking himself
  what he ought to do.

### boshesh and bushah
Five accounts of why the same root gives lateness and shame.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: it is the kind of delay to be ashamed of.
- Rav Hirsch: the root's core is disappointment, and shame is disappointment in
  oneself upon seeing one's own shortcoming, while *boshesh* is a delay so long
  that those waiting are disappointed in the man and give up on him.
- Rabbi David Golumb (*Targumna*): one who is shamed is so belittled and ashamed
  that nobody views him as important, and the upshot is that he is no longer
  deemed worth waiting for when he is delayed (*boshesh*).
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus: the core of ב-ש is something being delayed or withheld,
  whence both lateness and the shame that turns a man's face white, his blood
  withheld from travelling its usual way.
- Isaac Satanow, a Maskillic scholar, building on *Bava Metzia* 58b, that one
  publicly shamed first blushes and then turns white: *bushah* is related to the
  root ב-ש, which also means delaying, because shame first delays the regular
  circulation of the blood, which reddens the face. Rabbi Yehuda Leib
  Shapira-Frankfurter alludes to Satanow's explanation and rejects it.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 32:1: "long overdue," "so long in coming," or "held up." Not "was
  late" or "delayed," which are *ichur* and lose the very anomaly the Midrash
  and the Rishonim are reading.
- Trap: English "delay" absorbs all three. Where a passage sets them against
  each other, use "late" for *ichur*, "long overdue" for *boshesh*, and
  "tarried" or "lingered" for *hismahmeah*, and hold the assignment.
- Bereishis 19:16 and Shmuel II 15:28: "he tarried" or "he lingered." Do not
  use "delayed," which reads as an outside cause; the point is that the man
  himself was slow.
- Where a piece quotes Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's scheme, keep the three-way
  valence visible in the English: neutral, blameworthy, and commendable. Where
  it quotes Mecklenburg's citation of Wessely instead, the valences of *ichur*
  and *boshesh* swap; do not blend the two readings in one paragraph.
- *Bushah* is "shame," not "embarrassment"; see the *bushah*
  cluster entry.
