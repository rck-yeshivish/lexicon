# Nothing: hevel / rik / tohu / bohu (efes, blimah, meumah, klum)

## Bottom line

All of these are Hebrew words for "nothing," separated by nuances. *Hevel* and *rik*, which *Aleinu* pairs as what the other
nations serve, denote the impotence and powerlessness of their imagined deities.
Rabbi Avraham Bedersi makes *hevel* the less intense of the two, something that
does exist but has no purpose, and *rik* something that does not even exist. For
Shadal *hevel* is what appears valuable on the surface and is ultimately shown
to be worthless, a **futility**, and *rik* is toiling for a result that yields
nothing, labor **for naught**. Malbim takes the exact opposite approach: *hevel*
is total "nothingness," and *rik* is what has some value on the surface until
one digs deeper. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, tracing each to its two-letter root,
makes *rik* (ר-ק, emptying) what is completely lacking in import, as though
emptied of its very substance, and *hevel* (ב-ל, negation) what is futile,
somewhat substantial but still mostly useless. *Efes*, the "nothing" of *Aleinu*'s "nothing exists besides Him,"
means "nothing" or "non-existent" in Tanach and also "but, however"; Menachem
Ibn Saruk holds that sense distinct, while Radak implies that all its meanings
are interrelated. *Tohu* and *bohu*, both commonly translated "nothingness,"
describe the state of the world before the Six Days of Creation, and the
commentators read them in many ways, dividing among other things over which is
matter and which is form (Ramban: *tohu* matter, *bohu* form; Ralbag the exact
opposite). *Blimah*, *meumah* and *klum*, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds, are etymologically
related, all relating back to *mah*, "what." *Meumah* means "nothing" and can
also mean "anything," because Hebrew allows double negatives; *klum*, its
Rabbinic counterpart, also means "nothing."

## Distinctions

*Aleinu* contrasts the Jews, who worship the
"King, King of Kings, the Holy One, blessed be He," with the other nations, who
serve *hevel* and *rik*. Those two terms denote the impotence and powerlessness
of the imagined deities the gentiles worship, and amount to saying that those
nations worship "nothing." Later in the same prayer a third word for "nothing"
appears, *efes*. Though the Mishnah (*Chagigah* 2:1) writes that
asking what came before the Creation of the world and what will be after its
expiration is not a worthwhile course of study, the Torah itself (Bereishis
1:2) teaches that before the Six Days of Creation the world consisted of *tohu*
and *bohu*. Both words are commonly translated "nothingness"; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds
that understanding their nuances will help one better understand what the Torah
means to teach about the state of the world before Creation.

### hevel (הבל)
The 13th-century Provencal scholar Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*)
differentiates synonyms by the order in which they appear in Tanach when they
occur near each other. He postulates that when several terms for the same idea
appear in tandem, Tanach uses them in ascending order, from the least intense to
the most intense. The one time *hevel* and *rik* appear side by side (Yeshayahu
30:7; cf. 49:4) they come in precisely that order, *hevel* first, just as in
*Aleinu*. So *hevel* is the less intense "nothingness": something that does
exist (*yesh*) but has no purpose (*to'eles*), while *rik* is something that
does not even exist.

Shadal (Rabbi Shmuel Dovid Luzzatto) offers a comprehensive study of these
apparent synonyms. *Hevel* is specifically what appears on the surface to be
advantageous or otherwise valuable but will ultimately be shown to be worthless;
so beauty, which on the surface has some import, is *hevel* (Mishlei 31:30). In
line with this he explains the name Hevel that Adam gave his son (Bereishis
4:2): at first Adam thought Hevel had the potential to be one of the righteous
progenitors of mankind, but Hevel was killed before he could produce any
offspring, proving his futility. Expanding on this, Shadal surmises that Hevel
received the name only after he died, in retrospect of his unfruitful life,
which is why Tanach does not explicitly report that Chavah named him, as it
does for his brothers Kayin (4:1) and Sheis (4:25).

Malbim takes the exact opposite approach: *hevel* is that which is total
"nothingness" and has no absolute value, while *rik* is something that has some
value on the surface, but only when one digs deeper does one realize its folly.

Another approach to differentiating synonyms traces each word back to its
etymological forebear, and its champion, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau,
traces *hevel* and *rik* to their core two-letter roots. He lists *hevel* under
the biliteral root ב-ל, defined as "negation" and "loss of form," and derives a
whole family of words from it: *bli*, "without," a negation; *aval*, "but," the
negation of a previous clause; *bilui*, "worn out," by which something loses its
form; *teivel*, "the physical world," because all physical entities are subject
to wear and tear; *neveilah*, an animal carcass, a decomposing dead creature;
*neivel*, a leather flask made from the skin of a *neveilah*; *naval*, an
uncouth person, one who has lost his human form; and *yevul*, "produce,"
because it can easily rot and decompose if not properly preserved, or because
it comes from seeds that decompose to give way to what grows from them. Along
the same lines he explains *hevel* as something "futile," which has almost no
value and is thus negated: something somewhat substantial, but still mostly
useless and pointless. The 18th-century grammarian and *dayan* Rabbi Yehudah
Aryeh of Carpentras similarly writes in *Ohalei Yehudah* that *hevel* is a
portmanteau of *hei*, "this," and *balah*, "is worn out, useless, depleted."

In the Gemara *hevel* refers to highly intangible things, like vapors: the
*hevel*, the breathy utterances, of the mouths of children (Shabbos 119b); the
*hevel*, the bad air, inside a pit, by which it mainly causes damage, as opposed
to the blunt impact of falling into it (Bava Kamma 53a); and insulating foods in
certain materials that create heat, which are said to "add *hevel*" to them
(Shabbos 34a). As Radak explains in *Sefer HaShorashim*, verbal utterances are
*hevel* because they dissipate into the air very quickly and have no staying
power; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that the same is true of bad air and hot air.

Rabbi Avraham Dovid Kobernick compares ה-ב-ל to its metathesized counterparts
ב-ל-ה (*bilui*, "wearing out") and ל-ה-ב (*lahav*, "flame"), explaining that all
three are expressions of destruction and nothingness: *hevel* is something that
is right now "nothing," *bilui* something on its way to becoming "nothing," and
*lahav* that which consumes things and turns them into "nothing." He contrasts
these with their polar opposite, ל-ב (*lev*, "heart"), the mechanism that keeps
things alive and running.

Returning to the name Hevel, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds that Adam and Chavah's son was
likely given it because he lived only a short time before Kayin killed him, so
that his life was essentially useless and totally unimportant in the grand
scheme of things; and Christian writers such as Martin Luther and John Calvin
have variously translated the name as "vanity," "unprofitable," "useless,"
"worthless" or "a soon vanishing-vapor." The Ran (Rabbeinu Nissim of Gerona, to
Bereishis 4:1) and Ralbag (to Bereishis 4:2), however, speculate that perhaps he
was called Hevel because he was physically smaller than Kayin, so that his
existence was less substantial and closer to "nothing" than his brother's.
Alternatively, they suggest, perhaps Adam and Chavah prophesied that he was
destined to die young, and so named him Hevel to allude to his futile life.

### rik (ריק)
Shadal continues that *rik* refers specifically to toiling and working hard to
achieve something that instead yields "nothing," as in the curse the Torah
presents to those who fail to keep it, "and you will sow your seed for naught
(*larik*), as your enemies will eat it" (Vayikra 26:16). In many instances *rik*
appears alongside *yegiah*, toiling (Yeshayahu 49:4, 65:23; Iyov 39:16), and in
*Uva LeTzion* we ask Hashem to open our heart to His Torah and put love and fear
of Him in our hearts, "so that we do not toil for *rik*." For Bedersi *rik* is
the more intense word, what does not even exist (above).

For Malbim *rik* is what has some value on the surface (above), and he compares
it to the similar word *reik*, "empty," drawing an analogy to the shell or husk
of something that has no interior and is thus "empty." The shell itself may have
some value, even though the item as a whole is still "empty," and so essentially
amounts to "nothing."

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *rik* to the biliteral root ר-ק, "emptying" or
"depleting": *rik* means "nothing" or "vanity" in that it denotes something
completely lacking in import, as though emptied or depleted of its very
substance. The related *reikam*, "naught," with the added final mem, likewise
describes an act or utterance performed "for no reason." He lists many other
words from this root: *reik*, "empty," of something that is supposed to be full
but is now devoid of content; *rak*, "but, only," of what is exceptional or lies
outside the norm, as though emptied or separated from the rest; *merikah*,
"rinsing, washing," which empties something of all dirty particles; *tamrukin*,
"cosmetics," treatments that rinse the body of all impurities to leave it clean
and pristine; *marak*, "soup, broth," a liquid whose substance was distilled
from the essence of a solid food; *rok*, "spittle," a liquid excretion emptied
from the body; *rakos*, "slender, skinny," the body emptied of flesh and fat;
*rekes*, "temple," the area above the cheeks between the eyes and the side of
the head, typically described as not fleshy or fatty; *rakik*, "wafer," a thin,
brittle flatbread or cake not fried in oil, and so lean rather than fatty; and
more (*yerek*, *yarok*, *yerakrak*, *rikmah* and *rokem*).

Targum Onkelos renders *bohu* of Bereishis 1:2 as *reikanya*, an
Aramaicization of *reik*, a cognate of *rik*.

### hevel and rik in Aleinu
It may be because of the etymological association between *rik* and *rok*,
"spittle," Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests, that an old Ashkenazi custom developed of
spitting after saying *varik*, the word in *Aleinu* describing the object of
pagan worship; it is still practiced in some circles, especially among Chabad
Chassidim.

A medieval Ashkenazi tradition sees *hevel* and *rik* in *Aleinu* as allusions
to Jesus, because *hevel* in gematria equals *zol*, "cheap, disgraced," and
*varik* equals *Yeshu*, the rabbinic name for him (his Hebrew name was Yeshua).
Tanach itself already uses *hevel* of idolatry (Devarim
32:21; Melachim II 17:15), because that mode of worship is worthless and
produces nothing of significance. This wording in *Aleinu* made much trouble for
Jews in Christian Europe throughout history, and because of the damning
implications of this tradition, the line containing *hevel varik* has often been
excised by censors.

Although *Aleinu* as we have it reads "the seat of His honor (*yikaro*) is in
the Heavens above," some sources claim an old custom, found in *Seder Rav Amram
Gaon*, of refraining from saying *yikaro*, an inflection of the Aramaic *yakar*,
"honor," because it is an anagram of *varik*, and of saying its Hebrew synonym
*kevodo* instead.

### efes (אפס)
Later in *Aleinu*: "Our King is True, nothing (*efes*) exists besides Him." In
using *efes* to say that there is no other potent power in the world besides
Hashem, *Aleinu* mirrors a prophecy of Yeshayahu, who uses the word the same way
(Yeshayahu 45:14). According to Even-Shoshan's concordance, *efes* in Tanach
means "nothing" or "non-existent" (or the "edge" of existence) some thirty-six
times, and in another five a verb form, *afes*, denotes destroying or emptying
something in a way that renders it useless or impotent; so *efes* too is a term
of negation.

In seven more places *efes* means "but, however," broadly synonymous with
*aval*, *rak* and *ach*. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) sees this
meaning as distinct from the others, while Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) implies
that all these meanings are interrelated. On that view, as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein explains
it, *efes* is a conjunction that qualifies, minimizes or outright negates what
was said before, as though one recanted or partially recanted one's previous
statement, which is like turning that statement into "nothing," or at least
"emptying" it of its previous meaning. In Modern Hebrew
*efes* takes on a numeric sense, "zero."

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *efes* to the biliteral root פ-ס, taking the
initial alef as extraneous to the core root, and defines the root's principal
meaning as the "edge" or "end" of something: the *kesones passim* Yaakov gave
Yosef (Bereishis 37:3) is a pleated garment, which has many "edges" (*passim*).
*Efes*, then, is something that has reached its proverbial end and is therefore
non-existent, or close to it. He connects to the same root *pesiyah*,
"expansion," which moves the edges of something by spreading outward, and
*tefisah*, "holding, grabbing," a way of spreading one's domain by grabbing more
than before.

Rabbi Yitzchak Zeldin (eighteenth-century Germany) writes in *Shoresh Yesha*
that *efes* and its metathesized counterpart *osef*, "gathering," are both
related to the root ס-פ, *sof*, "end, edge": *osef* is a contraction of *ein
sof*, "there is no end," because it consciously tests the limits of the "end" by
gathering more, while *efes* denotes something whose beginning and end meet,
because it is non-existent or close to it.

### tohu (תהו)
*Tohu* occurs exactly twenty times in Tanach. Besides Bereishis 1:2, its only
other appearance in the Torah is Devarim 32:10, where, as Rashi explains it,
Moshe praises the Jews' devotion: their trust in Hashem can be found even in a
place of *tohu*, like the wilderness, into which they entered relying wholly on
Him. Eleven of the twenty are in Yeshayahu, who teaches that Hashem did not
create the world for *tohu* but so that it will be populated (45:18), and who
uses *tohu* to describe idolatry as "nothing, worthless, impotent" (41:29, 44:9,
59:4).

Targum pseudo-Yonasan and Targum Neofiti (to Bereishis 1:2) render *tohu* and
*bohu* as *tahiya* and *bahiya*, simply Aramaicizations of the Hebrew. Rashi
(there) and *Sefer HaBahir* (§2) explain *tohu* as astonishment and wonderment:
one who saw the sheer nothingness of the pre-Creation world would wonder and be
astonished. The same usage is found in *toheh al harishonos* (Kiddushin 40b), of
one who regretted his good deeds and was "astonished" at what he had nobly done,
and in *tahi bah*, the Talmud's phrase introducing a question of bewilderment
(Berachos 38b, Eruvin 66a, Kesubos 107b, Kiddushin 55b, Bava Kamma 76b and 112b,
Bava Basra 39b, Zevachim 13b, Bechoros 42b).

The Gemara (Yoma 54b) records a dispute over whether Hashem created the world
starting from the middle and expanding outward, or from the edges, filling in
until He reached the middle. The 14th-century Provencal scholar Rabbi Nissim of
Marseilles (to Bereishis 1:2) seemingly follows the first approach: both words
allude to the moment before Creation, when the entire world was concentrated in
the middle, from which everything was to expand outward (Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
compares how scientists nowadays understand the Big Bang; Rabbi Alexander Hool
argues, in *The Six Days of Creation*, that whereas modern science believes the universe is still expanding,
Judaism understands that it has actually stopped expanding). Rabbi Nissim likens *tohu* to
a person stuck "wondering" (*toheh*/*tamah*), who stays immobile in place,
moving neither right nor left because of his indecision: *tohu* is that moment
before Hashem catalyzed the world to expand outward.

Ramban (to Bereishis 1:1) famously takes a more philosophical approach: *tohu*
and *bohu* are the notions of matter (*chomer*) and form (*tzurah*). *Tohu* is
what the Greek natural philosophers called hyle (*hiyuli*), a primordial first
matter that was shaped and formed to fashion everything in Creation. A name can
apply only to something that has both matter and form, and anything missing
either one is effectively "nothing." So Ramban, like Rashi, connects *tohu* to
*toheh*: since *tohu* is matter without form, it eludes naming, and one who
encountered it could only wonder what it should be called. His explanation
echoes the *Zohar* (Bereishis 16a) and his predecessor Rabbi Avraham bar Chiyya
HaNasi, who wrote the same thing in *Higayon HaNefesh*. Ralbag (to Bereishis
1:2) takes the exact opposite position, *tohu* as form and *bohu* as matter, and
Abarbanel (there) rejects Ralbag's explanation.

Rabbi Moshe Botarel cites an otherwise unknown scholar, Rabbi Levi ben Shlomo of
Lunel, as likewise explaining *tohu* as matter that has no shape but is destined
to receive one later, and *bohu* as the concept of shape. Rabbi Levi is also
cited as explaining that existence hinges on polar opposites, so that *tohu* is
"absence" and *bohu* "presence"; somehow Hashem's tempering the balance between
these two poles brings the world into existence. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds
that the world is a place where opposites, concrete and abstract, are expected to
coexist, and Hashem is the One who makes peace between them; he also cautions
that Botarel has been known to fabricate sources, so his citation might not be the
most reliable.

Shadal: *tohu* is something pointless that brings no benefit (for his *bohu*,
see below).

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim sees the hei and vav of *tohu* and *bohu* as letters
added to the core root. *Tohu* derives from the monoliteral tav and denotes an
outline or silhouette that shapes something's outer contours but whose core
inner essence has not yet been filled in. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein notes that
this fits an esoteric passage in the Gemara (Chagigah 12a) that explains, on Yeshayahu 34:11,
*tohu* as "a green line that circumscribes the entire world" (something that
establishes the boundaries of the physical universe) and *bohu* as "the wet
stones embedded in the mantle from which water goes out" (the external source of
water, the essential ingredient of all life).

As an aside, Rabbi Avraham bar Chiyya HaNasi writes that *tehom*, "depths of the
sea," whose inflections appear thirty-six times in Tanach, derives from *tohu*,
because it denotes an unordered area that has no shape. The final mem of
*tehom*, he explains, is like the final mem of *chinam*, "free," and *reikam*,
"empty," which are declensions of *chein*, "granting, gracious," and *reik*,
"vacant, empty."

### bohu (בהו)
*Bohu* appears only twice more in Tanach beyond Bereishis 1:2 (Yeshayahu 34:11;
Yirmiyahu 4:23).
- Targum Onkelos renders it *reikanya*, and Rashi likewise relates it to *reik*,
  in the sense of "emptiness".
- *Sefer HaBahir* (§2) explains *bohu* as something that has some minimal amount
  of tangibility and is not completely abstract.
- Rabbi Nissim of Marseilles reads it as a contraction of *bo*, "inside," and
  *hu*, "it is": the entire world was still "in the inside," at the middle, and
  had not yet begun to spread out.
- For Ramban *bohu* is form, for Ralbag matter, and for Rabbi Levi ben Shlomo of
  Lunel shape and "presence" (all above).

Shadal: *bohu* is something that not only has no advantage but is actually bad.
Elsewhere *Sefer HaBahir* (§11-12) connects *tohu* with evil and *bohu* with
peace, and later (§135, §163) elaborates by connecting *tohu*'s association with
evil to its causing people to wonder and second-guess themselves, which leads
them to sin.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the root of *bohu* to be the monoliteral beis,
"inside": *bohu* is something's external structure whose interior still needs
to be filled in (for the Gemara's *bohu*, Chagigah 12a, see above). Rabbi Reuven Chaim
Klein adds a point Rabbi Pappenheim does not make: the name of the letter beis itself
relates to *bayis*, "house," a place into which a person might enter.

### blimah (בלימה)
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein holds that *blimah* and *klum*, like *meumah*, relate
back to *mah*. *Blimah*
appears only once in Tanach: Iyov describes Hashem as the One who "hangs the
land on *blimah*" (Iyov 26:7). On a simple level this means that Hashem actively
suspends the earth in space ("nothing") without its needing to lean on any other
cosmic body. Following this understanding, Rabbi Saadia Gaon, Ibn Ezra, Rabbi
Moshe Kimchi (to Iyov there) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) all explain
*blimah* as a portmanteau of *bli*, "without, there is not," and *mah*,
"anything," literally "what." *Bli* also relates to the
concept of "nothingness" (Rabbi Pappenheim's ב-ל family,
under *hevel* above), and *blimah* is sometimes written as two words.
In this spirit Rabbi Avraham bar Chiyya HaNasi (*Higayon HaNefesh*) writes that
*blimah* is synonymous with *tohu*.

On an exegetical plane, Chazal (Chullin 89a) explain *blimah* as a single word
from the root ב-ל-ם, "withhold, hold back," a cognate of which appears only once
in Tanach (Tehillim 32:9), so that the word is otherwise a hapax legomenon. When
Iyov says that Hashem hangs the world on *blimah*, the world's continued
existence hinges on the merit of those who respond to personal insults by
"holding back" and not retorting (see Rabbeinu Bachya to Devarim 33:27).

*Sefer Yetzirah* calls the ten *sefiros* *blimah*, meaning either that the
*sefiros* are not independent realities existing outside Hashem but are
"nothing" on their own, or that one ought to "hold back" from speaking of
esoteric matters and sharing them with the uninitiated (see Ramban to *Sefer
Yetzirah*). Rabbi Shabsai Donnolo, in his commentary to *Sefer Yetzirah*,
explains *blimah* as a portmanteau of *bli* and *meumah*: unlike other artisans
and craftspeople, Hashem was able to create the world without any tools or
materials.

Biblical *blimah* has nothing to do with the Yiddish girls'
name Blima, also pronounced Blume (responsa *Maharsham* vol. 4 §50, vol. 8
§242), which derives from Germanic cognates of the English *bloom*, *blossom* and
*blow* (and the German and Yiddish *blatt*). According to linguists, those
Germanic words derive from the Proto-Indo-European root *bhel*, "thriving,
blossoming." In Greek and Latin the first consonant of that root became an
f-sound (just as beis and pei can interchange in Hebrew), yielding the
etymological ancestors of the English *flower*, *flour*, *foliage*, *folio*,
*flora*, *flourish* and more; it is also the basis of the name of the Roman
goddess Flora (Fluusai in the ancient Oscan culture). None of this has anything
to do with the British English interjection *blimey*.

### meumah (מאומה) and klum (כלום)
*Meumah* is a tricky term: it means "nothing," but it can also mean "anything,
something, whatever, whatsoever." The same word bears these seemingly opposite
meanings because Hebrew allows double negatives: in Hebrew a person can say he
has nothing by saying "I do not have nothing," while in English saying the same
thing is an awkward way of implying that one does have something. According to
Even-Shoshan's concordance *meumah* appears thirty-two times in Tanach, plus
twice more without the final hei as *meum* (Iyov 31:7, Daniel 1:4).

The most popular etymology traces *meumah* to the biliteral root מ-ה, whence
*mah*, "what." Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras adopts it in *Ohalei Yehudah*,
where *meumah* is a contraction of *mah u'mah*, "what and what." The German
philologist Wilhelm Gesenius also offers this etymology in his lexicon of
Biblical Hebrew and Aramaic, and compares its construction to that of the Latin
*quidquid*, which means basically the same thing. Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein cites
this explanation but rejects it on technical grounds. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
traces *meumah* to the two-letter root מ-מ, which denotes "the smallest amount,"
so that *meumah* meaning "nothing" really means "not even something" (for
example, Bereishis 30:31, 39:23; Melachim I 10:21). For him Biblical *meumah* is
on par with the Rabbinic *mah shehu*, from *mah*, "what," and *shehu*, "that it
is," meaning "whatever amount that it is," and *kol shehu*, from *kol*, "all,
any," and *shehu*, meaning "any amount that it is."

*Klum*, a Rabbinic word, appears countless times in the Mishnah and also means
"nothing"; like its Biblical counterpart *meumah*, it usually appears in the
context of a double negative. Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Musaf He'Aruch*) writes
that *klum* is a contraction of *kol meum*, "nothing at all," a phrase in the
Hebrew part of Daniel (1:4), and Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein cites this etymology
approvingly. Rabbi Yair Chaim Bachrach, author of the responsa *Chavos Yair*,
writes in *Mar Kashisha* that he disagrees with Rabbi Mussafia's explanation but
does not offer an alternative etymology. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that
before he studied the etymologies of *klum* he used to think it related to
*kol*, "all, every," and *kulam*, "all of them," through the well-documented
phenomenon that a Hebrew root can sometimes refer to an idea and its polar
opposite, as the noun *shoresh* means "root" and its verb form *misharesh* "to
uproot"; so it made sense to him that "everything" and "nothing" would be
related words.

## Rendering guidance

- הבל: "futility," or "vanity" in Koheles where the register is fixed. Not
  "nothing," which loses Bedersi's point that the thing does exist.
- לריק / ריק of effort: "for naught," "in vain." ריק of a vessel or a place:
  "empty."
- תהו ובהו: "formless and void" is the safe pair. If the passage follows Ramban,
  say so in a note ("unformed matter and form") rather than forcing it into the
  translation itself, since Ralbag holds the reverse.
- Trap: English "nothing" will swallow *hevel*, *rik*, *efes*, *tohu* and *klum*
  at once. Where two of them stand in one phrase (*hevel varik* in *Aleinu*),
  render them apart: "futility and emptiness," in that order, since the order is
  Bedersi's whole evidence.
- Second trap, grammatical: לא ... מאומה and לא ... כלום are Hebrew double
  negatives that carry a single negative sense. Render "he did not have anything"
  or "he had nothing," never "he did not have nothing."
- Where a source darshens *blimah* as holding back (Chullin 89a), do not
  translate it "nothingness" in that line; the derashah needs "restraint."
