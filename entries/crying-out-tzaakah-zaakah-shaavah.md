# Crying out: tzaakah / zaakah / shaavah

## Bottom line

*Tzaakah* and *zaakah* both mean **to cry out**, and Rashi, Ramban, the Zohar,
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and others treat them as one word with two
interchangeable opening letters. *Shaavah* is not the same: it is **a cry for help**, and Rabbi Yehuda
Leib Shapira-Frankfurter holds it is addressed only to Hashem. In *tzaakah*
and *zaakah* the sufferer voices his pain, while in
*shaavah* he asks outright to be saved (Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg). Among those who
do separate *tzaakah* from *zaakah*, Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer and Malbim
make *tzaakah* the graver of the two.

## Distinctions

### tzaakah (צעקה) and zaakah (זעקה): one word
Rashi (to Iyov 6:17 and Sotah 36b), Ramban (to Iyov 40:17), the Zohar (Shemos
20a) and others hold the two wholly synonymous and interchangeable, since
*zayin* and *tzadi* yield similar phonemes, both articulated at the teeth, and
so substitute for one another. Rabbi Pappenheim likewise writes that there is no
difference; he notes that *tzaakah* stands close to *tzaar*, pain, but concludes
that both words denote an outward expression of a man's pain and suffering. This
reading is what lets the Midrash tie Esav's great and bitter cry when the
berachos were gone (Bereishis 27:34) to Mordechai's cry in Shushan (Esther 4:1):
because Yaakov caused Esav to cry out, Yaakov's descendants were later punished
with the threat of annihilation at the hand of Haman, a descendant of Esav, and
Mordechai had in his turn to cry out to Hashem. On the synonymous reading the
Midrash is entirely justified, since Tanach practically uses the same word for
both, even though the Torah writes *tzaakah* there and Megillas Esther *zaakah*
here.

### tzaakah and zaakah: those who separate them
Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms, the late twelfth century Ashkenazic scholar,
holds they are not synonyms: *zaakah* is a cry
that lodges a complaint, and *tzaakah* is not; and *zaakah* implies multiple
people crying, *tzaakah* does not. On his reading the Midrash's link between Esav
and Mordechai is somewhat difficult to justify, since Tanach uses two different
words for the two occurrences.

Tehillim 107 is the test case, since all four of its rescued sufferers cry out:
the traveler lost in the wilderness and the sailor in the storm with *tzaakah*,
the prisoner and the deathly ill man with *zaakah*.

- Rabbi Emmanuel Chai Ricci: the *tz* is simply the longer sound. The prisoner
  and the sick man are pinned in place, so they get the shorter word; the other
  two, who still have somewhere to turn, get the longer.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: the Midrash understands the four as the four
  kingdoms that would subjugate Yisrael, Bavel, Persia/Media, Greece and Rome,
  and asserts that the first and last of them, Bavel and Rome, were more
  detrimental to Yisrael than the middle two (Rabbi Klein adding, as his own
  guess, that this is probably because both destroyed the Beis HaMikdash and
  exiled the Jews from Yerushalayim). From this Rabbi Wertheimer deduces that
  *tzaakah* denotes the more intense crying out, indicating a more serious
  distress.
- Malbim (to Tehillim 107:6) says the same directly: the wayfarer and the sailor
  are in greater danger than the prisoner and the sick man, so the pasuk gives
  them *tzaakah*. Tosafos (to Berachos 54b) read the four as listed in order of
  danger instead, which cuts against that.

### shaavah (שועה)
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter, Rav Hirsch's great-uncle: *zaakah* and
  *tzaakah* may be directed at a fellow man, but *shaavah* applies only to one
  who pleads with Hashem.
- Rabbi Pappenheim ties it to *yeshuah*, on the shared root ש-ו-ע: it is a call
  made in the expectation that it will bring the caller's rescue.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg accepts that all three are cries made in hope of
  deliverance and puts the difference in what is actually said: with *zaakah*
  and *tzaakah* the sufferer puts his suffering into words but does not ask (a
  poor man calling out that he has not eaten in days), while with *shaavah* he
  says plainly what he wants (the same man saying, give me something to eat).
- The Zohar agrees that *tzaakah* and *zaakah* are the less explicit, but frames
  it differently: they are the silent cry of pain and *shaavah* is spoken
  tefillah, and one view there makes the wordless cry of the heart the greater
  plea of the two.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 14:13) suggests that *shaavah* is the act of yearning
  for a *teshuah*: unlike other words for crying out, which might simply voice
  one's suffering, *shaavah* is an unmistakable cry for salvation, a sort of
  prayer as opposed to a complaint.

## Rendering guidance

- *Tzaakah* and *zaakah*: "cry out," "cried out," "an outcry." Use the same
  English for both unless the passage is arguing from the difference.
- *Shaavah*: "cried out for help," or "plea." "Cry" alone loses the request,
  which is the whole of Rabbi Mecklenburg's distinction.
- Tehillim 107, where all four cries appear: keep one English verb across the
  four unless you are following Malbim or Rabbi Wertheimer, in which case mark
  the two *tzaakah* verses ("cried out in anguish") and leave the *zaakah* verses
  plain, with a note saying what you did and why.
- Trap: English "cry out" will absorb all three, and "cry" alone will also pull in
  weeping, which none of these words means. None of the three is *bechi*.
- Second trap: when a Midrash links two pesukim across the *tzaakah*/*zaakah*
  line (Esav and Mordechai), an English translation that renders them identically
  makes the derashah look trivial, and one that renders them differently makes it
  look impossible. Translate both "cried out" and put the two Hebrew words in a
  note.
