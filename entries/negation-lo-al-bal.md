# Negation: lo / al / bal

## Bottom line

All three negate. *Lo* is the Torah's word for a standing prohibition. Rabbi Ber
Oppenheimer explains that it also serves for the plain factual "did not," while
*al* is a negative imperative aimed at a future act, "do not." *Bal* is the word
Chazal use to *name* a prohibition that the Torah already issued with *lo*. So
*lo sashchis* in a pasuk is **"you shall not destroy"**, while *bal tashchis* in
a sugya is **"the prohibition of bal tashchis"**, not a fresh command. *Bal* is
not a Rabbinic coinage: it appears close to seventy times in Tanach, though
never in Chumash.

## Distinctions

### lo (לא)
The Torah's standard negation, and the marker of a negative commandment: *lo
sirtzach*, *lo sinaf*, *lo signov*, *lo saaneh*, *lo sachmod*.

- Rabbi Ber Oppenheimer: *lo* negates both past fact and future command, so it
  covers "you shall not eat" and "you did not eat" alike.
- Rabbi Aharon Maggid, as Rabbi Klein reads him ("if I understood him
  properly"): *lo* is addressed concretely to a second person, "*you* shall
  not."

### al (אל)
Rabbi Ber Oppenheimer: *al* is always a negative imperative, always about
something one might yet do, and never about a past act (he allows one
exception, Shmuel I 27:10). It is "do not," never "did not."

### bal (בל)
Rashi (to Yeshayahu 26:10) states plainly that the *bal* of Tanach means what
*lo* means, and *Machberes Menachem* places it in the first category of words
from the two-letter root ב-ל. But in Tanach it is confined to Yeshayahu,
Tehillim, Mishlei and Hoshea (with single instances at Iyov 41:15 and Divrei
HaYamim I 16:30), and in most of its appearances there it states a fact rather than issuing
a command: the world "shall not (*bal*) falter" (Tehillim 93:1, 96:10), the
tzaddik "shall not (*bal*) be moved" (Mishlei 10:30, 12:3). Chazal turn it into
the standard way of naming a prohibition: *lo sashchis* (Devarim 20:19) becomes
*bal tashchis* (Bikkurim 4:2, Kiddushin 1:7), *lo sosif* (Devarim 13:1) becomes
*bal tosif* (Zevachim 8:10), *lo seshaktzu* (Vayikra 20:25) becomes *bal
teshaktzu* (Shabbos 90b, Makkos 16b, Meilah 17a), *lo yeira'eh* (Shemos 13:7)
and *lo yimatzei* (Shemos 12:19) become *bal yeira'eh* and *bal yimatzei*
(Pesachim 3:3, 9:3). Chazal, possibly because of the word *bal*, even read a factual
passage prescriptively: Tehillim 147:19-20 records that Hashem did not (*bal*) make His laws known to the
nations, and Chagigah 13a derives from it that we do not transmit Torah to a
gentile.

### Why Chazal traded lo for bal
Over the generations several answers have been proposed, often arcane and little
discussed. Rabbi Yishaya Pick-Berlin raised the question in the introduction to
his unpublished glosses to *Sefer HaTishbi*; Rabbi Chizkiyah Medini of Chevron
was asked it by a talmid, agreed it was worth asking, and knew of no earlier
source until he later saw Rabbi Pick.
- Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi (*Shitah Mekubetzes* to Temurah 6b) cites Rabbi Meir
  HaMeili (*Sefer HaMeoros*), who says only, and cryptically, that *bal* is
  apparently somehow easier to pronounce and conveys a clearer meaning. Rabbi
  Klein suggests that this last point may be an allusion to the answer of Rabbi
  Reuven Margolios: to keep the negative *lo* from being heard as the homonym
  *lo*, "to him."
- Rabbi Ber Oppenheimer: *bal* states an ongoing fact rather than a command, so
  *bal tigzol* points at the man who stole and still holds the goods, and so
  with the other *bal* prohibitions, which point at a man who has not yet done
  teshuvah.
- Rabbi Aharon Maggid, again as Rabbi Klein reads him: *bal* names the
  prohibition in the abstract, without fixing on whom it binds.
- Rabbi Eliyahu Gutmacher, who expressly admits his suggestion is entirely novel
  and says he would have to run it through the whole Talmud to see whether it
  holds up, takes *bal* to mark the Rabbinic extension beyond the Biblical core
  (*bal tashchis* reaching every non-constructive destruction of property, where
  the pasuk speaks of a fruit tree); Rabbi Klein does not think that it pans
  out.
- Rabbi Avraham Eliezer Hirshawitz (*Otzar Kol Minhagei Yeshurun*), similarly:
  *lo* is an absolute, unconditional no, *bal* a general and more flexible one,
  fitting Chazal's work of defining when a prohibition applies and when it does
  not.
- Some scholars have suggested that *bal* is not a Chazalic expression at all
  but a later copyists' abbreviation of *b'lo*, a theory that has not gained
  much traction.
- Rabbi Yisroel Rubin (Chabad of Albany) proposes, but ultimately rejects, that
  Chazal changed the wording to avoid quoting pesukim by heart (Gittin 60b);
  Rabbi Eli Silberstein (Chabad of Ithaca), Rabbi Rubin relates, suggested that
  *bal* is short for *bli*/*bilti*, and Rabbi Rubin himself preferred to relate
  it to *bilui*, wearing out.
- Rabbi Klein's own suggestion, which he calls his pet theory and puts as "we
  may argue," rests on the Chasam Sofer (*Toras Moshe* to Vayikra 10:1), that
  *lo* is one of the names of the Angel of Death, hinted at in the *eish zarah
  asher lo tzivah osam*: Chazal avoided the word so as not to invoke that name,
  which might draw accusation against Klal Yisrael, who may sometimes have
  failed to keep those very commandments.

### The root ב-ל
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives the two-letter root ב-ל two core senses,
negation and loss of form: *bli*, *hevel*, *aval*, *bilui*, *neveilah*,
*neivel*, *naval*, *teiveil*, *yevul*. A second family from the same root turns
on mixing: *balal*, *tavlul*, *tevel*, *tavlin*, *aveil*, *behalah*, *yovel*,
*mabul*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Lo* + imperfect in a pasuk: **you shall not**. *Al* + jussive: **do not**.
  Keeping the two registers apart matters where a pasuk has both.
- *Bal* in Tanach: **shall not**, factual, not prohibitive. Tehillim 93:1 is
  "it shall not falter," not "let it not falter."
- *Bal* in Chazal: leave it transliterated and name it as a prohibition, **the
  issur of bal tashchis**, **bal yeira'eh uval yimatzei**. Do not render it as
  a command in English; that turns a citation into a fresh instruction.
- Trap: English "not" flattens all three, and "do not destroy" will do for both
  *lo sashchis* and *bal tashchis*, which is exactly the distinction Chazal
  built by changing the word. When a passage quotes the pasuk and then the
  Rabbinic label in one breath, translate the first and transliterate the
  second.
- Second trap: *lo* in a pasuk may be the prepositional *lo*, "to him." Rabbi
  Margolios's answer to the whole question turns on that ambiguity, so read the
  vowels before rendering.
