# Pole: mot / bad / neis / toren / shapud

## Bottom line

*Mot* is a thick carrying pole borne on the shoulders, and in the feminine
*motah* the bar of a yoke: **carrying pole, yoke-bar**. *Bad* is the same object
in the Mishkan vessels, a stave slid through rings: **stave**. *Neis* is a tall
upright pole raised to be seen, hence **banner, standard**, and on a ship the
**sail**. *Toren* is the **mast** that holds the *neis* up. *Shapud* is a
pointed **skewer** or spit. The trap: "pole" will absorb all five. Reserve
"stave" for the Mishkan *badim* (never "poles of the Ark" where a derashah
turns on *bad*, "alone"), and never render *neis* as "pole" where it carries a
banner or where the pasuk plays on *neis*, "miracle."

## Distinctions

### mot (מוט) and zemorah (זמורה)
Four occurrences: the spies' pole (Bamidbar 13:23), the poles for carrying the
Mishkan vessels (Bamidbar 4:10, 4:12), and Nachum 1:13. The feminine *motah*
appears a further twelve times, usually the bar of a yoke (Vayikra 26:13,
Yeshayahu 58:6, Yechezkel 30:18). On the spies' pasuk, "they cut a branch
(*zemorah*) and a single cluster, and carried it on the pole (*mot*) by twos,"
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah*) reads two objects, branch
and pole. The Zohar (*Shelach* 160b) reads one: the definite article on *mot*
points back to something already named, and the same piece of wood is called
*zemorah* while attached to the tree and *mot* once cut.

Roots: the triliteralists Ibn Janach and Radak, each in his *Sefer
HaShorashim*, מ-ו-ט. The medieval biliteralist Menachem Ibn Saruk
(*Machberes Menachem*), the two-letter מ-ט, which also yields *tamut*
(slipping), *mateh* (staff), *matteh* (tribe), *mitah* (bed) and Aramaic *mata*
(touching), so that for him the middle vav is extraneous. The Enlightenment-era
biliteralist Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes even the *mem* as extraneous and
works from ט-ה, whose core sense is changing the present
situation by inclining or turning: *natah*, *lematah*, *matah*, *mitah* (one
inclines to lie down), *mateh* (leaned on while walking). In *Cheishek Shlomo*
he offers two readings of *mot* and says the pesukim cannot decide between
them: either a thick pole from the lower part of a tree, hence *lematah*, or a
branch that turns out sideways from the trunk, hence *natah*. In *Yerios
Shlomo* he simply calls a *mot* a reinforced *mateh*.

### bad (בד)
Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (*Ohel Moed*) makes *mot* and *bad* synonyms. *Badim* are
the staves of the Shulchan (Shemos 25:28, 37:14), the golden Mizbeiach (Shemos
30:4, 37:27), the copper Mizbeiach (Shemos 27:7, 38:7) and the Aron (Shemos
25:13-15, 37:5). The root ב-ד-(ד) also yields *levad*, alone, and *boded*,
singular, and *hisbodedus*. Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) connects them two ways:
once a branch of the right quality is chosen for a stave it is the one branch
out of all the others, singular among its peers; or a *bad* is the thick branch
that comes off the tree right at the root and so looks like a tree standing
alone. That lower branch is thick and hard, which is why it serves for carrying
loads, for bars, and for the beam of a scale, and why an olive press is a *beis
habad* (*Shabbos* 1:9, *Nedarim* 3:2, *Bava Metzia* 10:4, *Bava Basra* 1:6 and
4:4-5, *Shevuos* 3:8, *Keilim* 12:3 and 20:3, *Taharos* 10:1-2 and 10:8,
*Zavim* 4:7).

### neis (נס)
A tall pole or flagpole, appearing about twenty times in Tanach on Even
Shoshan's concordance. The copper snake was set on a *neis* so that the bitten
could look at it and live (Bamidbar 21:8-9; the article prints 21:89), and we
daven to "raise a *neis* to gather in our exiles." Pappenheim defines it as a
tall *mot* and does not trouble himself over the near synonymy. Roots:
*Machberes Menachem*, נ-ס, which also yields fleeing and miracle; Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*), נ-ס-ס, and he links flagpole to fleeing because in ancient battle
when the flagbearer fell the whole army lost heart and fled. The nearby
triliteral נ-ס-ה is testing or training, as with Avraham (Bereishis 22:1);
*Machberes Menachem* takes even that word back to a one-letter root, samech.
Pappenheim (*Cheishek
Shlomo*) traces *neis* instead to ש-א, whose main derivative is *nasa*, to lift
and carry, with *sin* and *samech* interchanging: a *neis* as miracle is an
event lifted out of the ordinary order of nature, and a *neis* as pole is the
branch lifted above the rest of the tree. Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to
Bereishis 22:1): a *nisayon* from the All-Knowing cannot be for the tester to
learn something, so it is for the tested to reach new levels by prevailing
through the travails of a Divine test, another kind of lifting. Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 17:20 and 22:1) joins *neis* to *nasi*, the leader whose task is to
raise those under him, and, like Pappenheim, reads *neis* as lifting in both
senses at once, a man's rise in spiritual rank when he passes a test and a tall
pole for carrying or raising a banner; elsewhere (to Tehillim 4:7) he reads the
flagstaff as a sign that keeps those who see it faithful to their cause, which
is also what a miracle does. Midrash *Tanchuma* (*Vayeira* 43) links the roots
thematically: the merit of the *nisayon* of the Akeidah is what lets Israel
escape (*manos*) Gehinnom; the *Mechilta* to Shemos 15:25 reads "there He tested
them" at Marah as Hashem lifting them to greatness, which He did by revealing
parts of the Torah to them before Sinai.

### toren (תורן)
The ship's mast, paired with *neis* as its sail (Yeshayahu 30:17 and 33:23,
Yechezkel 27:5), and again in the Mishnah's ruling that a sold boat includes its
*toren* and its *neis* (*Bava Basra* 5:1, with Rambam's commentary there and
Rashi to *Bava Basra* 73a). Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak give the
triliteral ת-ר-ן, which yields nothing else. Pappenheim works from ת-ר,
furling and unfurling, whose derivative *meisar*, rope, gives him the mast,
since the mast is held fast by ropes.

### shapud (שפוד)
Mishnaic only: a wooden pole with a sharpened end, a spit or skewer (*Pesachim*
7:1-2, *Sukkah* 1:8, *Bava Kamma* 8:1, *Avodah Zarah* 5:12, *Zevachim* 11:7,
*Keilim* 5:5 and 7:3, *Ohalos* 1:3). Vocalization is unsettled: the Kaufmann
manuscript, the earliest vowelized Mishnah, reads *shfud* in most places,
*shfod* at *Keilim* 5:5 and *shifud* at *Keilim* 7:3, and *shpud*, *shafud* and
*shafod* are all current as well. Modern Hebrew says *shipud*, and that
pronunciation was given official recognition by the Academy of the Hebrew
Language in 2019, as reported by Doron Yaakov (*Academ* vol. 40 pp. 2-3). Rabbi
Ernest Klein (no relation to the columnist) argues that *shapud* is ultimately a
loanword from the Greek *spodos*, charcoal, embers or ashes, which came also to
mean a spit for roasting meat, as the Italian *spiedo* does. The column adds an
etymology of its own, flagging that it usually shies away from speculative ones:
English *spit* comes from German *spitz*, point or sharp end, from the
Proto-Indo-European root *spei-* or *spi-*, something pointy, which also gives
*spike* and *spoke*, and it seems fairly clear to the column that *spit* and its
cognates go back to the same Greek word as *shapud*, on the interchange of the
*t* and *d* sounds in Germanic under Grimm's Law, the Danish *spid* for English
*spit* showing that dynamic.

Rabbi Tzvi Matisyahu Abrahams (*Root Connections in the Torah*, 120-125) draws
the senses of *neis* together homiletically: the *nisyonos* are the wind in the
sail without which the boat, to which life is compared, does not move; only a
*neis*, Divine help rather than human intuition, carries a person through a real
test, and Nissan is named as the month of the greatest miracles in Tanach, the
birth of Yitzchak, the Exodus, the Splitting of the Sea, Haman's downfall; a
banner proclaims its message as a miracle proclaims Hashem's hand; and *nisah*,
fleeing, is sometimes the right answer to a test, as Yosef fled (*vayanas*)
rather than risk the sin (Bereishis 39:12). For that last link he leans on
Rashi (to Yeshayahu 30:17), who explains the *neis* as a military signal to the
troops either to rally or to flee.

## Rendering guidance

- Bamidbar 13:23: "on the pole" if you follow Mecklenburg, "on the pole
  [that same branch]" if you follow the Zohar. Do not write "on a pole"; the
  definite article is what the Zohar's reading rests on.
- Trap: "pole" absorbs *mot*, *bad*, *neis* and *toren*. Where two stand in one
  pasuk (*neis* and *toren* in Yeshayahu 33:23), you must differentiate: "mast"
  and "sail," or "mast" and "banner," never "pole" twice.
- Second trap: *neis* is "miracle" in later Hebrew and "banner" in Tanach. In
  liturgical prose ("raise a *neis*") keep "banner"; a reader who hears
  "miracle" loses the image of the ingathering signal.
- *Badim* in the Mishkan are "staves," not "poles" and never "rods"; the Torah
  is careful that they are not removed (Shemos 25:15), and the halachic term of
  art in English is settled.
- *Motah* is "yoke-bar" or, in the idiom of Vayikra 26:13, "the bars of your
  yoke." Do not level it to "yoke," which is *ol*.
- *Shapud*: "skewer" for food, "spit" for roasting; both are fine, but pick one
  per passage, since a mishnah often carries the same *shapud* across clauses.
