# Species: min / zan / sug

## Bottom line

*Min* is the Torah's word: **kind** or **species**, a grouping of individuals
alike enough in their properties to be classed as one kind (Ibn Janach, Radak).
*Zan* means the same in Aramaic, and Targum uses it for every *min* in Tanach
(Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur). Professor Avi Hurvitz argues that *zan* is the Late
Biblical Hebrew equivalent of Classical *min*, and Dr. Menachem Zvi Kaddari
proposes that it is a loanword from Old Persian. At each of its three
appearances in Hebrew, the meforshim debate whether it means "type" at all.
*Sug* is post-biblical: in the Mishnah it is a big container, and in the
language of logic it is the **parent category** (genus) above the *min* (Rambam
in Rabbi Moshe Ibn Tibbon's translation, Rabbi Shmuel Ibn Tibbon, the Ran,
Ramchal). Rabbi Klein notes that the Torah's *minim* do not necessarily line up
with the species of modern botany and zoology. On how the Teivah could hold
every known species, Ramban posits a miracle, while the Ran reads its *minim* as
the ancestors of today's species.

## Distinctions

### min (מין)
Thirty appearances in the Chumash, a plurality of them in Shemini: the
non-kosher birds "and its kind" (Vayikra 11:14-19, and Devarim 14:13-18), the
kosher grasshoppers (Vayikra 11:22), the impure creeping things (Vayikra 11:29).
It appears in Bereishis for the kinds of plants (1:11-12), animals (1:24-25) and
creeping creatures (1:21, 1:25), and in the account of the Teivah (Bereishis
6-7). Outside the Chumash it occurs once (Yechezkel 47:10). Ibn Janach and Radak
(*Sefer HaShorashim*): a *min* is a grouping of similar individuals sharing
certain properties, so that they are said to be of one kind and classed
together. Ibn Janach adds that *man*, the manna, is from *min*: a "type" of food
they could not categorize, "for they did not know what it was" (Shemos 16:15).

- Shadal (Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto, 1800 to 1865, to Shemos 20:4) takes
  *temunah*, an image, from MEM-(VAV)-NUN, which he relates to the Arabic
  cognate *mana*, "lie, false": a *temunah* is a picture that pretends to mimic
  something but cannot be a perfect representation of it. A step further, *min*
  is a group of things that look alike enough but are not exactly the same, so
  that grouping them together is, in a way, something of a "falsehood." Shadal
  compares this to Latin *species*, from "seeing," borrowed for things that
  "appear" similar enough to be categorized together. *Species* descends from
  the Proto-Indo-European root *spek-*, ancestor of *spectacle*, *special*,
  *aspect*, *suspect*, *scope* and others. Shadal extends the sense to *min*,
  heretic: each heretic's belief differs slightly from the next, yet they all
  fall into one category.
- Rabbi Yeshayah of Trani takes a different approach, associating *min* in this sense with the pagan deity Meni (Yeshayahu
  65:11).
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) takes the two-letter root מ-ן as
  "creating order out of causes and effects." One group of its derivatives is
  headed by *moneh* and *minyan*, counting, which creates mathematical order by
  quantifying a set of givens; *min* derives from it too, since it likewise
  determines what belongs in one grouping and what is excluded (in Rabbi Klein's
  colloquial gloss, what "counts" and what does not). Like Shadal, Pappenheim
  connects *temunah* to *min*, on his own reasoning: a *temunah* is a relatively
  vague picture that may show a certain species without being the image of any
  one individual in it, as a drawing shows a cow but not which cow. (See also
  Rav Hirsch to Shemos 20:4, who distinguishes *pesel* from *temunah*.)

*Minnim* at Tehillim 150:4, "praise Him with *minnim* and pipe," is the only
appearance of the instrument, possibly a *hapax legomenon*, and it is contested.
- Rav Saadia Gaon (882 to 942) explains *minnim* as the same as *minim*, the
  plural of *min*, seemingly an ensemble of types of instrument creating one
  unified sound (see also Ibn Ezra, *Sefas Yeser* 95, and to Tehillim 150:4 and
  Daniel 3:5). Or, as Pappenheim prefers, a single instrument that somehow
  incorporates elements of all the types (percussion, string and wind).
- Donash Ibn Labrat (920 to 990) disagrees with Rav Saadia: the *dagesh* in the
  *nun* sets *minnim* apart from *minim*, making it the name of a specific
  instrument unrelated to *min*; this seems to be the approach of *Machberes
  Menachem* (entry on מ-ן), Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer
  HaShorashim*) and Rashi (to Tehillim 150:4).
- Rabbi Yehoshua (Jeremy) Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation proposes that
  *minnim* is a metathesis of *nimim*, "hairs, strings," and so specifically a
  stringed instrument.

On whether the Torah's *minim* are today's species: Rabbi Klein notes that
ancient ways of categorizing flora and fauna do not necessarily line up with the
taxonomy of modern botanists and zoologists, and that the Torah might group
things by different criteria. He illustrates from the Teivah, whose known
dimensions could not hold every known species.

- Ramban (to Bereishis 6:19), the first to address the question, posits a
  miracle that let the Teivah house specimens of all those species.
- Rabbeinu Nissim of Gerona (to Bereishis 6:14) answers differently: the *minim*
  the Torah names there do not correspond to the species known today but are
  their ancestors from before they differentiated, so there were far fewer
  *minim* on the Teivah than one might think.

### zan (זן)
Three times in the Hebrew of Tanach (twice in Tehillim 144:13, once in Divrei
HaYamim II 16:14) and four times in the Aramaic (Daniel 3:5, 3:7, 3:10, 3:15),
where *zenei zemara* (Daniel 3:5) is glossed by Rashi as *minei zemer*, types of
song or music. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*): every time *min* appears
in Tanach, Targum renders it *zan*. The Aramaic *zan* clearly means "type" or
"sort"; the Hebrew is more debatable.

- "Our storehouses full, producing from *zan* to *zan*" (Tehillim 144:13): Rav
  Hirsch (there) seems unsure whether the Hebrew *zan* is related to *mazon*,
  "sustenance," and means types of nourishing food, as Menachem and Rashi
  explain, or to the Aramaic *zan*, meaning more broadly types of food, as Ibn
  Janach explains.
- Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim* (s.v. זון) takes the first approach, but
  elsewhere (there s.v. פוק, and in his commentary to Tehillim 144:13) the
  second.
- "Spices and *znim*" burned in honor of King Asa at his death (Divrei HaYamim
  II 16:14): *Machberes Menachem* reads a specific kind of spice or fragrance,
  not "types," and pseudo-Rashi and Ralbag (there) adopt that.
- Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) and Radak (to Divrei HaYamim II 16:14) read
  types of incense.

So at all three Hebrew appearances the meforshim debate whether it means what
the Aramaic means.

Rabbi Pappenheim, whose way is to set aside Aramaic meanings as far as possible
in favor of uniquely Hebrew explanations, ignores the possibility of a Hebrew
*zan* equal to the Aramaic and derives both Hebrew forms from the two-letter ז-ן,
sustenance and the fulfillment of needs: the first *zan* from *mazon*; the
second as related to *zenus*, lust (a vice that comes from overindulging in
sustenance), since it was a type of spice that served as an aphrodisiac (see
*Bava Kamma* 16b).

- If *zan* and *min* do mean the same thing, Rabbi Klein suggests they are still
  not quite synonymous, because they come from different languages.
- From where in Tanach each word appears, Professor Avi Hurvitz argues that
  *zan* is simply the Late Biblical Hebrew equivalent of the Classical Biblical
  Hebrew *min*.
- Dr. Menachem Zvi Kaddari (1925 to 2011), in his dictionary of Biblical Hebrew,
  proposes that *zan* is a loanword from Old Persian *zana*, "type, class."
- Rabbi Shimon Lavie (*Kesem Paz* to the Zohar, *Hakdamah* 1a) seems to
  understand *Bereishis Rabbah* (28:8) as tying *zan*, species, to the idea that
  many kinds of plants were produced by deliberate crossing, as though the
  plants had been *zonah*, crossed outside their own kind.

### sug (סוג)
Not in Tanach in the sense of "type," "category," or "species"; a later
neologism took on that meaning. In the Mishnah *sug* appears twice as a big
container: produce bought in bulk from a wholesaler may not be tithed from one
portion to exempt the whole load, even if it was all sold in one *sug* and is all
one *min* (*Demai* 5:6), and a *sug* is listed among vessels susceptible to
*tumah* (*Keilim* 16:3). Rabbi Klein's own proposal: perhaps a
category is a conceptual container, an abstract vessel that includes what meets
its criteria and excludes the rest; he therefore derives *sug* from biliteral
ס-ג, "create a boundary, fence," which yields *sugah* (Shir HaShirim 7:3) and
*siyag* (*Avos* 1:1). Its polar opposite, ג-ס, yields words for mixing, an army,
the overly thick, and the familiar or intimate, all of them crossing a line in
breaking down boundaries.

As a term of logic: Rabbi Moshe Ibn Tibbon's translation of Rambam's work on
logic (*Milos HaHigayon* 10) explains *min* as a category that includes
multiple like items and *sug* as the parent category (hypernym) that includes
multiple like *minim*; the same understanding is evident in Rabbi Shmuel Ibn
Tibbon's *Peirush HaMilos HaZaros* and the Ran's *Derashos* (*Derush* 12). In
standard logical English, *sug* is the genus and *min* the species. Ramchal
(Rabbi Moshe Chaim Luzzatto, 1707 to 1746, *Derech Tevunos* 11:9) expands with an
example: mankind is a *min* within the *sug* of living creatures, but compared
with physical entities, living creatures becomes a *min* within that broader
*sug*. So *min* and *sug* depend on context, while the
relationship between them always remains that of parent and child.

## Rendering guidance

- *Lemineihu* in Bereishis and Shemini: "according to its kind." Be careful
  with "species": Rabbi Klein cautions that the Torah's categories do not
  necessarily line up with modern taxonomy, and the Ran reads the Teivah's
  *minim* as ancestral kinds (Ramban posits a miracle instead). Shadal, for his
  part, likens *min* to Latin *species*, both named from looking alike, so the
  caution concerns modern taxonomy, not the word's origin.
- Trap: English "kind," "type" and "sort" will absorb *min*, *zan* and *sug*
  indifferently. In a logical passage the pair must be fixed: *sug* is "genus,"
  *min* is "species," and if "species" has been used for *lemineihu* elsewhere
  in the same work, add the Hebrew in brackets at the logical use.
- Where a Targum is quoted beside its pasuk, keep one English word across both,
  since Targum renders every *min* as *zan*; two words would invent a
  distinction the Targum does not make.
- Tehillim 144:13 is a genuine fork. "From kind to kind" follows Ibn Janach's
  Aramaic-sense *zan*; a rendering built on *mazon*, "sustenance," follows
  Menachem and Rashi. Rav Hirsch seems unsure, and Radak takes each in a
  different place. Pick one and footnote the other rather than blending them.
- *Min* as heretic is a separate entry's business; never let "sectarian" or
  "heretic" bleed into a passage where *min* means kind, and be alert that a
  derashah may deliberately play on both. In Modern Hebrew *min* also means
  gender or sex, since it makes two easily defined categories; do not carry that
  back into a pasuk.
