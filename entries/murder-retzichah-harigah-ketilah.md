# Murder: retzichah / harigah / ketilah

## Bottom line

*Retzichah* is **murder**: killing with no legal warrant, and the word itself
passes the verdict. *Harigah* is **killing** plain, lawful or not, direct or
brought about at a distance, and it covers animals and plants as well as people.
*Ketilah* is the Aramaic word the Targumim use for both, the ordinary Aramaic for
**kill**; in the few Hebrew pesukim it is disputed. *Lo tirtzach* means
"do not murder," not "do not kill," and the choice of root, Bedersi explains,
confines the prohibition of extra-judicial killing to people, not animals or
plants.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| רציחה | retzichah | murder | unlawful by definition (Rashbam); humans only (Bedersi); by one's own hand (Bedersi; Abarbanel disagrees) |
| הריגה | harigah | killing, slaying | value-free (Rashbam, Pappenheim); includes indirect killing and animals (Bedersi) |
| קטילה | ketilah | killing (Aramaic) | the Targum's rendering of both; "cutting" in some sugyos; Hebrew usage disputed |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether retzichah and harigah differ at all.**

- Rabbi Nathan Adler cites the two standing side by side in Tehillim 94:6 as
  evidence that they are synonymous.
- Rashbam, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*)
  hold they are not: the *rotzeach* of Bamidbar 35:16-18 and Devarim 19:4-6 is
  the murderer, whether *b'mezid* or *b'shogeg*, and Eliyahu's rebuke to Achav
  is *haratzachta v'gam yarashta* (Melachim I 21:19). *Harigah*, by contrast,
  sits on both sides of the line: the woman put to death by beis din for
  bestiality is killed with *harigah* (Vayikra 20:16), because that execution is
  legally justified, and so is Kayin's killing of Hevel (Bereishis 4:8), which
  was the first homicide and a murder.

**Whether retzichah reaches indirect killing.**

- Bedersi: *retzichah* is only for the one who did the deed himself, while
  *harigah* covers causing a death at a remove, which is why Shaul is said to
  have killed (*harag*) the kohanim of Nov (Shmuel I 22:21) and why Nosson tells
  David *oso haragta b'cherev bnei Ammon* (Shmuel II 12:9).
- Abarbanel (to Shemos 20:13) disagrees, citing Shoftim 20:4.

**What separates them, if not legality.** Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer, citing
Rabbi Shlomo Galblum's concordance *Sefer HaMilim*: *retzichah* is the death
blow delivered, while *harigah* is the act of killing as such.

**Whom retzichah covers.** *Sefer Yeraim* (§175) holds *retzichah* applies only
to killing a Jew; Rabbi Yaakov Berger (*Milon Leshon Mikra*) asks whether that
was meant about the word or about the halachah, and leaves it open.

**What ketilah means.**
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) identifies the Hebrew ק-ט-ל with the
  Aramaic and defines both as killing, so that for him the Hebrew *ketilah* is a
  synonym of *harigah*; Rashi likewise renders *ketilah*, in both languages, as
  *harigah* (Ovadiah 1:9; Daniel 2:13; Pesachim 111a; Gittin 59a; Bava Kamma
  23b; Chullin 11b; Niddah 20b).
- Rabbi Nathan Adler holds it need not mean killing at all but striking, since
  the Gemara speaks of full *ketilah* against partial *ketilah* (Bava Kamma 65a;
  Bava Metzia 95a; Chullin 35b; Niddah 55b), and there is no partial killing.
- Rabbi Pappenheim splits the languages: in Aramaic it equals *harigah* and
  *retzichah*, but the Hebrew *ketilah* is striking a man into a helpless state
  on the verge of death, so that though he may technically still be alive he has
  effectively been killed; he takes the root as a portmanteau of the biliterals
  ק-ט, "cutting/submitting," and ט-ל, "throwing/lowering." Rabbi Klein adds that
  there may be some support for this in *Sefer HaChochmah* (ascribed to Rabbi
  Elazar Rokeach of Worms), which has *ketilah* as killing in a prolonged and
  tortuous way rather than at one stroke.

Hence two accounts of Onkelos's *lo tiktol nefesh*: Wertheimer, that *ketilah*
alone would have implied animals; Rabbi Adler, only striking.

## Distinctions

### retzichah (רציחה)
Root ר-צ-ח. The one exception to the rule is Bamidbar 35:27, 35:30, where the
*go'el hadam* who kills the accidental killer is described with *retzichah*
though the killing is permitted; Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 20:13) addresses it.

- Bedersi draws the practical conclusion from the word's restriction to people:
  the Aseres HaDibros says *lo tirtzach* rather than a form of *harigah* so that
  the prohibition governs killing people and not animals or plants (contrast
  Vayikra 20:15, 20:16; Bamidbar 22:29; Yeshayahu 22:13, 27:1 for animals;
  Tehillim 78:47 for plants).
- Rabbi Pappenheim reaches it from the other side: only human beings form
  societies with laws.
- Rav Hirsch, working his system of etymological links between roots with
  phonetically similar letters, connects ר-צ-ח to ר-ש-ע, wickedness, which leads
  him to class murder as the ultimate act of evil and the most heinous of
  crimes; and to ר-צ-ה, "will," and the Aramaic ר-ש-ה, "permission," so that
  murder is an illegitimate expression of a man's free will, though otherwise he
  has permission to want whatever he chooses.
- Rabbi Yaakov Berger suggests reading ר-צ-ח as a metathesized form of ח-ר-ץ,
  "cutting," the murderer cutting off another's life.

### harigah (הריגה)
Root ה-ר-ג.

- Rabbi Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ר-ג, subduing something completely
  by closing or choking it off, since the killer subdues his victim in the most
  absolute way there is; from the same root he takes *regimah*, stoning, and
  *arigah*, weaving.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 35:25) hints at the same *harigah*/*arigah* link, and
  (to Bereishis 30:27) connects ה-ר-ג with ע-ר-ג, "desiring/yearning".
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus takes the ר-ג family further, to *regev* (a clump of
  dirt), *regel* (foot), *rogez* (anger) and *regesh* (feeling), all of them
  bound up, in one way or another, with subduing, conquering or trampling.

### ketilah (קטילה)
The standard Targumic rendering of both Hebrew words, seven times in the Aramaic
of Daniel (2:13-14, 3:22, 5:19, 5:30, 7:11), and the ordinary verb for killing
in Talmudic Aramaic; grammarians use ק-ט-ל as the Aramaic paradigm root the way
they use פ-ע-ל in Hebrew. It appears four times in the Hebrew of Tanach (Iyov 13:15,
24:14; Tehillim 139:19; Ovadiah 1:9). In a number of sugyos the Aramaic verb
means "cut" rather than "kill" (Shabbos 98a; Eruvin 28b; Moed Katan 12b;
Yevamos 121b; Bava Kamma 96a; Sanhedrin 33a, 74b; Shevuos 46a; Bechoros 8b);
perhaps, Rabbi Klein suggests, that is its literal sense, cutting the soul away
from the body, which is another way of characterizing death, so that *ketilah*
names an aspect of killing without being quite synonymous with *harigah* and
*retzichah*.

The Mishnah in several places (Shabbos 6:1; Sotah 1:6; Avodah Zarah 1:8; Meilah
5:1; Keilim 11:8) mentions a piece of jewelry called a *katla*, which Rashi
(Shabbos 57b; Sotah 7b) explains is a very tight necklace, a choker. Perhaps,
Rabbi Klein suggests, the name relates to *ketilah* in the sense of killing,
since a woman wearing such an adornment might feel as though she were being
killed.

### mitah (מיתה) and the rabbinic terms
Sometimes the verb for killing is a cognate of *mitah*, "death," the killer
causing death to visit his victim; that verbiage is a general term for death of
every sort, whether by natural causes, by murder, or by being killed otherwise.
Rabbinic parlance also has *netillas neshamah*, "taking the soul," and
*shefichas damim*, "spilling blood."

## Rendering guidance

- *Lo tirtzach*: "do not murder." "Thou shalt not kill" is the classic English
  error, and it makes the Torah forbid what it elsewhere commands.
- Trap: English "kill" absorbs all three roots. Where a passage contrasts them,
  use "murder" for *retzichah* and "kill" or "put to death" for *harigah*.
- הרג of an animal or a plant: "kill," never "murder"; where a king brought a
  death about at a distance (Shmuel II 12:9), "had him killed."
- קטל in a Targum or a sugya: "kill"; where it means "cut," "cut."
