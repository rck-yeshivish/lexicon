# Law: din / mishpat / chok / dat / nimus

## Bottom line

*Chok* is **statute** (or **decree**): a law whose reason is not revealed (Yoma
67b, Rambam), set and unchanging (Pappenheim). *Mishpat* is **ordinance** or
**judgment**: the rational, mostly interpersonal law, and the verdict a judge
reaches by weighing two sides (Rambam, Rabbi Moshe Shapiro). *Din* is
**judgment** or **law** in the sense of the case and its penalty: the dispute
itself (Bedersi) and the penal system (Wertheimer). *Dat* is **law, edict, royal
decree**: in Megillas Esther the king's law, and in Devarim 33:2, where the
Masorah reads *aish dat*, Divine law. Rabbi Klein calls "religion" a
mistranslation and holds that the original sense of *dat* may have nothing to do
with religion as such. *Nimus* (Rabbinic, from Greek *nomos*) is **law, rule**,
and Rabbi Nissim of Marseilles separates it from *dat*: *nimus* is the rules
society sets, *dat* what Hashem commands.

Rabbi Klein does not decide how *dat* differs from *din*. Many answers have been
offered over the generations, many of them differing only in nuance. He gives
what he calls a simplified overview, and says that doing the question justice
would mean working through every instance of both words in Tanach to see which
answer is most convincing.

## Distinctions

### chok (חק)
Root ח-ק-ק, "engrave." The pair is set by the pesukim: "this is the *chok* of the
Torah" of the parah adumah (Bamidbar 19:2), "there He placed for them *chok* and
*mishpat*" at Marah (Shemos 15:25), and "you shall do My *mishpatim* and
safeguard My *chukim*" (Vayikra 18:4-5). Yoma 67b on that last: *chukim* are the
mitzvos the *yetzer hara* mocks as pointless (pork, *shaatnez*, *chalitzah*, the
*metzora*, the *sa'ir la'azazel*); the Gemara raises the possibility that they
really are pointless and answers from the end of the pasuk, "I am Hashem, I
promulgated them (*chakaktiv*)," and you have no license to second-guess them.
Rambam (*Shemonah Perakim* 6, *Moreh* 3:26) follows the sugya closely: *chukim*
have a rationale hidden from us, *mishpatim* a rationale more evident. Rabbeinu
Bachya (to Bereishis 26:5; Shemos 15:26; Devarim
6:17, 7:12), Ibn Yachya (to Tehillim 147:19) and Radak (to Tehillim 119:1)
follow him: *chukim* are mitzvos whose reasons were not revealed, *mishpatim*
the intuitive laws of interpersonal life. *Peirush HaRokeach*, put simply:
*issur v'heter* versus *dinim*.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: because *chukim* are not intuitive, only the One who
commands them truly knows the reason and everyone else is following orders, so
they are the likelier to be forgotten and therefore tend to be committed to
writing to last (Yeshayahu 30:8, Iyov 19:23). He also holds that *chok* was borrowed for
any quota set from above (the brick tally of Shemos 5:14), whose prescribed
number is as arbitrary as an unexplained law. Rabbi Klein notes that *chok* is
commonly rendered "statute" in English, a type of law whose details seem
somewhat arbitrary: a statute of limitations sets a period that could as easily
have been longer or shorter. Pappenheim further reads engraved writing as
inseparable and everlasting, so *chok* is an unmitigated, unchanging law, which
is why the word is so often joined to "forever" (Shemos 12:14, 30:21; Vayikra
3:16, 23:14).

- Rabbi Eliyahu Menachem Margules (*Emunas Eliezer*): *chok* does not inherently
  mean a mitzvah with no known reason, but carries that implication through
  *chakuk*, engraved. Obedience out of rational understanding is conditional and
  lasts only while the reason holds; obedience out of commitment to Hashem's
  will is unconditional and so more engraved in the person, and the Mishnah's
  love that depends on nothing and is never lost (Avos 5:16) is the parallel.
- Rabbi Shlomo Astruc (to Shemos 15:26): a *chok* is so called because it
  engraves on the soul a picture of how Hashem's commands are to be carried out.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 119:5): kuf interchanges with gimmel, so *chok* is kin
  to *chag*, at root a circle, and a *chok* encircles us with mitzvos and
  further chances to develop ourselves. The basis for that connection is a
  midrash (*Shemos Rabbah* 15:25) on "He told His words to Yaakov, His *chukim*
  and *mishpatim* to Yisrael" (Tehillim 147:19), which reads it of the yamim
  tovim, since the *chagim* are elsewhere called a *chok* (Tehillim 81:4-5) and
  their dates turn on the calendrical rulings of the court.
- Shadal (to Bereishis 26:5): *chok* is related to *chag*, a circle: a statute
  fixes a law to which nothing may be added or taken away, as a circle marks
  exact bounds.

It might seem from all this that *chukim* outrank *mishpatim* and accomplish
more. Rabbi Yisrael Salanter points out that in practice the reverse is
generally true: *chukim* often serve as stepping stones toward *mishpatim*, since
keeping the mitzvos whose reasons are not revealed trains a man's intellect to
grasp the reasons behind the *mishpatim* and to keep those too. He describes
*chukim* as rungs on a ladder leading to the fulfillment of *mishpatim*, the
pinnacle of moral perfection.

### mishpat (משפט)
Root ש-פ-ט, "judge."
- Yoma 67b: the laws that would have been written had the Torah not commanded
  them (idolatry, arayos, murder, theft, blasphemy).
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: not "a law with a reason" by definition but a decision
  between two viable options, as a *shofet* decides between two legitimate
  litigants (*Tikkunei Zohar*, *Patach Eliyahu*: the Middle Pillar).
- Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*): against *din*, *mishpat* is the verdict rendered
  that settles the dispute.
- Pappenheim: what ethics demands; a worker's wage is a *mishpat*.
- Wertheimer: the judiciary's discretion among legal outcomes.
- Onkelos renders *mishpat* as *din* (Shemos 21:1) and as *halachah* (21:9).

Keeping a *mishpat*, whose reason is meant to be understood, means functioning
like a judge who weighs his options and reasons to a conclusion; a *chok* asks
no such decision, the law being already set in stone.

### chok and mishpat as Written and Oral Torah
Many sources tie the pair to the two Torahs, and they divide on which is which.

- Rabbi Berachiah Beirach Shapiro (*Zera Beirach*): *chok* alludes to the
  Written Torah, which is not fully explained, and *mishpat* to the Oral Torah,
  which reveals the logic behind the Written Torah's terse commandments.
- The *Zohar* (3:113a) takes the opposite view: *chok* is the Oral Torah and
  *mishpat* the Written. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg, explaining the *Zohar*,
  connects *chok* to *cheik*, the bosom, and so the middle or interior of a
  person, since what is engraved is cut into a thing's interior; the Written
  Torah is an accessible text with a plain sense on its surface and so
  corresponds to *mishpat*, while the Oral Torah digs into the interior for the
  less obvious meanings and so corresponds to *chok*.

Both readings stand, neither preferred.

### din (דין)
Root ד-י-ן.
- Pappenheim: DALET-NUN is judicial decision-making and its execution; hence
  *dan*, *din* itself, *dayan*, *madon* (litigation), *adon* (one with
  jurisdiction over another, a husband over his wife or a master over his
  slave), *Adonai* as the Master of all creation, and *medinah* (the area under
  one court system). Rabbi Klein adds on his own account the given names Dan,
  Daniel, Avidan and Dinah.
- Bedersi: the controversy or fight between two opposing litigants, so a
  litigant is a *baal din* (Avos 4:22). A similar point is implied by *gmar
  din*, a judicial decision or sentencing.
- Wertheimer: the penal system that metes out punishment to those who break the
  law.
- Maharal: law the intellect presumes true; or, alternatively, law between man
  and his fellow, that is civil law.
- Rabbi Yosef Nechemias (to Esther 1:13): the letter of the law.

*Din* is frequent in Tanach but occurs only twice in Chumash, both in the one
pasuk charging the Sanhedrin that sits in the Mikdash with deciding "between
*din* and *din*" (Devarim 17:8).

### dat (דת)
**Where it occurs.** Twenty times in Megillas Esther, where it covers the body
of law the king's jurists have mastered (1:13), the drinking "according to the
*dat*, without duress" (1:8), what is to be done with Vashti "according to the
*dat*" (1:15), Haman's charge that the Jews keep a *dat* unlike every other
nation's and do not keep the king's *dat* (3:8), and the promulgation of both
the decree against the Jews and the later leave to defend themselves, "the *dat*
was given in Shushan the capital" (3:15, 8:14). The only other occurrence in the
Hebrew of Tanach is Ezra 8:36, besides *dat* or *data* another fourteen times in
the Aramaic of Daniel and Ezra, and possibly the *krei* of אשדת (Devarim 33:2,
so Menachem Ibn Saruk in *Machberes Menachem*; Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon and Radak
do not list that verse under *dat*). Several proper names are connected to the
word: Dosan (Bereishis 37:17) per *Old Midrash Tanchuma* (*Vayeshev* §12),
Dasan (Bamidbar 16:1) per Sanhedrin 109b, and Yedusun (Tehillim 62:1) per
*Yalkut Shimoni* there. In today's Hebrew *dati* means religious, as in *dati
leumi*, and secular Israelis often call religious Jews *dosim* after the
Ashkenazi pronunciation of the tav; but Rabbi Klein holds that the word's
original sense may have had nothing to do with religion as such.

Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 11:7), writing on how a language expresses the essence
of things, observes that the Torah has no word for religion. Where religion is
one part of life it can be named, and the name marks it off from everything
else; where all of life, from birth until after death, is bound up with it, no
one can take its measure or give it a name, since it informs everything and
everything is included in it.

**What separates it from *din*.**
- Rabbi Shmuel Laniado is clear that one of the two words means the Torah's laws
  while the other means either pragmatic measures for temporary circumstances or
  the rules set by the governing power; Rabbi Klein's objection is that Laniado
  himself is unsure which word is which.
- Rabbi Yosef Nechemias (to Esther 1:13): *din* is the letter of the law and
  *dat* what was done in practice, so that even if Vashti was within her rights
  not to appear (*din*), there might still be ground to punish her as an
  extra-judicial deterrent to others minded to defy the king (*dat*); this is
  why the Megillah puts *dat* before *din*, though in theory *din* should come
  first.
- Ibn Ezra (to Esther 1:13): *dat* is the law of the land, here of the kings of
  Persia and Media, and *din* the particular verdict decreed on the offender;
  alternatively, he suggests, *dat* is what Divine fate dictates and *din* what
  the astrological forces decree, a reading also cited by Rabbi Moshe Chalavah
  there.
- Maharal: the laws dictated by the established norms of a polity, essentially
  common law, against *din* as the laws the intellect presumes true; or,
  alternatively, *dat* is law between man and himself, ethics, morality and
  religion, and *din* law between man and his fellow.
- Rabbi Yosef Zecharia Stern likewise: *dat* is the norms a country needs for
  governance or for a given situation, a pragmatic matter, and *din* is justice
  set in law, a logical and moral one.
- Wertheimer: *dat* is the rule of law itself, whether revealed or merely
  societal, while *mishpat* is the judiciary's discretion and *din* the penal
  system.
- Rabbi Tzadok HaKohen of Lublin: *din* is law promulgated on logic and
  rationality, *dat* the ruler's prerogative to legislate by fiat even where
  reason does not carry it; Haman's charge (Esther 3:8) alluded to *dina
  d'malchusa dina* (see Gittin 6b), that a government's *din* is recognized as
  halachah, and his claim was that the Jews kept Achashverosh's *din*, rooted in
  reason, but not his *dat*, which might be irrational, and were therefore
  disloyal. Rabbi Tzadok adds that in Torah the two are a tandem, *dat* and
  *din* being Written and Oral Torah, both needed to keep the law properly.

**Etymology.**
- Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) traces *dat* to the biliteral DALET-TAV or the
  monoliteral DALET: practice by force of custom, whether a custom of one
  person, a family, or a nation; he also connects *odos*, on the topic of, to
  the same root. He contrasts *mishpat*, what ethics demands, and *chok*, what
  the law of the land demands: a worker's wage is a *mishpat*, one's taxes a
  *chok*, and tipping the bearer of good news a *dat*.
- Others trace *dat* to the same etymon as *din*: Avraham Even-Shoshan, in his
  Hebrew dictionary, holds the tav of *dat* to be a suffix added to *din* with
  the nun dropped, so that the two share a root, and Yehoshua Yaron (*Mevo
  L'Midrash HaLashon*) proposes much the same.
- The prevailing understanding in scholarly circles, however, is that *dat*
  comes from Old Iranian: the etymology was first proposed by Gesenius, who
  traced Hebrew and Aramaic *dat* to Persian *data*, the passive past participle
  of *da*, "to give," which linguists now trace to the Proto-Indo-European roots
  *dō-* and *dǝ-*, "to give." Benjamin J. Noonan, *Non-Semitic Loanwords in the
  Hebrew Bible: A Lexicon of Language Contact* (2019), p. 89, writes that no
  reason exists to reject the loan hypothesis and that Biblical Hebrew and
  Aramaic *dat* undoubtedly come from Old Iranian *data-*. If so, how does a
  Persian word reach Chumash, written long before the Jews met the Persians?
  Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim La'Mikra* to Devarim 33:2) writes that this
  does not trouble him, since he finds Egyptian, Greek and possibly even Latin
  words in Chumash.

**אשדת (Devarim 33:2).** The word is obscure, and Bible critics have dealt with
it by a heretical emendation of the penultimate letter.

- The Masoretic tradition reads it as two words, *aish* and *dat*, and on that
  reading Ramban has a religion of fire, Onkelos a Torah given through fire, and
  Rashi the primordial Torah written in fire.
- Abarbanel has two faces to Torah, a fire that burns up sinners and a *dat*, a
  legal framework, for those who want to act justly.
- Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 33:2) derives *dat* from DALET-VAV-TAV, attested mainly
  in Mishnaic Hebrew: a *dut* is a cistern (Rosh HaShanah 3:7, Bava Basra 4:2,
  Keilim 5:6 and 7:1, Ohalos 5:6), always paired with *bor*, and unlike a *bor*
  dug into the ground a *dut* is built up at the level on which people walk, so
  that just as its life-giving water is easy to reach, *dat* gives guidance for
  life that a person can readily apply to himself. He explains *aish dat* from
  the ever-changing contours of a raging fire, which answer to the ever-shifting
  circumstances of life, the Torah being the gift that supplies guidance for
  every kind of situation. Hirsch further suggests, on the interchange of dalet
  and tav, a phonetic tie between *dat* and *dod*, an uncle being a close
  relative charged with the sort of help one expects from a parent, the
  implication being that Hashem gave the Torah as that kind of support against
  the *aish* of life; he suggests connecting *dud*, a pot, and *dad*, a breast,
  as well.

A second stream takes אשדת as written, without splitting it.

- Shadal (to Devarim 33:2) reads it as a form of *ashdos* or *eshed*, a
  waterfall or mountain slope (Bamidbar 21:15; Devarim 3:17, 4:49; Yehoshua
  10:40, 12:3, 12:8, 13:20), from ALEPH-SHIN-DALET; his student Rabbi Moshe
  Tedeschi (*Otzar Nirdafim* §402) traces those to the Aramaic SHIN-DALET-ALEPH,
  pouring, the slope being where melted snow and canyon water pour out.
- Ibn Janach, in *Sefer HaShorashim*, had already rejected that root when Abu
  Omar Ibn Yaqwi, a Hebrew grammarian of Cordoba, argued it, and follows the
  Masoretic view that אשדת is a portmanteau of *aish* and *dat*.

**Bedersi.** He does not treat *dat* and *din* as a pair at all. He discusses
*din* as a possible synonym of *mishpat*, and two entries later treats *dat* as
an ostensible synonym of *chok*, though Rabbi Klein finds it not quite clear
how Bedersi distinguishes those two. What is clear is that he rejects Ibn Janach's
definition of *dat* as *bris*, covenant, and prefers to see *dat* as effectively
coterminous with *din*.

### nimus (נימוס)
Rabbinic, and a loanword from Greek *nomos*, law, with the pronunciation, it
seems, slightly modified. *Nomos* underlies English *economy*, *astronomy*,
*autonomy*, *antinomy*, *nomism*, *antinomianism* and *nomic*, and the name
Deuteronomy is *duet*, twice, plus *nomos*, a direct Greek
rendering of Chazal's *Mishneh Torah*. It does not appear in Tanach and appears
once in Mishnah: a husband must tell his agent plainly to write and give a *get*,
and "free her," "sustain her," "do for her as the *nimus*" or "do for her as is
fit" are hints too vague to authorize it (Gittin 6:5), *nimus* there meaning the
law. It occurs elsewhere in both Talmuds, as in Mordechai being "crowned by his
*nimusim*" (Megillah 12b). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*, *Sefer HaTishbi*):
Targum uses it for *chok* (Vayikra 18:3), *mishpat* (Shmuel I 2:13, Melachim I
18:28, Yechezkel 5:7 and 20:18), and *torah* (Tehillim 1:2, Mishlei 1:8). Many
of the sources above on *dat* in fact use *nimus* to define it; Rabbi Nissim of
Marseilles (*Maaseh Nissim*) sets them apart: *nimus* is the rules and
regulations society sets, *dat* what Hashem commands. In modern Hebrew *nimus*
has lost the legal sense and means manners or etiquette.

## Rendering guidance

- חוקים ומשפטים: "statutes and ordinances" (or "decrees and laws"). Keep the
  order and keep two words; the pair is the Yoma 67b contrast.
- דת ודין (Esther 1:13): "law and judgment." Rabbi Yosef Nechemias reads the
  order as deliberate (practical *dat* before theoretical *din*); do not reorder.
- Standalone דת in Esther: "edict," "decree," or "law." Not "religion," which
  Rabbi Klein calls a mistranslation; on his reading the original sense of the
  word may have nothing to do with religion as such, and the modern *dati* sense
  should not be read back into the pasuk. Devarim 33:2: "a fiery law."
  Standalone דין in mussar: "judgment" (*middas hadin*) or "the law" (its
  strict letter); בעל דין "litigant," גמר דין "sentence."
- Trap: "law" covers all five, and "judgment" covers both *din* and *mishpat*.
  Where a passage sets two against each other, give each its own English.
  "Statute" is *chok* only; *dat* is "edict" (issued), not "engraved."
  Pappenheim's test case: wage = *mishpat*, tax = *chok*, gratuity = *dat*.
