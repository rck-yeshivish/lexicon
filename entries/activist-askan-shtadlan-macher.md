# Communal activist: askan / shtadlan (esek, shadal, shadar)

## Bottom line

An *askan* is a **communal activist**: he busies himself with whatever needs
doing and gets it done himself. A *shtadlan* is an **intercessor** or lobbyist:
he goes to the authorities on the community's behalf to change their position.
The whole distinction between the two is that the one acts and the other
persuades. Behind them stand *esek*, **dealing** with a matter (and in Tanach,
fighting over one), and *shadal*, **making an effort** or **persuading**. The
Yiddish *macher* is simply a **doer**.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| עסקן | askan | communal activist | verifies and does for himself (Rashi to Chullin 57b); gets his hands dirty |
| שתדלן | shtadlan | intercessor, lobbyist | goes to the authorities on the community's behalf; persuasion, not action |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where the Mishnaic esek comes from.**

- Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (1606-1675) in *Mussaf HaAruch*: the rabbinic sense of
  "busy, preoccupied" derives from Greek, though he supplies no cognate Greek
  word. Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894) in *Aruch HaShalem* presumes that Rabbi
  Mussafia meant the Greek ἀσχέω (*áschéó*), which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
  glosses, on the strength of Google Translate, as "I don't care", possibly what
  a man already busy with one thing feels about being asked to do another.
- Kohut himself rejects that etymology and assumes instead that the Rabbinic
  Hebrew sense grows out of the root's original Biblical meaning, the sin of
  ע-ש-ק morphing into a samech in Rabbinic Hebrew, an interchange that is not at
  all unusual.
- Kohut neglects to explain the semantic shift from *esek* as "fighting" to
  *esek* as "busy dealing"; Rabbi Klein supplies that bridge himself, and to him
  the connection is patently obvious, since fighting is marked by the obstinate
  persistence of parties who refuse to back down, and that same perseverance
  describes those who toil for the public good. He adds that even in rabbinic
  literature *esek* somewhat retains its Biblical sense, since it sometimes
  refers specifically to a matter that is subject to controversy (Rashi to
  Shavuos 31a, Bava Metzia 14a).

That is where Rabbi Klein comes out: Kohut's own reading, with his own
bridge.

**What eshtadur is.**

- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*): a quadriliteral root א-ש-ד-ר,
  unrelated to *shadal*.
- Ramban seems to take the aleph as extraneous to the core root ש-ד-ר, and reads
  the word as rebellion, contravening (Ezra 4:15, 4:19).

Ramban's reading is what brings *eshtadur* into the one family of *shadar* and
*shadal*; Rabbi Ernest Klein accepts Ramban's etymology connecting *shadal* with
*shadar*.

**How wide shadal is.**

- Ramban (to Bereishis 32:25, Shemos 22:15) points out that *shadal* has various
  meanings, and centers one of them on persuasion: Onkelos renders Yaakov's
  wrestling with the angel of Esav (*vaye'aveik*) as *ishtadel*, and Ramban sees
  that struggle as each side trying to convince the other, using *esek* itself
  to define *shadal*.
- But the Targum to Yechezkel 38:4 and Rus 2:19 uses *shadal* for any sort of
  attempt or effort, whether or not a second party is being worked on, which is
  the sense of the ordinary *hishtadlus*, and of *hishtadel lihyos ish* (Avos
  2:5, and see 4:18) and *yishtadel adam* (Berachos 58a).

To Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein it seems that "attempting" is the base, with
persuading, rebelling, and fighting derived from it, since each is a matter of
not backing down.

## Distinctions

### esek (עסק, עשק)
The root ע-ש-ק occurs exactly twice in Tanach, both in one pasuk: Yitzchak named
the well *Esek* because Avimelech's shepherds *his'asku*, fought, with his own
(Bereishis 26:20). In the Mishnah the samech form means being taken up with
something, often so taken up that another act is done haphazardly or unknowingly
(*Shekalim* 6:2, *Rosh Hashanah* 4:8, *Sotah* 1:9, *Kiddushin* 4:14, *Eduyos*
2:5, *Kerisos* 4:3, *Taharos* 7:8, *Niddah* 8:2, *Zavim* 2:2). From there: *osek
b'divrei Torah* for toil in learning; *esek* as an affair one must attend to
(*Maaser Sheini* 4:7, *Shabbos* 23:4); *esek gadol* for making much of the
cutting of the Omer (*Menachos* 10:3); and the Targumim's *esek* (Bereishis
12:17, 20:11, 20:18; Yehoshua 14:6) and *iska* (Koheles 3:1, 3:18, 7:8, 8:2) for
any matter needing attention. *Esek* also means commerce (*Avos* 4:10), as does
the Aramaic *iska* (Rashi to Berachos 56a, Shabbos 19b, Moed Katan 22a, Kesubos
80b, Kiddushin 30b), and it extends to other transactions including shidduchim
(Kesubos 8a, Bava Metzia 68b). In Modern Hebrew an *osek* is a freelancer and
*issuk* one's occupation.

Several old siddurim spell *la'asok* in the daily Birkas HaTorah with a sin in
place of the samech: so the *Siddur Areshet Sefatayim* (Amsterdam, 1906) and
Seligman Baer's *Avodas Yisrael*.

### askan (עסקן)
Rashi (to Chullin 57b): Rabbi Shimon ben Chalafta was called an *askan* because he went out to test for himself what other authorities reported. The word therefore carries the extra exertion of doing a thing with
one's own hands, which is exactly the modern communal *askan*.

### shadal (שדל) and shtadlan (שתדלן)
Targum Onkelos renders *pitui*, seducing or convincing, as *shadal* (Shemos
22:15), and Rashi (there, and to Yoma 35b, Kiddushin 31a) confirms that the
Hebrew and Aramaic terms match. The Aramaic term recurs throughout the Gemara
for convincing, cajoling, or seducing another into agreeing to what one wants.
It seems to derive from the root ש-ד-ל, but no words from that root appear in
Tanach itself. Sforno (to Bereishis 26:20) reads the shepherds' *his'asku* as
*hishtadlu*, that they were trying to talk Yitzchak's men out of the well,
which is the bridge from *esek* to *shtadlanus*. A *shtadlan*, then, is one
whose work is to work on others. (A homograph: the Italian scholar R. Shmuel
Dovid Luzzatto, 1800-1865, whom Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein calls controversial, is sometimes
known by his initials as Shadal.)

### shadar (שדר)
Biblical Aramaic, where it appears at least once: Daryavesh, distressed at
Daniel's sentence, *mishtadar* until sunset to save him (Daniel 6:15), meaning
he tried everything he could (Tosafos to Kiddushin 39b). Pseudo-Rasag there
gives an explanation similar to Ramban's and likewise reaches for *esek*, that
the king had to fight to convince his officers to save Daniel; Ramban theorizes
that *shadal* and *shadar* are cognates, on the ground that lamed and reish
often interchange, and notes that some versions of *Avos* 2:5 read *hishtadar*
for *hishtadel*. *Shadar* has another sense: in post-Biblical Aramaic it means "to send", which has nothing to do with striving (see the *malach*/*shaliach*
entry).

### macher (Yiddish)
The Yiddish *macher* comes from *mach*, to do, a cognate of English "make". The
word is not a technical one: a *macher* is just a man who goes around getting
things done for the good of the community, as the *askan* and the *shtadlan* do.

## Rendering guidance

- The three in one line: an *askan* is busy with the community's affairs and
  deals with whatever arises; a *shtadlan* intercedes with the authorities to
  change their minds; a *macher* just does things.
- Trap: "activist" and "operator" will each swallow both *askan* and
  *shtadlan*. Where a passage sets them side by side, use "activist" for the
  first and "intercessor" (or "lobbyist", in a modern setting) for the second.
- *Hishtadlus* is "effort" or "one's own effort", the Targum's wide sense, not
  "lobbying". Reserve the persuasion sense of *shadal* for where a second party
  is being worked on.
- Bereishis 26:20: *Esek* as a well's name wants a gloss, not a translation.
  "He called the well Esek, because they had contended with him" keeps the
  wordplay visible.
