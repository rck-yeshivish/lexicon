# Gate: shaar / tera / bava

## Bottom line

All three mean **gate**: *shaar* in Hebrew, *tera* in Targumic Aramaic, *bava*
in the Aramaic of the Gemara. They are not synonyms within one language, and
each carries a second, non-architectural sense. In the Mishnah *shaar* is
usually a gate and less commonly a market **price**, and in the Gemara *bava*
can also be a **section** of a text, as in Bava Kamma. The authorities differ
over whether that textual sense has anything to do with a gate. Rabbi Uriel
Frank says it has none. *Tosafos Yom Tov* reads the names of the three masechtos
as gates. Ibn Tibbon explains that a section of a book is called a *shaar*
because its statements are like the citizens of one city, who all share the one
gate; Rabbi Klein thinks it not farfetched to say the same of *bava*.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why *tera* means gate.**

- The obvious answer is that it is *shaar* Aramaicized, Hebrew *shin* becoming
  Aramaic *tav* with *ayin* and *reish* transposed.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) suggests another, which Rabbi Klein
  finds more convincing: the Targumim use *tera* to render Hebrew words for
  demolition, destruction, rupturing and breaching (Vayikra 14:45; Devarim 12:3;
  Shmuel II 6:8; Yechezkel 22:30), so a gate is seemingly nothing more than a
  break in the wall; that they also use it for *pesach*, "opening" (Bereishis
  18:1; Shemos 33:9; Shoftim 4:20), supports this.

**Why a section of a sefer is called a *bava* or a *shaar*.** Rabbi Uriel Frank
of Mitzpe Yericho, founder of the Maane Lashon Society, says the truth is that
this has nothing to do with gates, and rests it on the dictionary of Talmudic
Aramaic of his father, Rabbi Yitzchak Frank, which separates three senses of
*bava*: a gate, door or entryway; a set or unit, as in the *bavei* of shofar
blasts (Rosh Hashanah 33b); and a part or section of a text, the opening,
middle and closing *bava* of a statement (Avodah Zarah 68b). On that reading
*Bava Kamma*, and the divisions of *Shaarei Teshuvah* and of *Chovos
HaLevavos*, have nothing to do with gates; a body of statements gathered under
one heading is called a *bava* or a *shaar* because that is simply one of the
word's meanings.

Against that, Rabbi Klein found a source that does tie the senses together.
Rabbi Shmuel Ibn Tibbon (*Peirush HaMilos HaZaros*) writes that statements on
one subject are called a *shaar* because they are like citizens of one city who
all use the same city gate, so any section of a tract or book is a *shaar*; the
word also carries the Aristotelian sense of a "category"; and on the same figure
the standard buying rate is a *shaar*, since every buyer is subject to it as
they all use the one gate. Ibn Tibbon says all this of *shaar* and does not
explicitly extend it to *bava*; that the same reasoning would cover *bava* is
Rabbi Klein's own suggestion, offered as "not farfetched."

**Whether the three masechtos are named for gates.** The Meiri and others record
that Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia, and Bava Basra were once one thirty-perek
masechta, Nezikin, split into three tens, as *Tosefta Keilim* and *Seder Olam*
were. (The Cambridge manuscript of the Mishnah calls the middle one *Bava
Tinyana*, "the second *bava*.")

- *Tosafos Yom Tov* (introduction to Seder Nezikin) reads the name as gates:
  the Mishnah is the gateway into the Torah's laws, the Oral Torah opening that
  body of knowledge to the learner.
- Others tie it to the judges at the city gate, which fits civil law but does
  not explain *Tosefta Keilim* or *Seder Olam*.

## Distinctions

### shaar (שער)
Root ש-ע-ר, about 375 occurrences: most often the breach in a city, citadel, or
Mikdash wall through which people enter, and in some cases any settlement. The
same root twice means to assess a quantity: Yitzchak sowed and found "*meah
shearim*," a hundredfold (Bereishis 26:12), and "as he has reckoned (*shaar*) in
his soul, so is he" (Mishlei 23:7). In the Mishnah *shaar* most commonly means
the gate, especially of the Mikdash or where the judges sat (Berachos 9:5, Yoma
1:3, Middos 1:1, 1:3-5, 1:7, and many more), and less commonly a price or
exchange rate (Maaser Sheini 4:1-2, Bava Metzia 4:12, 5:1) and, as a verb, to
appraise (Terumos 4:6, Chullin 7:4). Shadal joins the senses: the merchants who
gathered at the city gate appraised goods and so set the going rate. *Shiur*, an
amount (Kerisos 4:1), belongs with the quantitative sense, and the *shiur* one
delivers is so called, perhaps, because the teacher measures his words to his
subject.

### tera (תרע)
Biblical Aramaic (Daniel 2:49, 3:26; Ezra 7:24), and the Targumim's ordinary word
for *shaar*.

### bava (בבא)
Absent from Biblical Aramaic, and never used by the Targumim to render anything,
though it appears in Targum to Esther 5:14 in an addition to the text. In the
Gemara Rashi renders it *shaar* (Moed Katan 22a) or, far more often, *pesach*
(Shabbos 32a; Yoma 56a; Kesubos 62a; Gittin 68b), and sometimes *deles*, a door
(Eruvin 104a).

- Shadal derives it from the root ב-ב, continuing the approach of Rabbi Shlomo
  Pappenheim, who gives the one-letter root *beis* the sense "in, inside," the
  plain force of the letter as a prefix; to it he assigns *navuv*, hollowed
  through (Shemos 27:8), the Mishnaic *biv*, the pipe into which waste flows
  (Eruvin 8:10), and the family of *bo* and *biah*, coming in, hence *mavoy*,
  *tevuah*, and *navi*. Shadal adds *levav*, the heart, and *bavas ayin*, the
  pupil; Rabbi Klein suggests why: possibly because the heart sits deep inside
  the chest, and the pupil is a hole light enters through. Menachem Ibn Saruk
  had filed *bavas ayin* and *yevavah*, weeping, under either the one-letter
  root *beis* or the two-letter ב-ב, and Rabbi Jeremy Steinberg of the
  Veromemanu Foundation clarifies, on Rashi to Shoftim 5:28, that both belong to
  the eye, the pupil being part of it and weeping its most visible act. On
  Shadal's derivation, *bava* is presumably the opening one comes in through.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 17:8) reads *bavas* as the eyelid rather than the
  pupil (see Rashi to Bechoros 38b), the gate that opens and shuts the eye, and
  says outright that it is connected to the Aramaic *bava*.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: all three are "gate." In a Mishnah about the Mikdash, שער is "gate";
  in one about markets, "the going rate."
- Trap: English "gate" absorbs the textual sense of *bava*, and the result is
  nonsense. "The first *bava*" of a Mishnah is its opening clause; transliterate
  the masechta names and leave the etymology to a note.
- שערים as the divisions of a sefer (*Shaarei Teshuvah*) is conventionally "gates"
  in English titles; keep the convention, but where a text explains the name,
  follow Ibn Tibbon rather than inventing an architectural image.
- שיעור stays transliterated in both senses; "measure" and "lecture" lose the link.
- תרע needs no separate English; a Targum-based argument cannot distinguish
  *shaar* from *pesach*, since Targum renders both by it.
