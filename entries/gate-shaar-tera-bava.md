# Gate: shaar / tera / bava

## Bottom line

All three render **gate**: *shaar* in Hebrew, *tera* in Targumic Aramaic, *bava*
in the Aramaic of the Gemara. They are not synonyms within one language, so
nothing hangs on choosing between them; what a translator must not miss is that
each carries a second, non-architectural sense. *Shaar* in a Mishnah is often a
market **price**, and *bava* in a Gemara a **section** of a text, as in Bava
Kamma. Neither is a gate.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| שער | shaar | gate | Hebrew; also a settlement, a price, or the act of appraising (Shadal) |
| תרע | tera | gate | Targumic Aramaic; from "breaching," a gate seemingly being a break in the wall (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur, who suggests this) |
| בבא | bava | gate; section | Talmudic Aramaic only; also a set, or a section of a text |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why *tera* means gate.** The obvious answer is that it is *shaar* Aramaicized,
Hebrew *shin* becoming Aramaic *tav* with *ayin* and *reish* transposed. Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*) suggests a more convincing one: the Targumim use
*tera* to render Hebrew words for demolition, destruction, rupturing and
breaching (Vayikra 14:45; Devarim 12:3; Shmuel II 6:8; Yechezkel 22:30), so a
gate is seemingly nothing more than a break in the wall; that they also use it
for *pesach*, "opening" (Bereishis 18:1; Shemos 33:9; Shoftim 4:20), supports
this.

**Why a section of a sefer is called a *bava* or a *shaar*.** Rabbi Uriel Frank
of Mitzpe Yericho, founder of the Maane Lashon Society, says the truth is that
this has nothing to do with gates, and rests it on the dictionary of Talmudic
Aramaic of his father, Rabbi Yitzchak Frank, which separates three senses of
*bava*: a gate, door or entryway; a set or unit, as in the *bavei* of shofar
blasts (Rosh Hashanah 33b); and a part or section of a text, the opening,
middle and closing *bava* of a statement (Avodah Zarah 68b). On that reading
*Bava Kamma*, and the divisions of *Shaarei Teshuvah* and of *Chovos
HaLevavos*, have nothing to do with gates; a body of statements gathered under
one heading is called a *bava* or a *shaar* because that is simply one of the
word's meanings.

Against that the column sets a source that does tie the senses together. Rabbi
Shmuel Ibn Tibbon (*Peirush HaMilos HaZaros*) writes that statements on one
subject are called a *shaar* because they are like citizens of one city who all
use the same city gate, so any section of a tract or book is a *shaar*; the
word also carries the Aristotelian sense of a "category"; and on the same
figure the standard buying rate is a *shaar*, since every buyer is subject to it
as they all use the one gate. Ibn Tibbon says all this of *shaar* and does not
extend it to *bava*; that the same reasoning would cover *bava* is the
columnist's own suggestion, offered as "not farfetched." Follow Rabbi Yitzchak
Frank when translating, since "section" is what the word does there, and Ibn
Tibbon when a passage expounds the name.

**Whether the three masechtos are named for gates.** The Meiri and others record
that Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia, and Bava Basra were once one thirty-perek masechta,
Nezikin, split into three tens, as *Tosefta Keilim* and *Seder Olam* were. (The
Cambridge manuscript of the Mishnah calls the middle one *Bava Tinyana*, "the
second *bava*.") *Tosafos Yom Tov* (introduction to Seder Nezikin) reads the
name as gates: the Mishnah is the gateway into the Torah's laws, the Oral Torah
opening that body of knowledge to the learner. Others tie it to the judges at
the city gate, which fits civil law but not *Tosefta Keilim* or *Seder Olam*,
and the column notes that gap against it.

## Distinctions

### shaar (שער)
Root ש-ע-ר, about 375 occurrences: most often the breach in a city, citadel, or
Mikdash wall through which people enter, and by extension any settlement. The same
root twice means to assess a quantity: Yitzchak sowed and found "*meah shearim*,"
a hundredfold (Bereishis 26:12), and "as he has reckoned (*shaar*) in his soul, so
is he" (Mishlei 23:7). In the Mishnah *shaar* is overwhelmingly the gate,
especially of the Mikdash or where the judges sat (Berachos 9:5, Yoma 1:3, Middos
1:1, 1:3-5, 1:7, and many more), but also a price or exchange rate (Maaser
Sheini 4:1-2, Bava Metzia 4:12, 5:1)
and, as a verb, to appraise (Terumos 4:6, Chullin 7:4). Shadal joins the senses:
the merchants who gathered at the city gate appraised goods and so set the going
rate. *Shiur*, an amount (Kerisos 4:1), belongs with the quantitative sense, and
the *shiur* one delivers is so called, apparently, because the teacher measures
his words to his subject.

### tera (תרע)
Biblical Aramaic (Daniel 2:49, 3:26; Ezra 7:24), and the Targumim's ordinary word
for *shaar*.

### bava (בבא)
Absent from Biblical Aramaic, and never used by the Targumim to render anything,
though it appears in Targum to Esther 5:14 in an addition to the text. In the
Gemara Rashi renders it *shaar* (Moed Katan 22a) or, far more often, *pesach*
(Shabbos 32a; Yoma 56a; Kesubos 62a; Gittin 68b), and sometimes *deles*, a door
(Eruvin 104a). Shadal derives it from the root ב-ב, whose sense Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim gives as "in, inside," the plain force of the letter *beis* as a
prefix; to it he assigns *navuv*, hollowed through (Shemos 27:8), the Mishnaic
*biv*, the pipe into which waste flows (Eruvin 8:10), and the family of *bo* and
*biah*, coming in, hence *mavoy*, *tevuah*, and *navi*. Shadal adds *levav*, the
heart, possibly because it sits deep inside the chest, and *bavas ayin*, the
pupil, possibly because it is a hole light enters through; the column marks both
of those as its own reading of why. Menachem Ibn Saruk had filed *bavas ayin*
and *yevavah*, weeping, under either the one-letter root *beis* or the
two-letter ב-ב, and Rabbi Jeremy Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation
clarifies, on Rashi to Shoftim 5:28, that both belong to the eye, the pupil
being part of it and weeping its most visible act. So *bava* is presumably the
opening one comes in through. Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 17:8) reads *bavas* as the
eyelid rather than the pupil (see Rashi to Bechoros 38b), the gate that opens
and shuts the eye, and says outright that it is the Aramaic *bava*.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: all three are "gate." In a Mishnah about the Mikdash, שער is "gate";
  in one about markets, "the going rate."
- Trap: English "gate" absorbs the textual sense of *bava*, and the result is
  nonsense. "The first *bava*" of a Mishnah is its opening clause; transliterate
  the masechta names and leave the etymology to a note.
- שערים as the divisions of a sefer (*Shaarei Teshuvah*) is conventionally "gates"
  in English titles; keep the convention, but where a text explains the name,
  follow Ibn Tibbon rather than inventing an architectural image.
- שיעור stays transliterated in both senses; "measure" and "lecture" lose the link.
- תרע needs no separate English; a Targum-based argument cannot distinguish
  *shaar* from *pesach*, since Targum renders both by it.
