# Gate: shaar / tera / bava

## Bottom line

All three render **gate**: *shaar* in Hebrew, *tera* in Targumic Aramaic, *bava*
in the Aramaic of the Gemara. They are not synonyms within one language, so
nothing hangs on choosing between them; what a translator must not miss is that
each carries a second, non-architectural sense. *Shaar* in a Mishnah is often a
market **price**, and *bava* in a Gemara a **section** of a text, as in Bava
Kamma. Neither is a gate.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|

| תרע | tera | gate | Targumic Aramaic; from "breaching," a gate being a break in the wall (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why *tera* means gate.** The obvious answer is that it is *shaar* Aramaicized,
Hebrew *shin* becoming Aramaic *tav* with *ayin* and *reish* transposed. Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*) gives a better one: the Targumim use *tera* to
render Hebrew words for demolition and breaching (Vayikra 14:45; Devarim 12:3;
Shmuel II 6:8; Yechezkel 22:30), so a gate is a break in the wall; that they also
use it for *pesach*, "opening" (Bereishis 18:1; Shoftim 4:20), supports this.

**Why a section of a sefer is called a *bava* or a *shaar*.** Rabbi Yitzchak
Frank's dictionary of Talmudic Aramaic separates three senses of *bava*: a gate or
doorway; a set, as in the *bavei* of shofar blasts (Rosh Hashanah 33b); and a part
of a text, the opening, middle, and closing *bava* of a statement (Avodah Zarah
68b). On that reading *Bava Kamma* and the divisions of *Shaarei Teshuvah* have
nothing to do with gates. Rabbi Shmuel Ibn Tibbon (*Peirush HaMilos HaZaros*) does
connect them: statements on one subject are like citizens of one city who all use
the same gate, and on the same figure a market price is a *shaar*, since every
buyer passes through it. Follow Rabbi Yitzchak Frank when translating, since
"section" is what the word does there, and Ibn Tibbon when a passage expounds the
name.

**Whether the three masechtos are named for gates.** The Meiri and others record
that Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia, and Bava Basra were once one thirty-perek masechta,
Nezikin, split into three tens, as *Tosefta Keilim* and *Seder Olam* were.
*Tosafos Yom Tov* (introduction to Seder Nezikin) reads the name as gates: the
Mishnah is the gateway into the Torah's laws. Others tie it to the judges at the
city gate, which fits civil law but not *Tosefta Keilim*, and so tells against it.

## Distinctions

### shaar (שער)
Root ש-ע-ר, about 375 occurrences: most often the breach in a city, citadel, or
Mikdash wall through which people enter, and by extension any settlement. The same
root twice means to assess a quantity: Yitzchak sowed and found "*meah shearim*,"
a hundredfold (Bereishis 26:12), and "as he has reckoned (*shaar*) in his soul, so
is he" (Mishlei 23:7). In the Mishnah *shaar* is overwhelmingly the gate,
especially of the Mikdash or where the judges sat (Berachos 9:5, Yoma 1:3, Middos
1:1-7), but also a price or exchange rate (Maaser Sheini 4:1, Bava Metzia 5:1)
and, as a verb, to appraise (Terumos 4:6, Chullin 7:4). Shadal joins the senses:
the merchants who gathered at the city gate appraised goods and so set the going
rate. *Shiur*, an amount (Kerisos 4:1), belongs with the quantitative sense, and
the *shiur* one delivers is so called, apparently, because the teacher measures
his words to his subject.

### tera (תרע)
Biblical Aramaic (Daniel 2:49, 3:26; Ezra 7:24), and the Targumim's ordinary word
for *shaar*.

### bava (בבא)
Absent from Biblical Aramaic, and never used by the Targumim to render anything,
though it appears in Targum to Esther 5:14 in an addition to the text. In the
Gemara Rashi renders it *shaar* (Moed Katan 22a) or, far more often, *pesach*
(Shabbos 32a; Yoma 56a; Kesubos 62a; Gittin 68b), and sometimes *deles*, a door
(Eruvin 104a). Shadal derives it from the root ב-ב, whose sense Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim gives as "in, inside," the plain force of the letter *beis* as a
prefix; to it he assigns *navuv*, hollowed through (Shemos 27:8), the Mishnaic
*biv*, the pipe into which waste flows (Eruvin 8:10), and the family of *bo* and
*biah*, coming in, hence *mavoy*, *tevuah*, and *navi*. Shadal adds *levav*, the
heart set deep inside, and *bavas ayin*, the pupil, a hole light enters through;
Menachem Ibn Saruk had filed *bavas ayin* and *yevavah*, weeping, under the same
one-letter root. So *bava* is the opening one comes in through. Rav Hirsch (to
Tehillim 17:8) reads *bavas* as the eyelid rather than the pupil, the gate that
opens and shuts the eye, and says outright that it is the Aramaic *bava*.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: all three are "gate." In a Mishnah about the Mikdash, שער is "gate";
  in one about markets, "the going rate."
- Trap: English "gate" absorbs the textual sense of *bava*, and the result is
  nonsense. "The first *bava*" of a Mishnah is its opening clause; transliterate
  the masechta names and leave the etymology to a note.
- שערים as the divisions of a sefer (*Shaarei Teshuvah*) is conventionally "gates"
  in English titles; keep the convention, but where a text explains the name,
  follow Ibn Tibbon rather than inventing an architectural image.
- שיעור stays transliterated in both senses; "measure" and "lecture" lose the link.
- תרע needs no separate English; a Targum-based argument cannot distinguish
  *shaar* from *pesach*, since Targum renders both by it.
