# Trampling: romes / dorech / boss (remes)

## Bottom line

*Dorech* is neutral: **tread**, **step on**, and in a winepress or olive press
**press**. *Romes* is destructive: **trample**, treading meant to smash what lies under
the foot. *Boss* is contemptuous: **trample down**, treading on what had no business
being trodden on, so that the act itself says the thing is worth nothing. When the three
are close together, keep "tread" for *dorech* and do not spend "trample" on it; Tehillim
91:13 pairs *tidroch* and *tirmos* in one pasuk and the English has to hold them apart.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| רומס | romes | trample | destructive treading, walking so as to damage (Malbim); the full weight of the body (Rav Hirsch) |
| דורך | dorech | tread, step, press | neutral; kin to derech, a path; can be constructive, as in pressing olives or grapes (Malbim) |
| בוס | boss | trample down, crush underfoot | treading on what was not meant to be trodden on, an expression of contempt (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim); linked to boz, disgrace (Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi) |
| רמש | remes | creeping creature | the lightest tread, of small creatures that move over the ground with the whole body (Ramban; Rav Hirsch) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether ר-מ-ס and ר-מ-ש are one root.** Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (*Sefer HaGalui*) and Radak
(*Sefer HaShorashim*) treat them as one, samech and sin interchanging, without saying
how the senses meet. Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Ibn Parchon list them as two separate
roots. Ramban (to Bereishis 1:25) supplies the link the Kimchis left open: creeping
creatures tread the ground with their whole body, so treading is their essence. Rav
Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:21) accepts a connection but grades it: ר-מ-ס is trampling with
full force to smash, ר-מ-ש the light tread of small creatures that does no damage, and
ר-מ-ז, the root of *remez*, the eyelid meeting the lower lid in a wink, the same motion
at its faintest.

**What separates romes from dorech.** Malbim: *dorech* is neutral treading over a
threshold, *romes* treading that damages. So he reads Yeshayahu 1:12, that those who
came to the Mikdash for an experience without willingness to learn *yiras Hashem* were
trampling the courtyard, their presence emptying it of purpose. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi
Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) instead makes both words describe the ground: *derech* from
*rach*, soft ground that yields a path, *romes* from *ram*, ground raised enough that a
man must stomp it flat. The Bottom line follows Malbim, whose distinction bears on
translation; each of Tedeschi's readings sets aside a root letter.

**Whether boss is a distinct word.** Rashi equates *boss* with *romes* (to Tehillim
60:14; see also Tehillim 44:6, Yeshayahu 14:25, Mishlei 27:7). Pappenheim and Tedeschi
keep them apart, Pappenheim on what is trodden (something not destined to be trodden
on), Tedeschi on the attitude (*boss* as kin to *boz* and *bizayon*). The dissenters
agree in substance, so the Bottom line gives *boss* its own English, noting that one
following Rashi will simply write "trample." On its root: Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and
Radak give ב-ו-ס, Ibn Saruk and Pappenheim the biliteral ב-ס; nothing in rendering turns
on it.

## Distinctions

### romes (רומס)
Nineteen occurrences in Tanach, none in Chumash, all of treading or stomping with the
feet. It carries into Chazal: Kiddushin 66a, where Yannai is advised to *romsem* the
Torah scholars, which Rashi glosses as killing them, the word having gone all the way to
destruction. Yeshayahu 1:12, in the haftarah of Shabbos Chazon, is the locus classicus:
*remos chatzeirai*, the trampling of My courtyard.

### dorech (דורך)
From ד-ר-כ, whose derivatives run past seven hundred and fifty in Tanach, and which also
yields *derech*, a path; *dorech* is the footfall of one walking a route. Devarim 11:24
(cited again at Yehoshua 1:3), "wherever the sole of your foot treads," and Devarim
33:29, "you shall tread upon their high places," both use it for conquest, treading on
an enemy figuratively. Malbim's proof that it is neutral is that the same verb does the
presses: olives at Michah 6:15, grapes at Amos 9:13, Yeshayahu 16:10 and 63:2, Yirmiyahu
48:33 and Nechemiah 13:15.

### boss (בוס)
A little over ten occurrences: Yeshayahu 14:19, 14:25, 63:6, 63:18; Yirmiyahu 12:10;
Yechezkel 16:6, 16:22; Zechariah 10:5; Tehillim 44:6, 60:14, 105:14; Mishlei 27:7. (The
article's list prints "Ezek. 16:6, 16:22, 14:25," repeating Yeshayahu 14:25 among the
Yechezkel loci.) Pappenheim: *boss* is treading on what is not already destined to be
walked over, so the tread registers the walker's estimate of it. He builds two other words
on the root: *eivus*, a feeding trough, since an animal given more than it can eat ends by
standing on the leftovers; and Rabbinic *basis*, a base or pedestal (Shabbos 47a and
elsewhere), the bottom part with something weightier resting on it. Rabbi Yisroel Zev
Horowitz suggests the name of the *Yevusi*, who held Yerushalayim before the conquest, may
come from this root, they being conquerors who trampled their way through.

## Rendering guidance

- Tehillim 91:13 is the test case: *tidroch* on the lion and the snake, *tirmos* on the
  cub and the serpent. "You shall tread upon the lion and the adder; you shall trample
  the young lion and the serpent." Letting "trample" cover both loses the pasuk's
  escalation.
- The trap is English "trample," which absorbs all three. Reserve it for *romes*; give
  *dorech* "tread" or "step on," *boss* "trample down" or "crush underfoot."
- A *dorech* in a press is **press** or **tread out**, never "trample": Michah 6:15 and
  Amos 9:13 are constructive work, and that is Malbim's whole proof.
- *Remes* is a noun for the creature, not a verb: **creeping things**, with no trampling
  carried into it. Where a mefaresh equates *boss* with *romes* (Rashi does), follow him
  inside the quotation and keep the distinction for the running text.
