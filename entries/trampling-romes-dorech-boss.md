# Trampling: romes / dorech / boss (remes)

## Bottom line

*Dorech* is neutral: **tread**, **step on**, and in a winepress or olive press
**press** (Malbim). *Romes* is destructive: **trample**, walking in a damaging way
(Malbim), with the full force of the body so as to smash what lies under the foot
(Rav Hirsch). *Boss* is **trample down**: for Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, treading on
what is not necessarily destined to be trodden on, so that the act registers that
the walker does not think it important, and Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi expresses
a similar sentiment (*boss* as kin to *boz*, disgrace). But Rashi equates *boss* with
*romes*, and the column finds *boss* used in very much the same ways as the other
two. Tedeschi also reads *romes* and *dorech* by the ground rather than the act, and
Pappenheim takes *romes* as treading on what is meant to be walked on; the Bottom
line follows Malbim because his line bears on translation. When the words are close
together, keep "tread" for *dorech* and do not spend "trample" on it; Tehillim 91:13
has *tidroch* and *tirmos* in one pasuk, and the English has to hold them apart.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether ר-מ-ס and ר-מ-ש are one root.** All the classical lexicographers (Ibn Saruk,
Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon, Radak) trace *romes* to ר-מ-ס. Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (*Sefer
HaGalui*) and his son Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) explain that ר-מ-ס is related to
ר-מ-ש, samech and sin interchanging, but do not quite explain how the connection works
out thematically. It seems that Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Ibn Parchon do not agree,
since they list ר-מ-ש separately, while the Kimchis conflate the two. To understand the
connection the column turns to Ramban (to Bereishis 1:25), who explains that creeping
creatures use their entire body to tread over the ground, so their very essence relates
to treading; hence, the column says, *remes* is associated with *romes*. Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 1:21) also recognizes a connection but understands it differently: since
samech, sin and zayin can interchange, ר-מ-ס, ר-מ-ש and ר-מ-ז all refer to treading at
varying degrees of intensity. ר-מ-ס is trampling with the full force of the body to
destroy what lies beneath; ר-מ-ש a lighter form, the way short, lightweight bugs walk on
the ground without causing as much damage; and ר-מ-ז, the root of *remez*, which he takes
primarily as winking, the upper eyelid touching the lower as a foot treads the ground,
a much less intense and more subtle form of the action.

**What separates romes from dorech.** Malbim: *dorech* is a neutral term, treading or
walking over a threshold; *romes* implies walking in a destructive, damaging way. So he
reads Yeshayahu 1:12: people came to the Mikdash for a sort of religious experience but
were not open to learning how to fear Hashem, so they might as well have been trampling
the courtyard, their presence rendering it purposeless. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi
(*Otzar Nirdafim*) instead relates both words to the ground being walked on, each time
by ignoring one letter of the root: *derech* from *rach*, soft ground that a man treads
into a path for others, *romes* from *ram*, ground higher than usual, which needs more
force to stomp flat and fashion a path. The article leaves both standing; the Bottom
line follows Malbim because his distinction is the one a translation can carry, and
that choice is this guide's.

**Whether boss is a distinct word.** The column says *boss* is used in very much the
same ways as the other two, and Rashi, explaining Tehillim 60:14, equates *boss* with
*romes* (see also Rashi to Tehillim 44:6, Yeshayahu 14:25 and Mishlei 27:7).
Pappenheim draws a line: *romes* is treading on something meant to be walked on and
usually walked on, *boss* on something not necessarily already destined to be trodden
on, so that the act registers the walker's feeling that it is not important enough to
avoid stepping on. The column says Tedeschi expresses a similar sentiment, relating
*boss* to *boz*/*bizayon* by the interchange of samech and zayin: stepping on something
to show disgust with it and besmirch it. So the Bottom line gives *boss* its own English,
noting that one following Rashi will simply write "trample." On its root: the
triliteralists, such as Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and Radak, give ב-ו-ס; the biliteral Ibn
Saruk gives ב-ס, and Pappenheim, following the biliteralist approach after a fashion,
does the same. Nothing in rendering turns on it.

## Distinctions

### romes (רומס)
The article counts nineteen occurrences of the root in Tanach, none in Chumash, all of
trampling or stomping with the feet. It carries into later rabbinic literature:
Kiddushin 66a, where King Yannai, slighted by a rabbi who criticized him for taking the
roles of both king and Kohen Gadol, is advised by someone else to *romsem* the Torah
scholars, which Rashi explains as killing them. Yeshayahu 1:12, in the haftarah read the
Shabbos before Tisha B'Av, opens the article: *remos chatzeirai*, the trampling of My
courtyard. Pappenheim's definition, against *boss*: treading on what is meant to be
walked on and usually is.

### dorech (דורך)
From ד-ר-כ, whose derivatives appear over seven hundred and fifty times in Tanach, and
which also yields *derech*, a path; *dorech* is the footfall of one walking a trail.
Devarim 11:24 (cited again at Yehoshua 1:3), "wherever the palm of your foot will tread,"
and Devarim 33:29, "you will tread over their high places," both refer to conquering the
land and subduing enemies, figuratively stepping on them. Malbim adds that *dorech* can
even describe the constructive act of the presses: olives at Michah 6:15, grapes at Amos
9:13, Yeshayahu 16:10 and 63:2, Yirmiyahu 48:33 and Nechemiah 13:15.

### boss (בוס)
A little over ten occurrences in the article's list: Yeshayahu 14:19, 63:6, 63:18;
Yirmiyahu 12:10; Yechezkel 16:6, 16:22; Zechariah 10:5; Tehillim 44:6, 60:14; Mishlei
27:7; plus two slips. The list prints "Ezek. 16:6, 16:22, 14:25," and Yechezkel 14 has
no verse 25; the verse meant is Yeshayahu 14:25 (*al harai avusenu*), which it cites for
Rashi. It also prints "Ps. 105:14," a verse with no *boss*; Tehillim 108:14 repeats
60:14 (*vehu yavus tzareinu*) and seems to be meant. Pappenheim builds two other words on
the root ב-ס. *Eivus*, a feeding trough, its aleph extraneous: an owner fills it hoping
the animal will overeat and fatten, and the animal ends by stepping on the leftover food,
disregarding its importance (he contrasts people, who store leftovers for a later meal).
And Rabbinic *basis*, a base or pedestal (Shabbos 47a, 117a, 120b, 125b, 142a; Pesachim
83a; Avodah Zarah 8b; Chullin 125a), the bottom part on which the rest of the object
rests, like the ground that is trodden with something more significant on top of it. The
column nonetheless holds that the more convincing etymology for *basis* lies in the
Latin *basis* and the Greek βάσις, forebears of English *base* and *basis*. In Modern
Hebrew *basis* is an army base, a usage that may have been influenced by English *base*
but may also relate conceptually to *basis* as foundation. Rabbi Yisroel Zev Horowitz,
an acclaimed scholar of the geography of Eretz Yisrael, conjectures that the name of the
*Yevusi*, who held Yerushalayim before the conquest, may be related to this word: known
as strong conquerors who trampled their enemies to clear a path, their very name
possibly reflects such deeds.

Rabbi Klein writes that he used to connect Hebrew *boss* with American English *boss*,
the master who lords it over his subordinates as one who tramples a thing has conquered
it; but according to the *Oxford English Dictionary*, English *boss* comes from Dutch
*baas*, which originally meant "uncle." The Modern Hebrew slang *basa*, disappointment or
defeat, clearly derives from an Arabic cognate meaning "miserable," but he thinks it
quite possible that both derive from the earlier Hebrew *boss*, defeating shown by
trampling, while noting that he has found no source that explicitly makes the
connection.

## Rendering guidance

- Tehillim 91:13 is the test case: *tidroch* on the lion and the snake, *tirmos* on the
  cub and the serpent. "You shall tread upon the lion and the adder; you shall trample
  the young lion and the serpent." Letting "trample" cover both loses the difference
  Malbim draws between the verbs.
- The trap is English "trample," which absorbs all three. Reserve it for *romes*; give
  *dorech* "tread" or "step on," *boss* "trample down" or "crush underfoot."
- A *dorech* in a press is **press** or **tread out**, never "trample": Michah 6:15 and
  Amos 9:13 are constructive work, which is Malbim's point.
- *Remes* is a noun for the creature, not a verb: **creeping things**, with no trampling
  carried into it. Where a mefaresh equates *boss* with *romes* (Rashi does), follow him
  inside the quotation and keep the distinction for the running text.
- Do not carry English "boss" into Hebrew *boss*: the column, citing the OED, traces
  the English word to Dutch *baas* and gives up its own earlier guess at a link.
