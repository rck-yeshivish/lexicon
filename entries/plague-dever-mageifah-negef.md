# Plague: dever / mageifah / negef

## Bottom line

*Dever* is **pestilence**, a lethal contagious disease. *Mageifah* is the wider
word for a **plague** or **blow**, from a root that means striking, and by
Rashi's reading it does not by itself mean anything fatal. *Negef*, *mageifah*'s
close relative, is the exception: Malbim reads it as a plague that does kill,
and Rashi glosses it as *dever*. The one thing not to get wrong: *mageifah* is
not a milder *dever* and not a graver one. It names the striking; *dever* names
the disease.

## Distinctions

### dever (דבר)
The fifth *makkah*, the sudden death of Egypt's horses, donkeys, camels, cattle
and sheep, and the word Moshe uses twice in warning about the tenth (Shemos 5:3,
9:14). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim links *dever* to *devorah* ("bee"), because
*dever* raises lesions on the body before it kills, and those lesions resemble
the inflammation of a bee sting; the description matches the buboes of the
bubonic plague. Rabbi Yair Chaim Bachrach (*Chavos Yair*): *dever* is any deadly
sickness that is contagious and passes easily from person to person or family to
family. He grounds this in the Mishnah (*Taanis* 3:4), which calls for fasts
when there is *dever* in a city and defines it as three deaths in three days in
a city of five hundred adult men, a rate that can only mean an infectious
disease; and in the Gemara (*Bava Kama* 60b), which advises staying indoors when
there is *dever* in the city. On his reading, *dever* is the sudden death by
pestilence that leaves no visible wound on any one part of the body.

### mageifah (מגפה)
Root ג-פ-ה (the strong letters are gimmel and peh). Rashi (to Shemos 7:27):
*mageifah* does not denote death at all but striking, smiting, pushing; he says
the same to Shemos 21:22 and 21:35 and at *Bava Kama* 2b. This is how he answers
why *tzfardea* counts as a *mageifah* when frogs do not kill: the word never
required death in the first place. Abarbanel rejects that premise, holds that
*mageifah* is inherently lethal, and is therefore driven to Rabbeinu Chananel's
reading that *tzfardea* are crocodiles rather than frogs. On Rashi's line,
*mageifah* is the general term for any striking of an opponent, and describes
what the striker does rather than what the victim contracts. Rabbi Yair Chaim
Bachrach gives it a narrower sense: an infected wound that can spread like venom
through the limbs and the bloodstream, which fits the ג-פ-ה family of words for
striking one particular part of the body and letting the harm travel from there.

### negef (נגף)
The exception inside the ג-פ-ה family. Malbim: *negef* does denote a plague that
brings certain death. Rashi (to Shemos 30:12) defines *negef* as *dever*, and
Rabbi Menachem ibn Saruk runs the definition the other way, defining *dever* as
*negef*. So the two words are close to interchangeable in the sources, and
neither is the neutral "striking" that *mageifah* is for Rashi.

### The rejected view
Rabbi Yair Chaim Bachrach cites an anonymous sage who held that *dever* and
*mageifah* name the same kind of fatal plague, with *mageifah* the deadlier of
the two, adducing the twenty-four thousand who fell in the *mageifah* after Baal
Peor (Bamidbar 25:9). He refutes it: the plague following Dovid HaMelech's
census killed seventy thousand and is called a *dever* (Shmuel II 24:15), so the
difference between the words is not one of scale.

## Rendering guidance

- דבר: "pestilence." Keep it, even where "plague" reads more naturally; it is
  the word that carries contagion and certain death.
- מגפה: "plague" in a narrative, "blow" or "striking" where Rashi's sense is
  the point (Shemos 21:22, a blow to a pregnant woman, is not a plague at all).
- נגף: "plague" too, but if *negef* and *mageifah* stand together, render
  *negef* "deadly plague" and *mageifah* "affliction" so Malbim's distinction
  survives.
- Trap: English "plague" absorbs all three. Shemos 9:14 puts *mageifos* and
  *dever* in one pasuk ("all My plagues... stricken you with *dever*"); render
  the first "plagues" and the second "pestilence," or the pasuk collapses into
  a tautology.
- Trap: do not translate *mageifah* as though it reports a death toll. It
  reports what the striker did; the toll, if any, is elsewhere in the pasuk.
