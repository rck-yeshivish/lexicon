# Mercy: rachum / chanun / chus / chemlah

## Bottom line

*Rachum* (*rachamim*) is **mercy**, **compassion**; in Rabbi Klein's own
analysis, the savior is moved by the sufferer's plight and acts. *Chanun* (*chein*) is **grace**, **favor**: the
sufferer finds favor and is given a free gift he need not deserve (Rashi:
*matnas chinam*). *Chus* is **sparing**, refusing to let something be lost
because one values it; *chemlah* is **pity** that will not see a thing ruined
for its own worth, and it can reach the one who cannot ask (Malbim, Wertheimer).
So for Rabbi Klein *rachum* looks at the giver's heart, *chanun* at the
receiver's standing; other commentators divide the two differently.

## Distinctions

### rachum (רחום), rachamim (רחמים)
Both *rachum* and *chanun* are of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy that Hashem
revealed to Moshe as he prayed for the Jewish People after the golden calf,
attributes that are key to how Hashem runs the world and to how a man attains
atonement; although one generally refrains from using adjectives to describe
Hashem, these are presented as such.
- Rabbeinu Bachaya (on Shemos) writes that *rachum* and *chanun* are Hashem's
  way of forgiving sinners who ask forgiveness, repent and undergo some
  affliction, but he does not there explain how the two differ; elsewhere he
  writes that they are actually one and the same attribute, His heeding prayer
  in time of need even before that person has repented, and finds support in the
  fact that Tanach often uses them together, sometimes in one order and
  sometimes in the other.
- Rabbi Klein's own analysis, by way of an analogy from the relationship between
  a victim and one with the power to save him: *rachum* focuses on the savior,
  who sees the victim's downtrodden state, empathizes with him, and ultimately
  decides to save; he acts not necessarily for the victim's sake but, in a
  sense, for his own.
- Bechor-Shor: *rachum* is for the poor, mercy that lifts one out of a dismal
  situation.
- Gloss to Tosafos (Rosh Hashanah 17b): *rachamim* is Hashem withholding a
  calamity before it falls.
- Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms: *rachum* acts beyond the letter of the law
  (others explain the opposite: *rachum* only for one who asks for mercy).
- Rabbeinu Bachaya again: *rachamim* is Hashem's general oversight of the world.
- Rabbi Avraham bar Chaim Ibn Ramoch, who lived in Spain at the beginning of the
  Spanish Inquisition (to Tehillim 86:16, 112:4, 145:8): *rachum* saves from any
  form of suffering.
- Malbim: *rachmanus* applies only to an intelligent being; one pities a child
  or an animal differently from an adult.
- Rabbi Klein, setting *rachamim* against *din*: under *rachamim* the focus is
  on the Giver, who gives an influx of His good without asking whether the
  receiver deserves it, while under *din* the focus is on the receiver and the
  flow is measured by what he deserves. Tradition ties the four-letter Name to
  *rachamim* and *Elokim* to *din*, and in the shofar the one straight, steady
  *tekiah* represents *rachamim*, against the broken *teruah* of *din*.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 13:2) defines the primary sense of ר-ח-ם as motherly
  protection, joining *rechem*, where a mother protects her unborn child, to
  *rachamim*, the dominant trait of a protective mother. Rabbi Yitzchak Avineri
  writes that one root can carry both love and mercy because love is the root of
  mercy: one only has mercy on those whom he loves.

### chanun (חנון), chein (חן)
- Rabbi Klein: *chanun* focuses on the victim, who finds favor (*chein*) in the
  savior's eyes (with Hashem, this may come through prayer) or is simply granted
  (*choneh*) a reprieve.
- Rashi (to Devarim 3:23): *chanun* recalls *matnas chinam*, a free gift, relief
  not necessarily deserved.
- Bechor-Shor: *chanun* is for the rich, a present regardless of need.
- Gloss to Tosafos: *chanun* is a special reprieve granted in the midst of
  distress.
- Rokeach: *chanun* uses the system of justice to heed another's call for
  clemency (others explain the opposite: *chanun* is even for one who does not
  request mercy).
- Rabbeinu Bachaya: *chanun* is His particular oversight of each element of
  creation.
- Ibn Ramoch: *chanun* grants intellectual gifts by which a man helps himself.

### chus (חוס) and chemlah (חמלה)
Neither is among the Thirteen Attributes. Malbim: both can be felt toward any
entity, not only an intelligent one (English calls this "caring"); *chus* is refusing
to let a thing be destroyed because one would lose the benefit one has from it
(the *chas* of *chas v'shalom*). *Chemlah* is refusing to let a thing be ruined
because of an innate quality in it, a beauty or a completeness one does not
want to see spoiled. Wertheimer: *chemlah* can extend to pitying one who cannot
ask for help, a child or one without the faculties to ask.

## Rendering guidance

- רחום / רחמים: "merciful," "mercy," "compassion"; רחמנות as a middah:
  "compassion" (or transliterate *rachmanus* in a mussar text).
- חנון / חן: "gracious," "grace," "favor"; חנינה: "pardon," "clemency."
- חוס / חס: "spare," "have regard for" (Yonah 4:10, *chasta* on the kikayon, reads
  "you took pity on," but the sense is sparing a loss); חמלה: "pity,"
  "compassion for" in the sense of not letting a thing be spoiled.
- Trap: English "mercy" and "pity" each absorb three of these. In the
  Thirteen Attributes and wherever רחום וחנון stand together, keep "merciful
  and gracious"; where חמלה or חוס joins them, use "pity" or "spare" so the
  third word is not a repeat.
- Together, order and attribution follow the pasuk (Rabbeinu Bachaya notes
  both orders occur); do not reorder for climax as one does with *bushah* and
  *klimah*.
