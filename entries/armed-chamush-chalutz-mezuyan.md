# Armed: chamush / chalutz / mezuyan

## Bottom line

All three mean **armed**, and the Midrashim equate them outright: *Bamidbar
Rabbah* 13:20 says *chamushim* means *chalutzim*, while *Mechilta d'Rabbi
Yishmael* to Shemos 13:18 and *Shemos Rabbah* 20:19 say it means *mezuyanim*.
*Chamush* is the Chumash's word (Shemos 13:18), tied by the grammarians to the
*chomesh*, the part of the body where a weapon is typically girded, and by the
derashos to *chamesh*, five.
*Chalutz* is, on Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's reading, the **vanguard**, the men
drawn out to go at the head of the army. *Mezuyan* is post-biblical, from Aramaic *zayin*, a weapon.
Targum renders *chamush* in all four places by *zariz*, ready at a moment's
notice, and often renders *chalutz* the same way; Onkelos gives *vayarek* of
Avraham by *zariz* too. At Shemos 13:18 "armed" and "a fifth of them" are
both live readings of one word.

## Distinctions

### chamush (חמוש)
Once in Chumash (Shemos 13:18) and three times after: the men of Gad and Reuven
expected to lead the conquest (Yehoshua 1:14) and doing so (Yehoshua 4:12), and
Gidon's war with Midyan (Shoftim 7:11).

- Ramban reads the peshat at Shemos 13:18 as armed, since Yisrael feared attack
  by the Pelishtim or others on the way.
- The Mechilta and Rashi read *chamushim* from *chamesh*, that only one in five
  (or one in fifty, or one in five hundred) left Mitzrayim.

(In Modern Hebrew the association with armaments went on developing, so that
*tachmoshes* is ammunition.)

Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon and Radak take the root as ח-מ-ש,
which also yields *chamesh*, *chomesh*, *chamishah*, *chamishim*. The noun
*chomesh*, typically translated "lower abdomen," occurs four times, all in
Shmuel (Shmuel II 2:23, 3:27, 4:6, 20:10), of men killed by a stab there;
Sanhedrin 49a places it by the fifth rib, where the gallbladder and liver hang,
so that a blow there kills.

- Ibn Janach, Rabbi Yehudah Ibn Balaam, Ibn Parchon, Ibn Ezra and Radak derive
  the adjective from that noun: an armed man typically wears his weapon girded
  at the *chomesh*.
- Ibn Saruk derives it the other way, the noun from the adjective.

The Yerushalmi (*Shabbos* 6:4) ties it to *chamesh* instead: the Mishnah there
lists five weapons a man might bear into a public domain on Shabbos, a sword, a
bow, a shield, a club and a spear, and *chamushim* means Yisrael left armed with
those five. (The standard editions read "fifteen"; the reading "five" is
preferred by *Korban HaEidah* there, *Torah Temimah* to Shemos 13:18, and Rabbi
Chaim Kanievsky, and appears to stand in *Mechilta d'Rashbi* and the Yemenite
*Midrash HaBeiur*; see also Baal HaTurim there.)

Several further routes to the etymology have been offered, all of them as
conjecture.
- Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein conjectures that *chamush* relates to five in the sense
  of a military formation divided in five parts, van, body, rear and two flanks.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds his own suggestion: the most basic weapon a man
  has is his own fist, which has five fingers, so a word for one bearing a
  weapon might well be tied to five.
- The German philologist Wilhelm Gesenius (1786 to 1842) sees Hebrew *chamush*
  as related to the Arabic *hamas*, to be eager or manly in battle, since a
  Hebrew shin often becomes a sin or samech sound in Arabic. David Curwin at the
  Balashon Blog (19 October 2023) clarifies that the name of the Arab terror
  organization Hamas is technically an acronym for the Arabic term for "The
  Islamic Resistance Movement," but was almost certainly influenced by the
  Arabic *hamas* as well.

Targum renders *chamush* in all four places by a cognate of *zariz* (encourage,
arouse, strengthen, embolden), which is to say that armed men are prepared and
ready for a fight at a moment's notice.

- The related verb *chimesh* (Bereishis 41:34, where Yosef advises Pharaoh) is
  read two ways: Targum Onkelos, Rashi and Ibn Parchon by *zariz*, that Pharaoh
  should spur his subjects to store.
- Rashbam, Ibn Ezra, *Daas Zekeinim*, Radak and others by *chamesh*, that he
  impose a fifth as tax (also implied in Ibn Saruk's *Machberes Menachem*).
- Ibn Janach reads it as a third thing, related to the Arabic *chamus*, hidden,
  a cognate of the Hebrew *kamus*.

### chalutz (חלוץ)
Seventeen occurrences in the military sense, mostly Bamidbar (31:5, 32:21,
32:27, 32:29-30, 32:32) and Yehoshua (6:7, 6:9, 6:13), also Divrei HaYamim
(I 12:24-25; II 17:18, 20:21, 28:14) and Yeshayahu 15:4. Moshe stipulated with
Gad and Reuven that their soldiers go *chalutzim* (Bamidbar 32:30, 32:32;
Devarim 3:18). Rashi (to Bamidbar 21:3) simply defines *chalutzim* as
*mezuyanim*.

The root ח-ל-ץ yields removing something from its place, slipping off a garment,
rescuing from danger, and *chalatzim*, the thighs, loins or hips; it also gives
the given name Cheletz, one of David's warriors (Shmuel II 23:26; Divrei HaYamim
I 11:27, 27:10) and another man of that name (Divrei HaYamim I 2:39).
- As with *chamush*, Ibn Balaam, Ibn Ezra (to Bamidbar 31:3) and Ibn Parchon
  derive the adjective from the body part: weapons are often fastened at the
  loins.
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) accepts that and adds the removal sense: fighting
  men are figuratively removed from the rest of the nation by being designated
  as soldiers.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim narrows it: *chalutzim* are the units extracted from
  the formation to serve at the front, ahead of the army.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein proposes a further possibility of his own, that
  *chalutzim* are those who have drawn their weapon from its holder or sheath,
  or are ready to do so at a moment's notice, while noting that he has found no
  philologist who says so explicitly.
- Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) derives it from shedding a garment, seemingly
  because men ready for battle often shed some of their civilian clothing.
- The Targumim again often render it by *zariz*. In the Torah journal
  *Aspaklaria*, Rabbi Elimelech Bleiberg and Rabbi Itamar Habshush debated
  whether the Modern Hebrew *chalutz*, a pioneer, grows out of that Targumic tie
  between *chalutz* and *zariz*, or is a Modern Hebrew coinage.

### The difference between chamush and chalutz
- Malbim (to Yehoshua 4:12) notes the overlap and separates them: *chamushim*
  are soldiers arrayed in formations of fifty, hence the tie to *chamesh*, while
  *chalutzim* are men slipped off and separated from the rest of the nation.
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim* 22) accepts Malbim on
  *chalutzim* but explains *chamushim* by the grammarians' route, men armed at
  the *chomesh*.

If both words are named for a body part, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein observes, they
differ only in which one.

### mezuyan (מזוין)
Not in Tanach and not in the Mishnah, but common in Gemara: *listim mezuyanin*,
armed robbers (Kiddushin 11a; Bava Kamma 57a-b, 80a, 114a; Bava Metzia 22a,
29b, 43a, 58a, 93b, 95a; Bava Basra 92b; Temurah 15b; Yerushalmi *Shekalim*
2:1, *Shevuos* 8:1). It comes from the Aramaic noun *zayin*, a weapon, which is
Targum's standard rendering of Hebrew *neshek* and comparable terms; the
letter zayin itself orthographically resembles a weapon, perhaps a
spear. The ordinary Gemara term is
*klei zayin* (Eruvin 45a, Nazir 59a, Sanhedrin 82a, Makkos 10a, Avodah Zarah
15b-16a, 25b; Yerushalmi *Peah* 1:1, *Pesachim* 4:3, *Avodah Zarah* 1:6, 2:1).
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) glosses *zayin* by German *waffen*
(a cognate of English *weapon*) and Latin *arma* (a cognate of English *arms*).
*Avos d'Rabbi Nosson* 29 uses *mezuyan* and "has a *zayin* in
his hand" interchangeably: one who has midrash without halachah is a strong man
unarmed, halachah without midrash a weak man armed, and both together an armed
warrior. *Yalkut Shimoni* to Devarim 534, having said that a bachelor should see
to it that he marries, gives a parable for a wife's protectiveness: do not
settle without a wife, rather make a holder for your *zayin*. (In Modern Hebrew
slang *zayin* took on a rude sense, which scholars like David Assaf and Yisrael
Bartel see as a bowdlerized form of the Hebrew *zanav*, tail, used
insultingly.)

The biblical cousin is *azeinecha*, a weapon (Devarim 23:14).
- Ibn Saruk takes the root as א-ז-ן.
- Radak holds the initial alef is not radical and the root is ז-ו-ן, which
  brings *azen* very close to *zayin*.
- Ibn Janach goes both ways.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces both to the biliteral ז-ן, provision and the
  meeting of needs: a weapon is what equips a fighter to withstand an assault.

(*Sefer HaTishbi* also cites some who explain *susim meyuzanim* at Yirmiyahu 5:8
as armed horses.)

### vayarek (ויירק)
Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (*Ohel Moed*) lists a fourth term beside *chalutz* and
*chamush*: *vayarek*, Avraham mustering his three hundred eighteen men to
rescue Lot (Bereishis 14:14). Targum Onkelos renders it by *zariz*, exactly as
it does the other two. It is a verb where they are adjectives, but it belongs
to the group. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ר-ק, emptying:
drawing a sword empties its sheath, and readiness to do so is a fighter's edge.

*Vayikra Rabbah* 25:4 gives the counterweight: Avraham's victories came "not
through a *zayin* and not through a shield (*magen*), but through tefillah and
supplication." Tehillim 91, said against every sort of danger and especially
for soldiers at war, contains no letter zayin, which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein reads as an
allusion to the idea that one who can draw on the power of tefillah has no need
of physical weapons.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 13:18: render "armed" with Ramban and the peshat, and footnote the
  Mechilta's "one in five." Do not try to carry both in the English; a hedge
  like "arrayed in fifties" satisfies neither reading.
- Trap: English "armed" absorbs all three, and "arms" absorbs *zayin*, *neshek*
  and *klei zayin*. Where the words stand together (the Midrashim equating
  *chamushim* with *chalutzim* and with *mezuyanim*), that flattening is the
  point and is fine; where a source distinguishes them (Malbim to Yehoshua
  4:12), give *chalutzim* "the vanguard" or "the men drawn out" and keep
  "armed" for *chamushim*.
- *Chalutzim* of Bamidbar 32 is "as a vanguard" or "in the lead, armed," not
  "pioneers"; whether the modern sense grows out of the biblical one is itself
  disputed (the *Aspaklaria* exchange above), so it does not belong in a
  translation of the pasuk.
- *Chomesh* in Shmuel is the "lower abdomen" or "the fifth rib," with Sanhedrin
  49a; do not render it "belly," which loses the anatomical precision the Gemara
  is supplying.
- Bereishis 41:34, *v'chimesh*: pick one, "he shall take a fifth" (Rashbam, Ibn
  Ezra, Radak) or "he shall organize" (Onkelos, Rashi), and name the other in
  a note. The two readings are not compatible in one English clause.
- *Zayin* stays transliterated where the source is playing on the letter-name
  (the *Avos d'Rabbi Nosson* passage, the absence of zayin from Tehillim 91);
  elsewhere it is "weapon."
