# Return: shuv (teshuvah) / chazarah

## Bottom line

*Shuv* is returning **to** a place or condition: **return**, and *teshuvah* is
**repentance** precisely because it is a return.
*Chazarah* is the about-face itself, the act of **turning back** or **reverting**
from where one now stands. *Shuv* names the destination, *chazarah* names the
U-turn. *Chazarah* is Mishnaic; it never appears in Tanach.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| שוב | shuv | return | return to a prior or natural state (Pappenheim); names the destination, not the U-turn |
| תשובה | teshuvah | repentance | repentance as return to man's natural righteousness, even if never there before (Rabbi Klein) |
| חזרה | chazarah | turning back, reverting | the U-turn from where one now stands; Mishnaic, never in Tanach |

## Distinctions

On Shabbos Shuvah we read Hoshea's words in the haftarah,
"Return O Yisrael until Hashem your God" (Hoshea 14:2). *Shuv* / *shuva* is the
basis of *teshuvah*, and cognates of *shuva* are used throughout Tanach for
repentance (for example Devarim 30:2). But there is a second Hebrew word for
returning, *chazar*, and a lapsed Jew who has come back to observance is
colloquially one who *chazar b'teshuvah*, a phrase built from two different
verbs both meaning to return. How, then, do *shuv* and *chazar* differ?

### shuv, teshuvah (שוב, תשובה)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814): the two-letter root ש-ב denotes
returning to a prior place or situation. He groups with it *hashavah*, returning a lost object
(Devarim 22:1) or stolen goods (Vayikra 5:23), since both address something
earlier in time and set it right; *lehashiv*, "to answer," because the answer
fills the hole the question opened; and *shev*/*yeshivah*, "dwelling," because
settling in a place creates a home base one always comes back to.

- Pappenheim adds a second sense that carries the whole idea of *teshuvah*: ש-ב
  can mean reverting to one's **natural** state even where that was never one's
  prior state. So *Shabbos* is a return to man's natural condition of rest even
  for someone who has never rested; so too *lasheves*, to sit, and *shevi* /
  *shevuyah*, a captive whose freedom of movement has been curtailed by his
  captors, as if stuck in a position of rest.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg also traces *Shabbos*, together with *shavua*,
  week, to ש-ב, returning, along a different path: as a *shevuyah*, a captive,
  is stuck within a closed system that repeats itself and constantly returns to
  the beginning, so *Shabbos* and *shavua* are circular periods of time that
  restart when they reach seven.

Similarly, Avraham's servant, charged with finding a wife for Yitzchak in
Avraham's homeland Charan,
could ask what to do if the woman would not agree to leave, "Should I return
(*hehashev ashiv*) your son to the land from which you left?" (Bereishis 24:5).
Although Yitzchak had never been in Charan, he could still return there, since
it was his father's homeland and so, by extension, could be considered his
natural place.

Rabbi Klein grounds this in Yechezkel 33:18-19 (see also Yechezkel 18:21-27)
read with Berachos 29a. The Gemara explains the righteous man who "returns"
from his righteousness as one who had been a sinner, repented, and then returned
to sinning, an actual prior state. It does not explain the wicked man who
"returns" from his wickedness as one who was first righteous, then became evil,
and then returned to his former righteousness. How, then, can the wicked man be
called a returnee if he had never been righteous to begin with? The answer is
that righteousness is man's natural state and sinfulness unnatural: *teshuvah*
brings a man back to his innate righteousness, so the wicked man who repents is
returning to that natural state. The converse does not hold. When a righteous
man recants his righteousness and becomes a sinner, he cannot justifiably be
said to be returning to anything, unless, as the Gemara explains, he had
previously been a sinner.

This is Rabbi Klein's own account of what *teshuvah* means. *Teshuvah*
denotes a man's journey to his natural sin-free state; he is returning to where
he is supposed to be, whether or not he was ever there before, returning to his
spiritual default position, his home base. It is the quest for
peace and tranquility, the return to one's roots. Moreover, the process of
*teshuvah*, in which one harnesses the power of his regret over past sins as
fuel for personal growth, enables him to revisit the past and recast his former
sins as merits: doing *teshuvah*, one goes back to his previous deeds to make
amends, in the same way that *hashavah* and *lehashiv* fill holes previously
created by thievery or by a question.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Vayikra 16:4, 23:24; Bamidbar 36:4)
reads *yovel* as a word of return. It means carrying, specifically carrying a
thing back to its original place, as in the Yovel year fields that were sold go
back to the families that are their rightful owners. He ties it to Yom Kippur in
two ways: the Yovel is proclaimed on Yom Kippur, for land and slaves to go back
to their former state, and it alludes to *teshuvah*, by which the sinner goes
back to his pristine, innocent state. So too produce is *yevul*, the field's way
of returning to the farmer many times over what he put into it.

### chazarah (חזרה)
Part of the *teshuvah* process entails making an about-face
and figuratively turning oneself around in a circle to correct course, and the
Hebrew word for that is *chazarah*. Neither *chazarah* nor its verbs
*chazar*/*chozer* occur anywhere in Tanach; they are Rabbinic (Mishnaic) Hebrew,
and they mean variously to regret, recant, retract, repeat, return, or repent.
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549), known in Latin as Elias Levita, notes that
the standard way the Targumim render Hebrew *sivuv*, "go around" or
"circumnavigate," is with the Aramaic/Syriac *chazar* and its cognates. Rabbi
Klein's own summary of the pair: *shuva* implies focusing on the goal of
getting back to where one used to be, while *chazarah* focuses on the act of
getting out of the rut one is currently stuck in and making a U-turn.

The closest word to *chazarah* in Tanach is *chazir*, "pig," whose root is also
ח-ז-ר.

- Rabbi Menachem Ricanati (to Vayikra 11:2): the pig is called *chazir* because
  in the future Hashem will "return" to us the right to eat it, our relationship
  with the pig thus reversing course, after a fashion. (Rabbeinu Bachya and
  Rabbi David Ibn Zimra write that this should not be taken literally.)
- Ibn Kaspi also points out that the root of *chazarah* appears in Tanach only
  in the word *chazir*, which he explains gets that name because pigs commonly
  turn around backwards to walk aimlessly in circles.

## Rendering guidance

- *Teshuvah*: **repentance**, and where the drashah turns on
  the root, gloss it "the return." Do not render it "atonement"; that is
  *kapparah* and belongs to a different cluster.
- *Shuv* in a pasuk: **return** or **turn back**. Where the pasuk is about
  reverting to sin (Yechezkel 33:18), "turns back" carries the sense.
- *Chazarah* in Rabbinic Hebrew: **going back**, **reverting**, **retracting**,
  or **repeating**, whichever the context wants; *chozer bo* is "retracts."
- Trap: English "return" will absorb both words. When a passage uses them
  together (the colloquial *chazar b'teshuvah*), do not write "returned in
  return." Render the pair by its two motions: he turned his life around and
  came back.
- Do not use *chazarah* to explain a Biblical verse's word choice; the word is
  not Biblical.
