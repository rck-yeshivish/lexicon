# Sin: chet / avon / pesha (aveirah)

## Bottom line

*Chet* is the general and lightest word: **sin**, and beside its neighbors the
**inadvertent sin** (*shogeg*). *Avon* is **iniquity**, the wanton or intentional
sin (*meizid*). *Pesha* is the **rebellious sin**, done to defy Hashem. That is
the Gemara's ladder (Yoma 36b), and the *vidui* keeps its ascending order: "I
have sinned, I have done iniquity, I have rebelled." Rabbi Netanel Weil: the
difference between *chet* and *avon* holds only when the two stand together; a lone *chet* or *avon* covers
every kind of sin and is simply "sin." *Aveirah*, the Rabbinic catch-all, is
**transgression**.

## The Kohen Gadol's vidui

In the Yom Kippur confessionals we mimic the vidui of the Kohen Gadol in the
Beis HaMikdash. The Mishnah (*Yoma* 4:2) relates that
when the Kohen Gadol confessed his own sins and his household's he admitted
specifically to three kinds: *chet*, *avon* and *pesha*. These are not
synonyms but different degrees of sin, and the Gemara (Yoma
36b) explains *chet* as an inadvertent sin, the state of mind called *shogeg*,
*avon* as a wanton or intentional sin, *meizid*, and *pesha* as a sin of
rebellion. There are, however, other ways of explaining the differences.

## Distinctions

### chet (חטא)
Root ח-ט-א, whose plain sense is to miss, to lack. Near the end of David
HaMelech's life, when his oldest surviving son Adoniyahu began proclaiming
himself king, Batsheva came before David demanding that he keep his promise that
Shlomo would succeed him, saying that if Adoniyahu secures the throne "then I
and my son Shlomo will be *chataim*" (Melachim I 1:21).
- Rashi explains *chet* there as "lacking": Batsheva and Shlomo would be lacking
  the royal titles due them.
- Probably based on Rashi's comment, the Vilna Gaon (Mishlei 1:10, 13:6) writes
  that a *chet* is a sin through a lacking, that is, the failure to perform a
  positive mitzvah.
- Rabbi Yechezkel Landau (1713-1793) also takes *chet* as the lack of fulfilling
  a positive mitzvah, but synthesizes it with the Gemara's inadvertent sin by
  making it specifically the failure to fulfill the mitzvah of repenting after
  one has sinned inadvertently.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935-2017) defends the classic Rabbinic definition of
  *chet* as an inadvertent sin, and still draws a lesson from Rashi's "lack": a
  *chet* is not simply the lack of something but the failure to achieve a goal,
  the sinner deviating from the goal of mankind and missing his intended
  objective.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814), similarly: *chet* denotes the
  sinner's lack of intention or mindfulness when committing his sin.
- Malbim, whose approach is slightly different, holds that all three words
  *could* refer to an offense committed on purpose, differing in motive: a
  *chet* is a sin committed because a person was swayed by his physical
  temptations and purposely indulged in what he knew to be wrong.
- *Peirush HaRokeach*: inadvertent, as the Gemara has it.

### avon (עון)
Root ע-ו-ה, crooked.
- Gemara: the wanton or intentional sin, *meizid*.
- Pappenheim: the sin of one who "thinks too much," wrongly concluding that
  sinning is the proper way to go and acting accordingly.
- Malbim: a sin a person commits because his intellect had been negatively
  persuaded, causing him to stray.
- *Peirush HaRokeach*, which slightly disagrees with the Gemara's way of
  dividing the three: *avon* is specifically a sin from which one derived
  physical pleasure or gained some other benefit.

### pesha (פשע)
Root פ-ש-ע.

- Gemara: the sin of rebellion.
- Pappenheim: the sinner knows his forbidden actions are completely wrong and
  should not be done, and carries them out anyway in order to rebel against
  Hashem.
- Malbim: the iniquities of one who shamelessly sins as a way of rebelling
  against Hashem.
- *Peirush HaRokeach*: simply the willful sin (having given *avon* the sense of
  benefit).

In another account, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim asserts that rebellion is not the
primary meaning of *pesha* at all: it primarily denotes negligence that comes
about through laziness. The *poshea* does not try to rebel against or anger the
one he sins against; his indolence shows that he does not care about the
repercussions of his actions, as the term *poshea* is used of a watchman who did
not live up to the vigilance expected of him (see Shemos 22:8; Bava Kamma 21b,
29a). Before a king, and especially before the King of kings, a higher degree
of mindfulness is expected, and laziness and apathy slight the king's honor; it
is in this sense that *pesha* was borrowed to mean rebellion.

### aveirah (עבירה)
A fourth term, in Rabbinic sources but not in Tanach. It literally means
"transgression" or "violation," and although it once referred specifically to
crimes of indecency it now serves colloquially as a general term for every kind
of wrongdoing.

- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785-1865): *aveirah* comes from the root
  ע-ב-ר, which means "passes" in a physical sense, the idea being that a man
  morally passes over his own thoughts so as not to focus on the nefariousness
  of his deeds.
- Rabbi Klein suggests that one who commits an *aveirah* has crossed a Rabbinic
  red line, noting that the Gemara (Shabbos 40a) holds that even a person who
  violates a Rabbinic prohibition can be called an *avaryan*.

## How sharp are the categories?

Rabbi Netanel Weil (1687-1769) writes that the differences between *chet* and
*avon* are apparent only when the two terms stand juxtaposed to each other. In
such a context *chet* means whatever *chet* means and *avon* whatever *avon*
means; but when either appears on its own, without the other, it includes all
types of sin and not only the specific type it otherwise names.

Nor, Rabbi Klein says, is the classifying black and white. In *Hilchos Teshuvah*
the Rambam codifies the penitent's obligation to confess by saying *chatati,
aviti, pashati*, the same formula the Kohen Gadol said in the Beis HaMikdash.
Rabbi Yosef Babad (1801-1874) in *Minchas Chinuch* writes that one need not
necessarily make all three declarations, but should confess whatever sins are
relevant in the situation. Rabbi Elazar Menachem Mann Shach (1899-2001) disagrees
and holds that the Rambam's wording implies one should always recite all three,
for two reasons: because the formula instituted for the vidui includes all three
types of sin, and because no sin is so clear-cut as to fit fully into one of the
three categories, every sin carrying elements of all of them. A man may have
sinned inadvertently and the sin still carry elements of wantonness and
rebelliousness, or he may have sinned rebelliously and the sin still carry traces
of inadvertency or of pure wantonness.

## Rendering guidance

- The triple, in the *vidui* or in a pasuk: "sin, iniquity, and rebellious
  sin" (or as verbs, "I have sinned, I have done iniquity, I have rebelled").
  Keep the ascending order; never collapse two of them into "sin."
- Standalone: *chet* is "sin"; *avon* is "sin" too (Weil), and "iniquity" only
  where the text sets it against *chet*; *pesha* alone is "rebellious sin" or
  "willful transgression," so the defiance stays audible.
- *Chet* is "inadvertent," not "accidental" or "a mistake": the *vidui*
  confesses it, and Rabbi Moshe Shapiro reads it as a goal missed.
- Trap: "sin" absorbs all three, and "transgression" is ambiguous between
  *pesha* and *aveirah*. Reserve "transgression" for *aveirah*; give *pesha*
  the word "rebellious."
- Malbim and Peirush HaRokeach re-sort the three (by motive; by benefit). If a
  derashah turns on their reading, follow it and say so; otherwise the Gemara's
  *shogeg* / *meizid* / *mered* ladder is the default.
