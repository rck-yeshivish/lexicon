# Stranger: zar / nachri (chiloni, hedyot)

## Bottom line

*Zar* and *nachri* both mean "stranger," but they measure different distances. A
*zar* is estranged **in one respect**, and is often a member of the same nation
or household: render him **outsider** or **stranger**. A *nachri* is a
**foreigner** in every respect, from another nation entirely. Malbim states the
rule plainly: the *nachri* is more unfamiliar than the *zar*. In Kodashim
contexts *zar* is a technical term, the **non-Kohen**, and should be rendered
that way, never "stranger." *Chiloni* is the Targum's Aramaic for *zar* and the
Midrash's word for a non-Kohen; *hedyot* is a Greek loanword for a **commoner**
or **layman**, the ordinary man against the leader, the amateur against the
craftsman, the *Kohen hedyot* against the Kohen Gadol.

## Distinctions

### zar (זר)
Ibn Ezra (long commentary to Shemos 21:2): *zar* implies someone of the
speaker's own nation but a different tribe within it. The Vilna Gaon (to Mishlei
27:2) reads the pasuk the same way. Malbim: a *zar* is from the familiar
locality but counts as strange with respect to some particular matter, strange
in one way only.

Malbim's own illustrations carry the point. A non-Kohen is a *zar* toward
terumah and the korbanos he may not eat (Vayikra 22:10, 22:12-13), yet he is
plainly no total stranger. A man other than a married woman's husband is a *zar*
to her (Devarim 25:5, Yechezkel 16:32) because she is forbidden to him, and no
more than that. Chazal derive *mamzer* from *zar* (Yevamos 76b), which fits: a
*mamzer* is a full Jew, foreign only as regards marrying into unblemished
lineage.

The same reasoning explains a Gemara. Tehillim 81:10 pairs *el zar* with *el
nechar*, yet Shabbos 105b declines to read the first clause as literal idolatry
and applies *el zar* to the yetzer hara. Since from a Jewish perspective no
"other god" could be anything but wholly foreign, the word *zar* would have no
place; the yetzer hara, on the other hand, is intimate (it lives inside a man)
and estranged (it stands against everything the Torah is) at once.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *zar* to the two-letter ז-ר, "estrangement,
disconnection": the stranger is cut off from the society he finds himself in.

### nachri (נכרי)
Ibn Ezra: from an entirely different nation. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer
notes that *nachri* is several times set against a relative (Bereishis 31:15,
Devarim 23:21). Malbim: a complete foreigner, from another land and another
people, strange in many ways at once.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: the root נ-כ-ר bears opposite senses,
"recognizing" (*makir*, *lehakir*) and "not recognizing" (*nachri*), and a verb
of the same root means to deny or repudiate (Devarim 32:27, Iyov 21:29). This is
why the Mechilta (to Shemos 12:43) defines *ben-neichar* as any heretic who
denies Hashem, Jew or not.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim holds that *nachri* and *zar* denote the same degree of
estrangement and differ only in what their roots fix on. *Nachri* is tied to
*lehakir*, recognition: meeting a *nachri*, one has no ready mental picture of
him, and so feels the pull to find out who he is. *Zar* fixes on his
disconnection from the society around him. One and the same man can be called
both, depending which aspect is in view.

Mishlei (2:16, 5:20, 7:5) likens foreign wisdoms to a *zarah* and a *nachriah*.
*Sefer Chassidim* 619: the *nachriah* is a non-Jewish woman, the *zarah* a
Jewish one. Malbim reads the two as two kinds of wisdom: the *zarah* is a
discipline outside Torah that a man may nonetheless press into Torah's service,
as he could marry that woman were the procedure right; the *nachriah* is a
wisdom that can never enhance Torah, as a Jew may never marry a non-Jewish
woman, its heresies staying irreconcilable.

### chiloni (חילוני)
The Targumim render Hebrew *zar* into Aramaic as *chiloni* (Onkelos to Shemos
29:33, 30:33), and *nachri* as *nochrae* (Targum to Devarim 17:15, Shmuel II
15:19, Mishlei 27:2); the Targum to Tehillim (81:10, 137:4) reverses the pair.
The Midrash (Vayikra Rabbah 24:7) uses *chiloni* for a non-Kohen: a Kohen Gadol
meets a *chiloni* on the road who asks to walk with him, and is told that a
Kohen keeps to ritually pure paths and does not pass through cemeteries; join me
at that standard or I walk alone. In Modern Hebrew *chiloni* is a secular Jew,
estranged in exactly this sense, failing to live at the standard of the rest of
the nation.

### hedyot (הדיוט)
Frequent in the Mishnah in three senses: a commoner against the political or
spiritual leadership (Nedarim 5:5, Sanhedrin 7:10, Horayos 3:2-3); an ordinary
non-professional against a skilled craftsman (Moed Katan 1:8, Bava Metzia 4:4,
Keilim 26:1); and as a modifier, *Kohen hedyot*, the ordinary Kohen against the
Kohen Gadol (Yoma 7:5, Yevamos 2:4, Sotah 8:3). A Greek loanword, from the word
that also gave English "idiot," but the Hebrew carries none of that sense.

## Rendering guidance

- *Zar* in Kodashim or terumah: **non-Kohen**, always. Elsewhere: **stranger**
  or **outsider**, and where the estrangement is partial, "one who is a
  stranger to it."
- *Nachri*: **foreigner** or **gentile** according to context; *ben-neichar*,
  **an alien** (Mechilta: a denier).
- *Hedyot*: **commoner**, **layman**, or **ordinary** as a modifier. Never
  "idiot."
- *Chiloni* in the Targum or Midrash: whatever *zar* was rendered in that
  passage. In a modern context, **secular**.
- Trap: English "stranger" absorbs both *zar* and *nachri*, and the pesukim that
  use them together are exactly the pesukim where that ruins the sense. Mishlei
  27:2, "let a *zar* praise you and not your own mouth, a *nachri* and not your
  own lips," is climactic: render it "an outsider... a stranger from elsewhere,"
  keeping the widening distance. The same holds at Yeshayahu 28:21, 61:5,
  Ovadiah 1:11, and Tehillim 81:10.
- Trap: do not import "gentile" into *zar*. The *zar* is normally a Jew.
