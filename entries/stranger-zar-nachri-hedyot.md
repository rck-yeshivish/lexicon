# Stranger: zar / nachri (chiloni, hedyot)

## Bottom line

*Zar* and *nachri* both mean "stranger," and it seems from most of the
commentators that they measure different distances. A *zar* is estranged **in
one respect**, and is often a member of the same nation or household: an
**outsider**, a **stranger**. A *nachri* is a complete **foreigner**, strange in
many ways, from another nation entirely. Malbim puts the rule in a line: the *nachri* is
*more* unfamiliar than the *zar*. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim dissents, holding the
two equally estranged and differing only in what their roots fix on. In Kodashim
contexts *zar* is a technical term, the **non-Kohen**. *Chiloni* is often the
Targum's Aramaic for *zar*, and the Midrash's word for a non-Kohen; *hedyot* is a Greek
loanword for a **commoner** or **layman**, the ordinary man against the leader,
the amateur against the craftsman, the *Kohen hedyot* against the Kohen Gadol.

## Distinctions

### zar (זר)
Ibn Ezra (long commentary to Shemos 21:2): *zar* implies someone of the
speaker's own nation but a different tribe within it. The same understanding can
be gleaned from the Vilna Gaon's comments to Mishlei 27:2. Malbim: a *zar* is
from the familiar locality but counts as strange with respect to some particular
behavior or habit, strange in one way only, while the *nachri* is strange in
many ways at once. (Rabbi Klein adds an aside: he used to
think English *bizarre* was related to *zar*, but the *Oxford English
Dictionary* feels otherwise.)

Malbim's own illustrations carry the point. A non-Kohen is a *zar* toward
terumah and the korbanos he may not eat (Vayikra 22:10, 22:12-13), yet he is
plainly no total stranger. A man other than a married woman's husband is a *zar*
to her (Devarim 25:5, Yechezkel 16:32) because she is forbidden to him, even if
he is not a complete stranger. *Mamzer* is said to be related to *zar* (Yevamos
76b), which, Rabbi Klein notes, makes sense given Malbim's understanding: a *mamzer* is a full Jew, foreign
only as regards marrying into unblemished lineage. He is therefore termed a
*zar* and not a *nachri*.

Rabbi Klein applies the same reasoning to a Gemara. Tehillim 81:10 pairs *el zar* with
*el nechar*, yet Shabbos 105b seemingly declines to read the first clause in its
literal sense as outlawing foreign gods, and applies *el zar* to the yetzer
hara. The forced explanation presumably stems from the word *zar*: from a Jewish
perspective no "other god" could be anything but wholly foreign, so *zar* would
have no place. The yetzer hara, on the other hand, is intimate (it rests within
a person) and foreign (it goes against everything the Torah stands for) at once.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814) traces *zar* to the
two-letter ז-ר, "estrangement, disconnection": the stranger is cut off from the
society he finds himself in.

### nachri (נכרי)
Ibn Ezra: from an entirely different nation. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer
(1866 to 1935) points out that *nachri* is in several places used to mean the
opposite of a relative (Bereishis 31:15, Devarim 23:21). Malbim: a complete
foreigner, from another land and another people, strange in many ways at once.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865): the root נ-כ-ר bears opposite
senses, "recognizing" (*makir*, *lehakir*) and "not recognizing" (*nachri*), and
a verb of the same root means to deny or repudiate (Devarim 32:27, Iyov 21:29).
This is why the Mechilta (to Shemos 12:43) defines *ben-neichar* as any heretic
who denies Hashem, Jew or not.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim holds that *nachri* and *zar* denote the same degree of
estrangement and differ only in what their roots fix on. *Nachri* is tied to
*lehakir*, recognition: meeting a *nachri*, one has no ready mental picture of
him, and so feels the pull to find out who he is. *Zar* fixes on his
disconnection from the society around him. One and the same man can be called
both, depending which aspect is in view.

Mishlei (2:16, 5:20, 7:5) likens foreign wisdoms to a *zarah* and a *nachriah*.
*Sefer Chassidim* 619: the *nachriah* is a non-Jewish woman, the *zarah* a
Jewish one. Malbim reads the two as two kinds of wisdom: the *zarah* is a
discipline outside Torah that a man may nonetheless press into Torah's service,
as he could marry that woman were the procedure right; the *nachriah* is a
wisdom that can never enhance Torah, as a Jew may never marry a non-Jewish
woman, its heresies staying irreconcilable.

### chiloni (חילוני)
The Targumim often render Hebrew *zar* into Aramaic as *chiloni* (Onkelos to
Shemos 29:33, 30:33; *Peirush Yonatan* to Targum Yonatan to Bereishis 42:7), and
*nachri* as *nochrae* (Targum to Devarim 17:15, Shmuel II 15:19, Mishlei 27:2);
as an aside, the Targum to Tehillim (81:10, 137:4) reverses the trend. Either
way *chiloni* denotes foreignness or strangeness just as *zar* and *nachri* do
(see *Yair* to Devarim 25:5 and *Me'at Tzari* to Bamidbar 1:51).

The Midrash (Vayikra Rabbah 24:7) uses *chiloni* the way Scripture uses *zar*,
of a non-Kohen. The Midrash explains that the Torah commands all Jews to "be
holy" (Vayikra 19:2) because if they want to walk with Hashem they must live up
to His standards, and illustrates it with a parable: a Kohen Gadol
meets a *chiloni* on the road who asks to walk with him, and is told that a
Kohen keeps to ritually pure paths and does not pass through cemeteries; join me
at that standard or I walk alone. In Modern Hebrew *chiloni* is a secular Jew,
likewise estranged, failing to live at the standard of the rest of the nation.

### hedyot (הדיוט)
Frequent in the Mishnah in three senses: a commoner against the political or
spiritual leadership (Nedarim 5:5, Sanhedrin 7:10, Horayos 3:2-3); an ordinary
non-professional against a skilled craftsman (Moed Katan 1:8, Bava Metzia 4:4,
Keilim 26:1); and as a modifier, *Kohen hedyot*, the ordinary Kohen against the
Kohen Gadol (Yoma 7:5, Yevamos 2:4, Sotah 8:3). A Greek loanword, from the word
that also gave English "idiot."

## Rendering guidance

- *Zar* in Kodashim or terumah: **non-Kohen**, always. Elsewhere: **stranger**
  or **outsider**, and where the estrangement is partial, "one who is a
  stranger to it."
- *Nachri*: **foreigner** or **gentile** according to context; *ben-neichar*,
  **an alien** (Mechilta: a denier).
- *Hedyot*: **commoner**, **layman**, or **ordinary** as a modifier. Never
  "idiot."
- *Chiloni* in the Targum or Midrash: whatever *zar* was rendered in that
  passage. In a modern context, **secular**.
- Trap: English "stranger" absorbs both *zar* and *nachri*, and the pesukim that
  use them together are exactly the pesukim where that ruins the sense. Mishlei
  27:2, "let a *zar* praise you and not your own mouth, a *nachri* and not your
  own lips," is climactic: render it "an outsider... a stranger from elsewhere,"
  keeping the widening distance. The same holds at Yeshayahu 28:21, 61:5,
  Ovadiah 1:11, and Tehillim 81:10.
- Trap: do not import "gentile" into *zar*. The *zar* is normally a Jew.
