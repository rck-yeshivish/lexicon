# Teaching: hora'ah / limmud / alef (and hadrachah)

## Bottom line

For Shadal, cited by Malbim, *hora'ah* is **instruction** given once in the
expectation that the pupil will retain it, and *limmud* is **teaching by
drilling**, repeating a lesson until it is engraved in the pupil's heart; in
Mishnaic Hebrew *hora'ah* narrows to **halachic ruling**. *Alef* is read two
ways: for Malbim, teaching that happens **inadvertently**, a person or thing
serving as the example from which a lesson is drawn; for Rabbi Pappenheim, the
learner's **firm grasp** of material won through repeated instruction.
*Hadrachah*, Malbim's contrast term, is **guidance** or counsel in applying
what one already knows, not necessarily new information. *Hora'ah* and
*limmud* are stages, not synonyms (Shadal and Rav Hirsch, as Rabbi Klein pairs
them), and Shadal reads a whole tragedy in Tanach off the difference.

## Distinctions

### hora'ah (הוראה)
Ibn Janach and Radak: from the root yud-reish-hey, which yields three meanings,
throwing (*yarah vayam*, Shemos 15:4), teaching (*yoreh dei'ah*, Yeshayahu
28:9), and rain (*yoreh* and *moreh*, Devarim 11:14; Yoel 2:23). Menachem Ibn
Saruk traces these to the one-letter root reish and adds a fourth sense, spittle
or bodily discharge (*rir*, *rar*: Vayikra 15:3; Shmuel I 21:14; Iyov 6:6). None
of the grammarians intimates a thematic connection among the four; Rabbi Klein
proposes his own, unidirectional movement (like a *ray* in English): throwing
away from oneself, rain and spittle only downward, and teaching from teacher to
pupil and not back, as in a lecture where the teacher "throws" information and
hopes the student catches it.

In Mishnaic Hebrew *hora'ah* is specifically the ability and responsibility to
render halachic decisions (*Avos* 4:7 and 6:6; *Horayos* 1:1 and 1:4; *Parah*
7:9), and masseches *Horayos* treats the consequences of mistaken rulings by the
Sanhedrin and other batei din. Rabbi Klein sees his unidirectional theme here
too, jurisprudence flowing down from the authority to the less knowledgeable.
Modern Hebrew keeps the neutral sense of teaching or instructing, without the
halachic connotation.

Radak adds that *Torah* itself (and Aramaic *Orayta*) is from yud-reish-hey;
on that account, Rabbi Klein notes, *Torah* might best be rendered "a teaching"
or "instruction." Radak also weighs Ibn Janach's alternative, the root
tav-vav-reish, investigation or scouting (which, Rabbi Klein remarks, would not
account for the Aramaic cognate), from which come system and order, and the word
*tor*, one's turn within a fixed arrangement; on that reading *Torah* is the law
laid out as a systematic code. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 26:5; Devarim 4:1) takes a
third root, hey-reish-hey, to impregnate: the Torah is, as it were, the seeds of
goodness and morality, from which a person instilled with its values can grow
into a paragon of virtue.

### limmud (למוד)
The column opens with a count: words from lamed-mem-dalet appear eighty-six
times in Tanach, but in the Chumash only in Devarim, and most of those in
Va'eschanan.

Malbim, in the name of Shadal: *limmud* is going over the lesson again and again
until it is engraved on the pupil's heart and he knows it well, where *hora'ah*
is teaching a thing once and expecting it to be retained. Shadal reads Melachim
II 12:3 accordingly: Yoash did right in Hashem's eyes all the years Yehoyada was
alive, as Yehoyada taught him (*horahu*), and once Yehoyada died he no longer
kept those teachings, and even ended up deifying himself. The wording is a
criticism of the method. Yehoyada used *hora'ah* and not *limmud*, never
repeating the essential lessons to the boy-king; had he reinforced them again
and again, they would have become second nature, and Yoash would not have
strayed once his teacher was gone.

Rav Hirsch makes a similar point from Moshe's own usage: in Devarim, Moshe
describes his teaching of Klal Yisrael with cognates of *limmud*, not with
*Torah*, because he did not suffice with planting the seeds but sought to see
them to fruition. Rabbi Klein pairs the two: both Shadal and Rav Hirsch
understand *hora'ah* as one step in the learning process and *limmud* as
bringing it to its conclusion. The early grammarians note that the *malmad
habakar*, the ox-goad of Shoftim 3:31, used in training cattle to plow, is from
the same root.

### alef (אלף)
Iyov 33:33 and 35:11; Mishlei 22:25. Malbim: this is teaching done
inadvertently, where a person or thing serves as a prototype from which a lesson
can be derived, though the teaching is not done actively or even purposely. In
Rabbinic Hebrew, deriving such a lesson is *yalfusa* (verb *yalif*), usually
inferring an idea from a pasuk or a derashah; Modern Hebrew *ulpan* comes from
the same root.

Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) gives the root as aleph-lamed-peh,
with the aleph sometimes dropping so that lamed-peh serves as well; on that
basis he and Radak suggest that *talpiyos* (Shir HaShirim 4:4, its only
occurrence) belongs to this root. The word's meaning there is obscure; Chazal
read *talpiyos* as *tel* and *piyos*, the mound to which all mouths direct their
prayers, that is, the Beis HaMikdash (Berachos 30a). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
traces *alef* to the two-letter element lamed-peh, a strong hold hard to break
free of: hence *yalefes*, a boil leaving a scar that can never be removed
(Vayikra 21:20); *aluf*, a chieftain ruling from strength; and *elef*, a
thousand, the number of constituents a warlord needs to claim the title. The
lamed-peh-tav verbs of grasping (Shoftim 16:29; Rus 3:8) and *lefes*, the
turnip of Rabbinic Hebrew, firmly entrenched in the ground, carry the same
theme. On this account the learning called *alef* is the learner's firm grasp
of material won by repeated instruction. Like Menachem and Radak, Rabbi
Pappenheim connects *talpiyos* to *alef*, but unlike them he explains the
connection: a tower for instructing young soldiers in the art of war, a sort of
shooting range. Malbim explains *aluf* differently, as a powerful leader who
must teach his constituents how to act. (In Modern Hebrew *aluf* is a sporting
champion and the rank of Major-General.)

*Alef* also names the ox (Devarim 7:13, 28:4, 28:18, 28:51; Tehillim 8:8, 50:10;
Mishlei 14:4; Yeshayahu 30:24). Malbim: a *trained* ox, one that has learned to
work the land. The Vilna Gaon reads *alafim* in Mishlei 14:4 as students,
drawing on *alef*'s sense of teaching, so that "for want of oxen the trough is
an empty pit" warns the teacher that without students he will become an empty
vessel; with students to answer for, a teacher fills himself with knowledge.
About this Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi said, "I learned much from my teachers, more from
my colleagues, and from my students most of all" (Makkos 10a); Taanis 7a has
the same saying from Rabbi Chanina.

### hadrachah (הדרכה)
Malbim's contrast term. The common thread of *hora'ah* and *limmud* is the
imparting of new knowledge (and, Rabbi Klein adds, ostensibly of *alef*). The
*madrich*, a guide, counselor or coach, gives *hadrachah*, counsel that guides
the student in the practical application of what he already knows, laying out a
*derech*, a path, for him to follow; he is not necessarily providing new
information as such.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "teach" will absorb *hora'ah*, *limmud*, and *alef*. Where a
  passage sets two of them against each other, use "instruct" for *hora'ah*,
  "drill" or "teach thoroughly" for *limmud*, and "train" or "school" for
  *alef* (the last fits Rabbi Pappenheim better than Malbim; a judgment call).
- *Hora'ah* in a Mishnaic or halachic context is "ruling," not "teaching."
- Where a mussar passage cites Melachim II 12:3, keep the verb visible: "as
  Yehoyada instructed him," so that the contrast with drilling survives in
  English.
- *Hadrachah* is "guidance" or "counsel," not "instruction," which would pull it
  back into *hora'ah*.
- *Alef* as a noun in a pasuk is an ox; as a verb it is to train or teach. In an
  unpointed text let the context decide, and note that the Vilna Gaon's derashah
  on Mishlei 14:4 turns on reading the ox-word in its teaching sense.
