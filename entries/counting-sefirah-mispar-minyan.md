# Counting: sefirah (sofer, mispar) / moneh (minyan)

## Bottom line

On Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg's reading, *sofer* / *sefirah* is a count where
each unit is marked and matters in itself, and *mispar* is a number that sets a
limit. *Moneh* / *minyan* is a count whose point is the total; the single unit is
not necessarily significant on its own. *Sefirah* is **counting**,
with qualitative weight where the sense is the daily obligation; *moneh* is to
**count** or **tally**, and *minyan* a **tally** or, in the shul sense, a
**quorum**. The Torah's choice of *sofer* for *Sefiras HaOmer* is deliberate,
and it differs from the *moneh* of the Kohen Gadol's sprinklings.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which word is general and which is specific.** The Vilna Gaon: *minyan* is the
count in general, *mispar* the particular number arrived at. Rabbi Wertheimer
runs it the other way, bringing evidence that *mispar* is the general number and
*mifkad* the word for a specific count. Rabbi Klein offers all of this with the
caveat that these are possible ideas and none of them hard and fast answers.

**Whether mispar and minyan are distinguished at all.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
of Breslau ties *mispar* to *sippur*, a story: the parts of a story follow one
another in their own order, and so do the numbers of a count, one not saying
random numbers any more than one tells a story out of unrelated incidents. Even
so, as Rabbi Klein notes, this approach ostensibly does not account for how
*mispar* differs from *minyan*, which is the question at hand.

## Distinctions

### sofer, sefirah, mispar (ספר, ספירה, מספר)
Root ס-פ-ר. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: *mispar* is a number in its limiting
function. The Torah caps flogging at a *mispar* (Devarim 25:2), which the
mesorah gives as thirty-nine; the Aramaic *sfar* is a nation's border, which
limits its territory, and the Aramaic *sapar* is a barber, who limits the growth
of the hair. On this reading the Torah says *tisperu* for the Omer (Vayikra
23:15-16) because that counting is itself bounded, running only until the morrow
of the seventh week. Mecklenburg suggests a second dimension: *sefirah* is not the
bare recitation of numbers but a count in which each unit calls for something
extra, a count of quality as well as quantity. The goal of a *sefirah* count is
not just the raw numbers but also improving oneself, cleansing oneself of impurities; and
he connects *sefirah* to *sapir*, the sapphire, since the counting cleanses a
person as a precious gem is free of impurities.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro explains that all words from ס-פ-ר are interrelated and
refer to unifying disparate parts: *sippur* joins many details into a single
narrative; a *sfar*, a border, confines everything within it and makes it one
unit; a *sefer* binds all the contents recorded in it into one entity; and
*mispar* and *sofer*, number and counting, are the system in which the numbers
are all bound together in an organized and logical way.

The name *Sefiras HaOmer* comes from the count's joining
the annual barley offering brought on the second day of Pesach, the *Korban
Omer*, to the annual wheat offering of Shavuos. And halachically each day of the
Omer may be reckoned a separate mitzvah, so that one who misses a whole day
continues counting, though without a berachah. Many have used the forty-nine
days for a forty-nine step scheme of character development, or for the ways of
acquiring Torah listed in the sixth perek of Avos, or for a forty-nine daf
masechta such as *Sotah* or *Shevuos*. On that model the point of the counting
is not necessarily only the destination of Shavuos but the days themselves: if a man works
each day at becoming better, then not only the final count has significance but
every single day. That is exactly what a *moneh* count is not.

### moneh, minyan (מונה, מנין)
Root מ-נ-ה.

- Mecklenburg derives *moneh* from *maneh*, a respectable portion, so that what
  enters a *moneh* count is by definition worth counting.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the two-letter root מ-ן as creating order out of
  causes and effects, and heads one group of its derivatives with *moneh* and
  *minyan*, since counting seeks to create mathematical order by quantifying a
  set of givens; *min*, a species, derives from the same root, since it too
  determines what belongs in one grouping and what is excluded.

In a *moneh* count the final number is the only requirement: the ten men needed
for the parts of davening that call for a quorum, or the seven sprinklings the
Kohen Gadol makes toward the Kodesh HaKodashim, which the Yom Kippur liturgy
describes with *moneh* and not *sofer*. No individual man and no individual
sprinkling necessarily carries weight on its own.

### mifkad (מפקד)
Root פ-ק-ד. A third family for counting and numbering, *poked*/*mifkad*.
Malbim holds that *pakad* denotes remembering something in order to take some
action; so understood, it primarily means remembering something in order to
attend to it, and the most basic way of attending to a thing is to determine
whether it is actually present, hence *pakad* means both "to count" (Bamidbar
1:21, 2:32; Shmuel I 15:4) and "to be absent" (Bamidbar 31:49; Yeshayahu 38:10).

## Rendering guidance

- *Sefiras HaOmer*: "the counting of the Omer," and where the source is making
  the point, say what kind of count it is rather than reaching for a heavier
  English word. The weight is in the mitzvah, not in the verb.
- Trap: English "count" absorbs both *sofer* and *moneh*. Where a passage sets
  them against each other, as the Yom Kippur *moneh* stands against the Omer's
  *sofer*, mark the difference in the surrounding words: "tallied" for *moneh*,
  "counted off" or "counted each day" for *sofer*.
- מספר as a limit is easy to miss. In Devarim 25:2 "a number of lashes" reads
  as vagueness in English, where the Hebrew means a fixed cap; "a set number"
  or "a limited number" carries the sense.
- מנין in a halachic context is "quorum"; in a plain counting context it is
  "tally" or "the count." Do not import "quorum" into a pasuk.
- Where the two roots stand in one phrase, keep the qualitative word in the
  *sefirah* slot, since that is where each unit has its own standing.
