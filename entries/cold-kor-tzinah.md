# Cold: kor / tzinah

## Bottom line

*Tzinah* is ordinary coldness, the kind a body is built to withstand: **cold,
chill**. *Kor* is the severe kind, the cold that hurts: **bitter cold,
freezing** (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim). Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg draws the line
differently: *kor* is the drop in temperature, *tzinah* is a cold that
surrounds and envelops a person like snow. Both words are rare in Tanach.

## Distinctions

### kor (קור)
Cognates appear three times in Tanach: the annual season called *kor*
(Bereishis 8:22), and *mayim karim*, cold water, twice (Yirmiyahu 18:14,
Mishlei 25:25). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *kor* and *kar* to the biliteral
ק-ר, a strong impact from concentrated mass or speed. Coldness fits because the
body holds itself warm, so meeting something cold is a collision of opposed
temperatures. The same root gives *mikreh* and *keri*, occurrence, the sudden
collision of a person with new circumstances; and *kir* (wall), *korah* (beam)
and *tikrah* (ceiling), all of them points where weight bears down.

On the scale: Pappenheim holds *kor* is the extreme, felt only on the coldest
winter days, and it also describes a solid gone so cold that touching it hurts,
cold metal or marble. Tanach names the winter season *kor* not because the
season regularly reaches that pitch but because that is when real *kor* is
likeliest.

### tzinah (צנה)
In this sense the word seems to appear only once: "like the *tzinas sheleg* on a
harvest day, so is a trusty messenger to those who send him" (Mishlei 25:13).
- Rashi: the longing in harvest heat for the cold of the snow season.
- Rabbi Yosef Kimchi: a cool winter breeze blowing on a summer day.
- Ibn Ezra and Rabbi Yishaya of Trani: the point of the mashal is that a touch
  of icy cold on a summer day gives the same relief as being able to rely on
  one's agent.

The word's other sense is "shield," and the two are linked in both directions.
- Rabbi Moshe Kimchi (in the Mishlei commentary mistakenly attributed to Ibn
  Ezra) suggests that cold is called *tzinah* because it shields from the summer
  heat.
- Abarbanel (Amos 4:2) runs it the reverse way, that a shield guards from the
  heat of battle.
- *Shoresh Yesha* links both to *tzanua* and *tzenius*: as the modest person
  keeps to himself, winter is the season of withdrawing indoors, and a shield
  likewise covers and conceals.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *tzinah* to the biliteral צ-נ, whose core sense
is a thorn. The *tzinah* shield is the spiked kind, used to strike as well as
to guard; *tzinah* as cold is the stinging bite of frost, like the prick of a
thorn. Rav Hirsch (Shemos 16:33) similarly: *tzinah* is primarily a *painful*
cold. On the scale, though, Pappenheim puts *tzinah* at the bottom: the plain
coldness that typically describes a solid kept from any heat source, or a liquid
that simply feels cold, a natural cold that neither sickens nor injures.

Three further words are read by some as cognates: the *Midbar Tzin* (Bamidbar
13:21, 20:1, 27:14, 33:36, 34:3; Devarim 32:51; Yehoshua 15:1), which Rabbi
Dovid Golumb (*Targumna* to Shemos 17:1) explains as a notably cold place; the
*tzintzenes* that held the manna (Shemos 16:33), which Rav Hirsch describes as
an ice box keeping the manna cold; and *tzinim* in Mishlei 22:5, thorns to most
commentators but cold to Rabbi Yosef Nechemias and to Radak in *Sefer
HaShorashim*. Bamidbar 33:55 warns that the remaining Canaanites will be
*sikim* in your eyes and *tzninim* in your sides: most read both as kinds of
thorn, but Rashbam ties *tzninim* to *tzinah*, cold. Rashbam does not say how
that fits the context; Rabbi Klein suggests, as his own reading, that the Torah
may mean a sharp, stinging coldness that vexes a man and hampers what he can
get done.

Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation uses the pairing of
*tzinah* with *sheleg*, snow, in Mishlei 25:13 to join the word's two senses:
just as snow covers a wide area and surrounds a person on every side, so does a
*tzinah*, a shield or perhaps armor, cover a wide area and surround the man it
protects. On that basis he argues that *tzinah* as coldness does not name the
drop in temperature, as *kor* does, but an all-encompassing cold that covers a
wide area and envelops a person entirely.

### tzonen (צונן), the rabbinic form
Radak: in rabbinic Hebrew the final *nun* doubled, giving *tzonen*. In the
Mishnah *tzonen* is coldness, usually as the opposite of hot (Maasros 4:4; Shabbos 3:4-5, 22:4, 22:6;
Pesachim 3:3-4; Yoma 3:5; Nedarim 8:7; Zevachim 11:7; Keilim 3:7; Mikvaos 10:6;
Machshirin 5:10). The form spread: an animal that is *metzuneness* is not a
tereifah (Chullin 3:5), which Rashi (Chullin 58b) explains as one sickened by
cold; and Sanhedrin 39b says Achav was *metzunan*, frigid.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *tzinah* = **cold** or **chill**; *kor* = **bitter cold** or
  **freezing**. Reserve "cool" for *tzonen* against hot, never for *kor*.
- Bereishis 8:22, *kor vachom*: "cold and heat." The pair is a merism for the
  whole year, so the plain word is right there even though *kor* is the strong
  term; if the passage is being darshened on intensity, note it.
- Mishlei 25:13, *ketzinas sheleg beyom katzir*: "like the **chill** of snow on
  a harvest day." Following Rabbi Steinberg, "the enveloping cold of snow" is
  defensible where the drashah needs the covering sense; say which reading you
  took.
- Trap: English "cold" absorbs both words and "chilly" collapses the scale. Fix
  the grades before translating a passage that has both, and do not let *kor*
  come out milder than *tzinah*.
- Trap: *tzinah* is also "shield." Decide by context,
  and where a commentary is deliberately playing the two senses off each other
  (Rabbi Moshe Kimchi, Abarbanel), transliterate once and gloss both senses
  rather than picking one and losing the argument.
