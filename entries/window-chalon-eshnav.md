# Window: chalon / eshnav

## Bottom line

*Chalon*, the ordinary **window**, appears thirty-one times in Tanach, and its
root describes what a window is: an empty space in a wall (Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim; Radak and Ibn Janach, under ח-ל-ל). *Eshnav* appears only twice, and
at Shoftim 5:28 Sisera's mother looks through both. Rashi, Ibn Janach and Radak
define *eshnav* as *chalon*, which suggests the two mean the same thing. Others
distinguish them, and they do not agree how: a small window (Rabbi Yosef Kara,
Meiri), a peephole (Rabbi Yeshayah of Trani), an enchanted window used for
divination (Malbim to Shoftim; *Kli Yakar* makes the *chalon* the divining
mirror instead), a lens that brings the distant near (Malbim in *Yair Ohr*), a
lattice (Menachem Ibn Saruk, Rabbi Moshe David Valle), a shuttered window (Rabbi
Moshe Yitzchak Tedeschi-Ashkenazi, and independently Dr. Shmuel Yeivin), or a
window for the wind (Rabbi Aharon Marcus, Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein, Rabbi Dr.
Ernest Klein). Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein does not choose among them. Modern
Hebrew's *eshnav*, a service window, is a neologism.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether eshnav differs from chalon at all.** Rashi (to Shoftim 5:28), Ibn
Janach and Radak define *eshnav* as *chalon*, which suggests that both words
mean "window" in the same sense. The commentators below distinguish the two.

**What kind of window an eshnav is.** Six readings, besides the enchanted
window taken up below:

1. **Smaller.** Rabbi Yosef Kara (to Shoftim 5:28) and the Meiri (to Mishlei
   7:6): an *eshnav* is a small window, while *chalon*, presumably, is the
   general term for any window.
2. **A peephole.** Rabbi Yeshayah of Trani (the Rid, 1180 to 1250): smaller at the end
   that opens to the outside and wider at the end that opens to the inside. He
   then gives a Latin or Italian translation of the word, which Rabbi Shaul
   Goldman reads as *balustraria*, a narrow opening or slit from which arrows
   may be fired.
3. **Latticed.** Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970): the mesh or lattice openings
   on upper floors. Rabbi Moshe David Valle (1697 to 1777) similarly: wooden
   latticework that pampered women tied to their windows so they could look out
   without being seen, the apparatus called in Latin *gelosia* (related to the
   English word *jealous*). Valle then reads the word itself as an abbreviation
   of *ishah nosenes b'chalonoseha*, "a woman places [this] at her windows."
4. **Shuttered.** The Italian scholar Rabbi Moshe Yitzchak Tedeschi-Ashkenazi
   (1821 to 1898), in *Hoil Moshe*: the root is ש-נ-ב, related through the
   interchange of nun and lamed to ש-ל-ב, "step" or "layer," so an *eshnav* is
   specifically a window equipped with metal shutters. The Israeli archaeologist
   Dr. Shmuel Yeivin (1896 to 1982) came to this explanation independently. In a
   1959 article in *Leshonenu* he supported it with ivory artifacts found across
   the Levant showing a woman looking out from the top half of a window (the
   images were said to depict the Canaanite fertility goddess Ashtoreth), the
   bottom half typically closed with mesh or lattice bars. On his view the
   Biblical *eshnav* is exclusively a window partly blocked with such blinds.
   Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that, once the word is tied to ivory images of
   women at windows, one could also consider parsing *eshnav* as a compound of
   "man" (that is, woman) and "tooth" (that is, ivory).
5. **For the wind.** Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) suggests that it comes,
   by metathesis, from נ-ש-ב, the blowing of the wind, since opening a window
   lets the wind blow in. Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein (1860 to 1941) writes the
   same and adds that an *eshnav* is specifically a window used for cooling.
   This is the etymology preferred by the eminent linguist Rabbi Dr. Ernest
   Klein (1899 to 1983). Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein compares English *window*,
   derived from *wind*, and *fenster* (more common in German and Yiddish),
   borrowed from Latin *fenestra*, "hole" or "breach," whose sense resembles the
   explanation of *chalon* below.
6. **A lens.** Malbim, in his later work *Yair Ohr* (on synonyms): an *eshnav*
   is a window, mirror or lens that makes distant objects appear closer, and, as
   Malbim notes, making one requires somewhat advanced knowledge of optics.
   Rabbi Chaim Futernik points out that Malbim gives no source for this novel
   interpretation. The same explanation appears almost word for word in two
   works by Rabbi Elazar Reines (d. 1903), *Shorashei Leshon HaKodesh* and
   *Mishlei Shlomo*.

These readings stand; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein does not choose among them.

**Which of the two was the enchanted window.** Malbim (to Mishlei 7:6) comments,
somewhat cryptically, that through a *chalon* one sees what is revealed and
through an *eshnav* what is hidden. The *Zohar* (Toldos 140b) relates that some
idolaters used the powers of astrology to see things hidden from the naked eye,
gazing through a window enchanted by witchcraft, and says explicitly that
Sisera's mother did this when she looked out to learn whether her son would
return. Another example is Avimelech using a window to divine that Rivkah was
Yitzchak's wife and not his sister (see also *Tzror HaMor* to Bereishis 26:8,
*Sefer HaIkkarim* 4:43, and Abarbanel and Alshich to Shoftim 5:28). On this
basis Malbim (to Shoftim 5:28) writes that the *eshnav* was an enchanted window,
made through witchcraft, by which Sisera's mother expected to see her son's
fate, and the *chalon* an ordinary window. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein finds this
somewhat difficult, since in Bereishis 26:8 Avimelech looks through a *chalon*,
which suggests the *Zohar*'s enchanted window belongs to *chalon*, not *eshnav*. In
fact Rabbi Shmuel Landiado of Aleppo (d. 1610), in *Kli Yakar* to Shoftim 5:28,
writes the opposite of Malbim: the *chalon* was a mirror on the wall used for
divination and the *eshnav* a real window in her room opening onto the street,
so that she first consulted her "hexed window" and only then looked out through
the real one.

## Distinctions

### chalon (חלון)
Thirty-one appearances.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814) traces it to the biliteral
  ח-ל, circular movement and the empty space inside a circle. He puts a wide
  family under that root: *chalil*, the hollow flute; *machol*, a dance in a
  ring; *chalom*, a dream, thoughts going round and round in the mind;
  *chillul*, the void left where holiness was; *challal*, a corpse emptied of
  its life-force; *choli* and *machalah*, a sickness that affects the body all
  around; and *cheil*, the short wall around a higher wall that leaves an empty
  space between them. In *Yerios Shlomo* he explains that *chalon* belongs here
  because a window is essentially an empty space or hole in a wall, and in
  *Cheshek Shlomo* he adds that *chalon* is specifically a round window, tying
  the word to both core meanings of the biliteral.
- Radak and Ibn Janach, who do not accept biliteral roots, list it under the
  triliteral ח-ל-ל, emptiness, with the same meaning.

### eshnav (אשנב)
Twice: Shoftim 5:28, Sisera's mother at the window, and Mishlei 7:6, in the
warning of how the strange woman (a metaphor for strange wisdom) might entice a
person through the window. Modern Hebrew's *eshnav*, the service window of a
bank or post office, is nothing but a modern neologism and is not the Biblical
sense.

## Rendering guidance

- חלון: "window." The one reading that departs from this is *Kli Yakar*'s to
  Shoftim 5:28, where the *chalon* is a divining mirror; that belongs in a note.
- Shoftim 5:28: give the two words two English renderings. "She peered through
  the window… through the lattice" or "…through the casement" keeps the pair;
  "window" twice reads as mere repetition.
- Trap: English "window" absorbs both. Where a commentary is built on the
  difference (Malbim, *Kli Yakar*), leave both words plain and explain the
  reading in a note rather than presuming one.
- Mishlei 7:6: the Meiri, commenting on this verse, reads "small window";
  "lattice" follows Ibn Saruk and Valle and carries the sense of looking out
  unseen.
- Do not render *eshnav* "service window," which is modern. "Peephole" commits
  to the Rid's reading, as "lattice" commits to Ibn Saruk and Valle and "small
  window" to Kara and the Meiri.
