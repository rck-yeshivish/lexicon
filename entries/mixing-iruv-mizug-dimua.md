# Mixing: iruv / mizug / dimua (and dimah, navoch)

## Bottom line

*Iruv* is for **dry** things intermingling:
**mixing, intermingling**. *Mizug* is for **liquids**, specifically the pouring
of one into another: **mixing, diluting** (a cup of wine). *Dimua* is the
Mishnaic term for mixing, used for instance of **terumah** that has fallen into
unsanctified produce: **commingling**. *Dimua* and *dimah* ("tear") are the same root ד-מ-ע, and how the
two senses relate has been the subject of much discussion;
most of the commentators below hold them connected, Ibn Janach being the
one who does not.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| עירוב | iruv | mixing, intermingling | dry things or people intermingling (Analak); Ezra's mixed seed |
| מזיגה | mizug | mixing, diluting | liquids, diluting wine with water (Analak); the Seder's mixed cup |
| דימוע | dimua | commingling | Mishnaic, of *terumah* mixed into *chullin*; shares a root with *dimah*, tear |

## Distinctions

### iruv (עירוב)
Root ע-ר-ב, a quite versatile root with a plethora of meanings. In this sense it serves
in Tanach (Mishlei 14:10, 20:19, 24:21; Tehillim 106:35; Ezra 9:2) and in
Biblical Aramaic (Daniel 2:41, 2:43): Ezra's *hisarvu*, of Jewish seed mixed
with the seed of the nations, and Tehillim's *vayisarvu*, of Jews mingling with
the gentiles and learning their ways. Declensions: *taarovess* and *irbuv*
(mixtures), *eiruv*. Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak of Shidlitz: *iruv* is the
mixing up of **dry** objects, as with people intermingling.

### mizug (מזיגה)
Apparently from מ-ז-ג, a root that surfaces in Tanach exactly once, in the noun
*mezeg* of mixed wine (Shir HaShirim 7:3). In Mishnaic Hebrew it is the ordinary
word for mixing a cup, which then meant diluting strong wine with water: *mazgu
lo* for each of the Four Cups (Pesachim 10:2, 10:4, 10:7), also Nazir 2:3 and
Demai 7:2, with *mezigah* (Shabbos 8:1), *mozeg* (Avodah Zarah 5:5), *mozgin*
(Berachos 8:2), *mazug* (Negaim 1:2, Niddah 2:6-7) and *limzog* (Pesachim 7:13,
Sukkah 2:9); two original modern uses are *mezeg avir* (weather, literally the
mixing of air) and *mazgan* (air conditioner). As early as Menachem Ibn Saruk, a
thousand years ago, it has been suggested that the root is really the two-letter
ז-ג, the *mem* not belonging to it; *zag* is the seed or skin of a grape (Nazir
6:2), though how that connects to *mizug* is not so clear.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim accounts for the connection: *mizug* is the mixing
  that pours a drink from one vessel into another, and when a juice such as wine
  is stored in a receptacle that vessel stands in for the natural skin of the
  fruit the juice was drawn from, so the *mizug* takes the place of the *zag*.
- Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehudah*) makes a similar point:
  *mizug* preserves the wine's taste as the *zag* naturally does.

Analak: *mizug* is for **liquid** ingredients, wine mixed with water.

### dimua (דימוע)
Root ד-מ-ע. The Mishnaic term for mixing, used for instance of terumah mixed
into chullin (Terumos 1:4, 3:1,
3:9, 4:7, 5:5-6, 9:4; Challah 1:4, 3:2; Orlah 2:4, 2:6; Demai 1:3; Shabbos
21:1; Chagigah 3:4; Gittin 5:4; Avodah Zarah 3:4, 4:7; Ohalos 16:4; Taharos
3:4). In Tanach this sense seems absent, with one possible exception: the
warning not to delay one's *dimah* (Shemos 22:28), which Temurah 4a and the
Mechilta read as terumah. Rashi there says outright that he does not know why
terumah should be called *dimah*; to Temurah 4a he explains that terumah is
nullified only at one in a hundred rather than the usual one in two or sixty,
so the name invokes its special mixture-rule. Tosafos there object that this
ratio is only a rabbinic stringency and cannot explain a name the Torah itself
uses; they read *dimah* as implying something wet, terumah being the one tithe
that may be taken from produce already in liquid form. Rabbi Meir Posner (*Beis
Meir* to Even HaEzer §67) defends Rashi: the Torah may name a thing in
anticipation of a later enactment. This is reminiscent of Kesubos 10b, which
reads *almanah* against the *manah* of a widow's kesubah. And Rashi to Pesachim 44a treats the
one-in-a-hundred rule as Biblical anyway.

### dimah (דמעה), tear
Root ד-מ-ע again, eleven times in Tanach as the noun and twice as a verb
(Yirmiyahu 13:17), and in Mishnaic Hebrew too (Sotah 7:8, of Agrippa weeping at
the pasuk barring a foreign king, Devarim 17:15).
- Rabbeinu Yonah Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the two senses are unrelated
  meanings of one root.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*): as tears are pristine liquid from
  the eye, so *dimah* is pristine liquid produce, wine or oil without sediment.
- Ibn Ezra and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): pressed wine and oil emerge drop by
  drop, like tears.
- Ramban also suggests that perhaps the agricultural sense is borrowed from the
  tear, or that neither is borrowed and *dimah* means tear-like droplets of any
  kind.
- Maharal (*Gur Aryeh*): terumah is *dimah* because it applies once the wine or
  oil has separated from the fruit, as a tear separates from the eye.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah*) bridges the two
  meanings: mixture is the core sense, as it more commonly is in Mishnaic
  Hebrew, and tears show outwardly how mixed up a man's feelings are.

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 22:28) takes a different tack: the obligation of terumah
begins only once produce has been fully worked by man, unlike bikkurim, which
begins once the fruit ripens on its own, so *dimah* as tithable produce points
to the manmade character of wine and oil, which have to be pressed out of the
grape and the olive rather than occurring naturally. He notes that wine is
called poetically the blood (*dam*) of grapes (Bereishis 49:11; Devarim 32:14),
pressing being a sort of violence done to the fruit. It is unclear whether Rav Hirsch meant this as an etymology at all, and, if he did,
whether he held *dimah*, with an ayin, to be etymologically related to *dam*,
without one. Elsewhere (Vayikra 7:19, 11:43; Bamidbar 18:29) Rav Hirsch
compares ד-מ-ע with the phonetically near roots ט-מ-ע (mixing, assimilating),
ט-מ-א (impure), ט-מ-ה (lost, assimilated, destroyed) and ד-מ-ה (resemble,
be quiet), on the interchange of dalet with tes and of ayin, aleph and hei, and
suggests that they share a fundamental sense of loss of identity through
absorption into something else, sometimes to the point of total obliteration
(*tamyon*): a thing that "resembles" another, or that is silenced, is likewise
losing something of what is its own.

### navoch (נבוך) and bocheh (בוכה)
Pharaoh, chasing after the Jews, took heart from their seeming lost: *nevuchim
hem ba'aretz* (Shemos 14:3).

- The Mechilta there explicates *nevuchim* as an expression of mixture, Pharaoh
  thinking they were confused and mixed up about where they were headed;
  alternatively it reads *nevuchim* as related to *bocheh*, weeping, Pharaoh
  foreseeing that they were destined to cry in the wilderness (Bamidbar 14:1).
  Rabbi Klein holds there is reason to think the two explanations are actually
  related.
- Rabbi Yisroel Yaakov Algazi (*Kehillas Yaakov*) explains *navochah* of Shushan
  (Esther 3:15) similarly: on the simple level the Jews were confused as to what
  the future held, and Algazi adds that *navochah* also bears on *bocheh*, one
  of the last avenues left them being to cry out to Hashem to save them.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (*Cheshek Shlomo*) likewise traces both
  *bocheh* and *navoch* to the biliteral ב-כ, whose core sense he sees as
  confusion and perplexity, weeping being the involuntary outward reflex of
  confused feelings (cited too in *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Bereishis 50:3).
- Rabbi Eliyahu Menachem Margulies (*Emunas Eliezer*) develops the point that
  crying comes of a man's being unclear about what the future holds: as tears
  blur a man's sight, so he cries precisely because his view of the future is
  blurred, and this is hinted at in the tie of *dimah* to *dimua*, since it is
  the mixture of possibilities before him that brings him to tears. He sees a
  further allusion in *bocheh* being related to the root of *navoch*, perplexed,
  as in the Rambam's *Moreh Nevuchim*; and since the tzaddik can live through
  the uncertainty by relying on Hashem to set everything right in the end, the
  pasuk says *hazorim b'dimah b'rinah yiktzoru*, those who sow in tears will
  reap in joy (Tehillim 126:5).
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: the antidote to crying is *nechamah*, which per Rashi
  (Bereishis 5:29, 6:6, 27:42; Shemos 32:12) is not only comfort but a shift in
  thinking, the word used even of Hashem's "regretting" (so to speak) the making
  of man (Bereishis 6:6); it is the clear view of the future that removes the
  need to cry, and just such a clear view is what Yeshayahu opens with, *nachamu
  nachamu ami* (Yeshayahu 40:1).

## Rendering guidance

- Choose by state of matter: people, seed and populations intermingling take
  *iruv* ("mixed in with," "intermingled"); wine and water take *mizug*.
- A cup at the Seder: "they **mixed** him a cup," not "poured." The Mishnah's
  verb is about dilution, and "poured" loses it.
- *Dimua* in a halachic context: keep it, "the mixture became *meduma*," and
  gloss once. English "mixed" will not carry the terumah-specific rule.
- Trap: English "mix" absorbs all three at once. Where a source lines them up,
  fix the renderings (intermingle / dilute / commingle) before translating.
- Trap: *dimah* is both "tear" and the terumah-tithe. Shemos 22:28 is not about
  tears; render by the tithe and footnote the tear sense if a drashah needs it.
- Where ד-מ-ע means tear and mixture at once (Bava Basra 15a, where one view is
  that Yehoshua wrote the last eight pesukim of the Torah and another that
  Moshe wrote them *b'dema*), name the reading you follow: "with tears," that
  is, while crying, the classical understanding, so Ritva and *Sheilas Yaavetz*
  vol. 1 §13; in tears instead of ink, so Rashba and Maharsha,
  and Rabbi Dovid Tzvi Hoffmann prefers this since the Gemara said *b'dema* and
  not *b'dima'os*; or letters written mixed up and unreadable until after his
  death, so the Vilna Gaon (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Devarim 34:5; *Kol
  Eliyahu*), with Rabbi Kalonymos Kalman Epstein reading *dema* as Moshe's own
  mind clouded as at no other prophecy.
