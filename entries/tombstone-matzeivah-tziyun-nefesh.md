# Tombstone: matzeivah / tziyun / nefesh

## Bottom line

*Matzeivah* is the **monument** or **headstone**, the common word in spoken
Hebrew, and the one the Torah uses at Kever Rachel. *Tziyun* is the **marker**
(the article's gloss). *Nefesh* is the Mishnah's word, literally "soul"; this
guide renders it **memorial** where it names the structure. Rabbi Yechiel
Michel Tukachinsky reads the three as three reasons for erecting a stone at
all: to make the grave visible for those who come to daven, to delineate where
the body lies so that others (Kohanim especially) avoid tumah, and to honor the
departed and his soul. Others read all three instead as the deceased's desire
to go on serving Hashem, cut off by death. A translator who renders all three
"tombstone" loses the reason each is being used.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why the three words.** Rabbi Yechiel Michel Tukachinsky (*Gesher HaChaim*):
three purposes, as above. Others explain instead that all three reflect the
deceased's desire to continue serving Hashem and keeping the mitzvos had he
remained alive: *matzeivah*, which also names the single-stone altar, says he
wished to go on worshipping Hashem and was forced to stop because he died;
*tziyun*, "remarkable" or "outstanding," the word Tanach uses in the parts
written after the *matzeivah*-altar became forbidden, says he strove for
excellence throughout his life and death cut that pursuit off; and *nefesh*,
related to will (see Rashi to Bereishis 23:8), conjures the departed's will to
do good.

**What the matzeivah is really named for.** Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Ehrenreich takes
a different approach: the tombstone shares its word with the single-stone altar
because the two share a common feature. Just as korbanos on an altar atone for
sins, so does the death of the righteous, who are memorialized with a headstone
(Moed Katan 28a). Rabbi Moshe Shick: we colloquially call a tombstone
*matzeivah* because the word is related to *netziv*, "standing" or "erect," and
the name instills the belief in *Techiyas HaMeisim*, that the dead will stand up
again. Rabbi Asher Pollak adds that the pasuk itself alludes to this: Kever
Rachel's stone is "the *matzeivah* of Rachel's grave *ad hayom*," and *hayom* is
a codeword for the day Mashiach will arrive, as in Tehillim 95:7, which
Eliyahu HaNavi cited as a prooftext that Mashiach will come today, if only
Israel would listen to Hashem's voice (Sanhedrin 98a).

**What tziyun means here.** Tukachinsky: the stone that delineates exactly where
the deceased is buried. A Rabbi Pollack (see Sources on which one) writes that a
tombstone is called a *tziyun* because, like a road marker, it reminds those who
see it where they are going and what is expected of them. The "others" above
read *tziyun* as "outstanding."

**What nefesh means here.** Three readings. Tukachinsky: the soul, honored at
the place where it may linger near its former body. The "others" above: will,
the departed's will to do good (Rashi to Bereishis 23:8). Rabbi Eliyahu Katz:
in the context of tombstones the word is actually related to *menuchah*, rest,
from *shavas vayinafash* (Shemos 31:17), so a stone that marks one's eternal
resting place is itself named for resting. The Bottom line renders "memorial"
because no one English word carries all three readings; that choice is this
guide's.

## Distinctions

### matzeivah (מצבה)
Yaakov set a *matzeivah* over Rachel's grave (Bereishis 35:20), and this is the
common word in spoken Hebrew. Its other sense is the single-stone altar that was
halachically permitted for avodah in the days of the Avos and later forbidden,
and both Ehrenreich's reading and that of the "others" work off that double
sense; Shick and Pollak work instead off *netziv*, standing.

### tziyun (ציון)
Graves marked with a *tziyun* appear at Yechezkel 39:15 and Melachim II 23:17.
The article glosses the word "marker." For Tukachinsky its function is to
delineate the exact spot so that others can avoid tumah, which is especially
pertinent for Kohanim (Vayikra 21:1 to 4); for Rabbi Pollack it is a road marker
reminding the passerby where he is headed; for the "others," the sense
"remarkable, outstanding" speaks of the deceased's striving for excellence.

### nefesh (נפש)
Shekalim 2:5: one view in the Mishnah holds that money left
over from what was collected for a man's burial should go to building a *nefesh*
at his grave (see also Ohalos 7:1). The literal sense is "soul," but the word is
used here for the structure.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "tombstone" will absorb all three, and in a halachic passage that is a
  real loss, since on Tukachinsky's reading only *tziyun* carries the tumah
  function.
- Do not render *nefesh* "soul" where the Mishnah means the monument; "memorial"
  or "memorial structure" is right, with the literal sense in a note if the
  passage plays on it.
- *Matzeivah* in Bereishis and *matzeivah* as the forbidden altar are one word
  and two institutions. In a Chumash context render "pillar" for the altar and
  "monument" over a grave, and note the shared word where a derashah rests on it.
- *Tziyun* is "marker," not "sign," where graves are the subject; "sign" reads
  as an omen.
- Where a source is building on *netziv* and the Resurrection, keep the standing
  image alive in English ("a stone set upright") rather than flattening to
  "headstone."
