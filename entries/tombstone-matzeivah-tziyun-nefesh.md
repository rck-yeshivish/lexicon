# Tombstone: matzeivah / tziyun / nefesh

## Bottom line

*Matzeivah* is the **monument** or **headstone**, the ordinary word, and the one
the Torah uses at Kever Rachel. *Tziyun* is the **marker**, which fixes where
the body lies. *Nefesh* is the Mishnah's word, best rendered **memorial**, and
never literally "soul" in this sense. Rabbi Yechiel Michel Tukachinsky reads the
three as three reasons for erecting a stone at all: to be visible for those who
come to daven, to warn off tumah, and to honor the departed. A translator who
renders all three "tombstone" loses the reason each is being used.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מצבה | matzeivah | monument, headstone | makes the grave visible for those who come to daven (Tukachinsky) |
| ציון | tziyun | marker | delineates the spot so Kohanim avoid tumah (Tukachinsky) |
| נפש | nefesh | memorial | honors the departed and his soul (Tukachinsky); or rest, from vayinafash (Rabbi Eliyahu Katz) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why the three words.** Rabbi Yechiel Michel Tukachinsky (*Gesher HaChaim*):
three purposes, as above. Against that, others read all three as expressing what
the deceased would still have wanted to do had he lived: *matzeivah*, which also
names the single-stone altar of the Avos, says he wished to go on serving Hashem
and was stopped by death; *tziyun*, in the sense of "outstanding," says he
strove for excellence and was cut off; and *nefesh*, which carries will (see
Rashi to Bereishis 23:8), says he willed to do good. Note that *tziyun* enters
Tanach in the later books, after the *matzeivah*-altar had become forbidden.

**What the matzeivah is really named for.** Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Ehrenreich: it
shares its word with the single-stone altar because the two share a function.
Korbanos on an altar atone, and so does the death of a *tzaddik* (Moed Katan
28a). Rabbi Moshe Shick: the word is kin to *netziv*, standing upright, and the
name plants the belief in *Techiyas HaMeisim*, that the dead will stand up again.
Rabbi Asher Pollak finds this in the pasuk itself: Kever Rachel's stone is "the
*matzeivah* of Rachel's grave *ad hayom*," and *hayom* is the day Mashiach comes,
as Eliyahu HaNavi proved from *hayom im b'kolo tishma'u* (Tehillim 95:7;
Sanhedrin 98a).

**What nefesh means here.** Tukachinsky: the soul, honored at the place where it
may linger near its body. Rabbi Eliyahu Katz: not soul at all but rest, from
*shavas vayinafash* (Shemos 31:17), so a stone that marks an eternal resting
place is named for resting. The Bottom line renders "memorial" precisely because
the two readings will not both fit one English word.

## Distinctions

### matzeivah (מצבה)
Yaakov set a *matzeivah* over Rachel's grave (Bereishis 35:20), and this is the
word ordinary Hebrew uses. Its other sense is the single-stone altar that was
permitted for avodah in the days of the Avos and later forbidden, and both of the
readings above work off that double sense.

### tziyun (ציון)
Graves marked with a *tziyun* appear at Yechezkel 39:15 and Melachim II 23:17.
The word means a marker or a sign, and its function is legal before it is
honorific: it tells a Kohen where not to go (Vayikra 21:1-4).

### nefesh (נפש)
Shekalim 2:5: one view holds that money left over from what was collected for a
man's burial should go to building a *nefesh* at his grave (see also Ohalos 7:1).
The literal sense is "soul," but the word is used here for the structure.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "tombstone" will absorb all three, and in a halachic passage that is a
  real loss, since only *tziyun* carries the tumah function.
- Do not render *nefesh* "soul" where the Mishnah means the monument; "memorial"
  or "memorial structure" is right, with the literal sense in a note if the
  passage plays on it.
- *Matzeivah* in Bereishis and *matzeivah* as the forbidden altar are one word
  and two institutions. In a Chumash context render "pillar" for the altar and
  "monument" over a grave, and note the shared word where a derashah rests on it.
- *Tziyun* is "marker," not "sign," where graves are the subject; "sign" reads
  as an omen.
- Where a source is building on *netziv* and the Resurrection, keep the standing
  image alive in English ("a stone set upright") rather than flattening to
  "headstone."
