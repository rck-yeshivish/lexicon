# Forehead: meitzach / padachat

## Bottom line

*Meitzach* is the biblical word, some thirteen times in Tanach; *padachat* is the
word of Chazal, absent from Tanach. From the Geonim onward they are treated as
the same thing in two strata of the language (Rav Hai Gaon; Rabbeinu Chananel;
*Sefer HaAruch*; and Rashi, who glosses *padachat* as *meitzach* every time), so
render both **forehead**. The one thing not to get wrong is Shemos 28:38: since
most authorities hold the *tzitz* sat above the forehead where the *tefillin*
go, the Ritva and *Tosafos Yeshanim* either read the pasuk as "above his
*meitzach*" or take biblical *meitzach* as **the top of the head**, not the brow.
Do not render that pasuk "on his forehead" without knowing which reading you are
committing to.

## Distinctions

### meitzach (מצח)
Thirteen or so occurrences in Tanach, always in the construct form, which is why
the vowelization outside the construct is disputed (see below). Targum Yonasan
to Shemos 28:38 renders it *padachta*. On the root:

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim ties *meitzach* to *netzach* and *nitzachon*, victory:
  the forehead is the most conspicuous part of a man and so seems to overpower
  the rest of him. He runs both back to the biliteral צ-ח.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche takes the shorter route to the same core: a
  forehead is smooth and hairless, and so is fittingly called *tzach*, clean and
  clear.

### The problem at Shemos 28:38
Most commentators hold the *tzitz* was worn above the forehead, on the place
where *tefillin* are laid, which sits badly with a pasuk that puts it "on
Aharon's *meitzach*." The *Chiddushei HaRitva* and *Tosafos Yeshanim* (to Yoma
71b) give two answers. Either *meitzach* does mean forehead and the pasuk means
*above* the forehead; or *meitzach* means forehead only in the Hebrew of Chazal,
while in biblical Hebrew it means the top of the head, or at least includes it
along with the brow. On the second answer "forehead" is simply the wrong English
for the pasuk. *Tosafos HaShalem* (to Shemos 39:28) states outright that
*meitzach* does not mean *padachat*.

### padachat (פדחת)
Not in Tanach; standard in the Mishnah and Gemara. A child counts as born once
its *padachat* has emerged (Niddah 3:5), and a corpse may be identified for the
sake of permitting his widow only when the *padachat* and the face are intact
(Yevamos 120a). Neither text defines the word. The identification with *meitzach*
goes back to the Geonim: Rav Hai Gaon in his commentary to Niddah 3:5, and his
student to Shabbos 80b; Rabbi Zecharia Aghmati (*Sefer HaNer* to Shabbos 80b)
citing Rav Hai; then Rabbeinu Chananel to Shabbos 80b and the *Aruch*. Rashi
glosses *padachat* as *meitzach* at Shabbos 151b, Yevamos 120a, Kesuvos 75a, and
Bechoros 46b, without exception. Where the word came from is unsettled, since the
root פ-ד-ח is unattested elsewhere in Hebrew:

- Rabbi Yaakov Yehuda Zilberberg de Kassif groups three roots that use *peh*,
  *dalet*, and *ches* in different orders: *dachaf*, to push, *pachad*, fear, and
  *padachat*. Alarm pushes its way to the front of a man's feelings, and the fear
  is then pushed outward and shows on his forehead.
- Rabbi Chelouche reads *padachat* as a cognate of *metzach* outright, *mem*
  interchanging with *peh* (both sounded at the lips) and *tzadi* with *dalet*.
- Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg links פ-ד-ח to פ-ר-ח, blossoming, on the interchange
  of *dalet* and *reish*, which gives something that protrudes. That sits well
  with Rashi (to Shabbos 151b), who describes the forehead as the smoothest and
  most gleaming part of the face and therefore the most prominent.

### metzach or meitzach
Because the biblical forms are all construct, they carry a *tzeirei* (or
*chirik*) under the *mem*. How to point the absolute form is disputed: some keep
the *tzeirei*, giving *meitzach*, and others give it a *segol*, *metzach*. The
practical case is the Yom Kippur vidui, where some siddurim read *azus metzach*
and others *azus meitzach*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Padachat* in the Mishnah and Gemara: "forehead," with no hesitation.
- *Meitzach* in Tanach generally: "forehead," or "brow" where the register
  wants it (*chazak meitzach*, a hard brow).
- Shemos 28:38: "upon his forehead," with a note that most hold the *tzitz* sat
  above the forehead, and that the Ritva and *Tosafos Yeshanim* read the word
  either as "above" or as the crown of the head. If your source is arguing from
  the placement, follow whichever reading it uses and say so.
- *Azus metzach*: "brazenness," literally "hardness of forehead." Where the
  vidui is being quoted, keep the siddur's own pointing rather than normalizing
  it.
- Trap: English "forehead" covers both Hebrew words and hides the fact that they
  belong to different layers of the language. Where a Rishon is proving that
  *meitzach* and *padachat* are or are not the same, the two must stay visible;
  transliterate rather than translate in that argument.
- Second trap: "brow" in English can mean the eyebrow. Do not use it where the
  anatomy is at stake, as at Niddah 3:5.
