# Suddenly: peta / pitom

## Bottom line

Both mean **suddenly**, but seem to refer to different degrees of intentionality
in the one who did or experienced the sudden event. *Peta* is the rarer word
(seven times in Tanach), and the Gemara (Kerisus 9a) holds that it means
*shogeg* only; *pitom* (twenty-five times) is for the Gemara the more ambiguous
word, reaching *shogeg*, *oness* and even *meizid*. Not all agree: in the Sifrei
Rabbi Yoshiya makes *pitom* *oness* and Rabbi Yonatan reverses the pair; Rashi's
reading of *peta* as immediacy seems to fit *oness*, and Bartenuro reaches
*oness* by another route; Rav Hirsch makes *peta* the subjective surprise and
*pitom* the objective one, and Rabbi Pappenheim the exact opposite. Most sources
point to a marked difference, though Ibn Ezra seems to say the two are one and
the same. Where they stand together, as in Bamidbar 6:9, the Gemara reads them
as a halachic pair.

## Where the authorities disagree

What, if anything, separates the two? These are the views.

1. **Kerisus 9a**: *peta* means *shogeg* only; *pitom* is the more ambiguous one
   and can mean *shogeg*, *oness*, or *meizid*. The Mishnah (Kerisus 2:2) has the
   Nazir who became tamei bring his *asham* whether *shogeg*, *oness* or
   *meizid*; one might have thought *pitom* alone means only *shogeg*, as an
   *asham* generally comes only for *shogeg*, so the Torah added *peta*, which
   only means *shogeg*, and *pitom* must then mean something more. The proofs
   that *pitom* covers all three are verbal: *petaim* in
   Mishlei 22:3 for deliberate sinners, *peti* in Mishlei 14:15 for the naive
   one whose folly leads to a mistake, and *pitom* in Bamidbar 12:4, where
   Hashem spoke suddenly to Moshe, Aharon and Miriam, a thing wholly outside
   their control. That *peta* is *shogeg* is learned from Bamidbar 35:22, since
   only the *shogeg* killer goes to an *ir miklat*.
2. **Sifrei to Bamidbar 6:9**: not all the Sages accept the Gemara's
   assumption. The Sifrei agrees that the two words are two different degrees of
   unintended action, but records a machlokes: Rabbi Yoshiya holds *peta* is
   *shogeg* (as the Gemara assumes) and *pitom* is *oness*; Rabbi Yonatan holds
   *peta* is *oness* and *pitom* *shogeg*.
3. **Rashi to Bamidbar 6:9** seems to follow Rabbi Yonatan. Rabbi David HaLevi
   Segal (1586 to 1667), author of the TaZ, explains in *Divrei David* there how
   Rashi actually follows the Gemara in adopting Rabbi Yoshiya's view; see also
   *Biur HaGra* to Mishlei 6:15. Elsewhere Rashi explains *peta* as immediacy
   (see Distinctions), which Rabbi Klein finds seems to fit Rashi's ostensible view
   that *peta* is *oness*; Bartenuro reaches *oness* by a slightly different
   route.
4. **Rav Hirsch** (to Bamidbar 6:9): *peta* is a **subjective** surprise, one a
   person could have foreseen had he thought about where his course was
   heading, which is the profile of *shogeg*; *pitom* is an **objective**
   surprise, which no amount of forecasting would have caught, and its lack of
   foreknowledge leaves one like a *peti*, who could not have anticipated it.
5. **Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim** writes the exact opposite: *peta* is the surprise
   whose cause was not objectively visible, and *pitom* the one that those
   affected could not subjectively prepare for though its signs were plain.
6. **Ibn Ezra** (to Bamidbar 35:22) seems to say the two are one and the same,
   and Rabbi Avraham Menachem Rappaport (*Minchah Belulah* to Bamidbar 12:4)
   seemingly follows this when he suggests they are etymologically related,
   since ayin and aleph can interchange, so that פ-ת-ע equals פ-ת-א(ם). On this
   approach the two may mean exactly the same thing, not synonyms but different
   forms of one word.

Rabbi Klein concludes that, whatever the exact difference, most sources point to
a marked difference between the two; Ibn Ezra's reading stands in contrast to
all of them.

## Distinctions

### peta (פתע)
Root פ-ת-ע (technically *feta*). Seven appearances: one is the killer who pushed
his victim *b'feta* (Bamidbar 35:22); four are alongside *pitom* (Bamidbar 6:9;
Yeshayahu 29:5, 30:13; Mishlei 6:15), and two more stand alone (Chavakuk 2:7;
Mishlei 29:1).

- Rashi (to Bamidbar 35:22, Mishlei 6:15, Makkos 7b) explains it as closeness
  and immediacy: something so quick in succession that one could not possibly
  have been careful to prevent it. Modern Hebrew *haftaah*, surprise, is derived
  from *peta*. Rabbi Klein finds that all this seems to fit Rashi's ostensible
  view that *peta* is *oness*.
- Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenuro (*Amar Nakeh* to Bamidbar 6:9) offers a slightly
  different take on why *peta* should be *oness*: peh often interchanges with
  the phonetically similar beis, so פ-ת-ע may be viewed as ב-ת-ע, a permutation
  of ב-ע-ת, "startled" or "frightened" (Esther 7:6; Yirmiyahu 8:15), making
  *peta* what catches a person totally off guard. The Arabic *baghta*,
  "unexpected event," is a cognate of this root. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740
  to 1814) derives ב-ע-ת from the two-letter ב-ע, what reveals itself from
  beneath the surface, as *bua*/*avabua*, boils that bubble up on the skin
  (Shemos 9:9-10; Yeshayahu 64:1); as a corollary the root came to mean fear,
  which is often accompanied by goose-bumps on the surface of the skin.

### pitom (פתאום)
Twenty-five appearances.

- Rabbeinu Efrayim ben Shimshon of Regensburg (to Bamidbar 12:4) parses it as a
  compound of *peh*, mouth, and *to'em*, fits or matches; he ostensibly means
  that what happens *pitom* comes as fast as words leave the mouth.
- Rabbi David Golumb (1861 to 1935) similarly takes the ת-א-ם element as fitting
  or matching: a person may be in a calm, serene moment where everything fits
  together, when suddenly something disrupts it.

### peti and pitui (פתי, פתוי)
Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 9:27 and Bamidbar 6:9) ties *pitui*, persuading or
enticing, to *pesach* and *patuach*, "open": one can be talked into a thing only
if he stands open to outside impressions, and the *peti*, the simple one, is
open in just that way, easily deceived or talked into things. The Gemara's
proofs from *peti* and *petaim* (above) rest on their resemblance to *pitom*;
Bartenuro cites Yechezkel 45:20 to illustrate the *pitom*-*peti* connection.

## Rendering guidance

- The couplet פתע פתאום (Bamidbar 6:9): render it as two words. "With sudden
  suddenness" is stiff but honest; "suddenly, all at once" reads better and
  still keeps two terms. A single "suddenly" destroys the drashah.
- Trap: English "suddenly" covers both words completely, so any passage that
  distinguishes them needs the difference carried in an added phrase, not in
  the adverb, and the phrase has to follow the view in play: for the Gemara and
  Rav Hirsch *peta* is the surprise one could have foreseen ("unwarily"), for
  Rashi's immediacy it is "too quickly to guard against"; *pitom* for Rav
  Hirsch is "with no warning at all."
- Where a passage is quoting Kerisus 9a, keep *shogeg*, *oness* and *meizid* in
  transliteration; "by mistake," "by accident" and "on purpose" are serviceable
  glosses but blur the halachic categories if used alone.
- פתי in Mishlei: "simpleton" or "simple-minded fool" (Rabbi Klein renders
  Mishlei 14:15 and 22:3 with "fool"); on Rav Hirsch's reading the word is
  about being open and easily swayed.
