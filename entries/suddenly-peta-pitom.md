# Suddenly: peta / pitom

## Bottom line

Both mean **suddenly**. *Peta* is the narrower and rarer word (seven times in
Tanach), and the Gemara pins it to *shogeg*, a mistake one could have avoided.
*Pitom* is the wider word (twenty-five times), reaching *shogeg*, *oness* and
even *meizid*. Where they stand together, as in Bamidbar 6:9, they are a
halachic pair and not a stylistic doubling; render the couplet with two words
("suddenly, all at once") and never collapse it into one.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| פתע | peta | suddenly | shogeg alone (Kerisus 9a); immediacy too quick to guard against (Rashi); subjective surprise (Rav Hirsch) |
| פתאום | pitom | suddenly, all at once | the ambiguous word, covering shogeg, oness and meizid (Kerisus 9a); objective surprise (Rav Hirsch) |
| פתי | peti | simpleton | open, and so easily swayed by what is outside him (Rav Hirsch) |

## Where the authorities disagree

This is one of the most disputed pairs in the column, and the views do not
reconcile.

1. **Kerisus 9a**: *peta* means *shogeg* only; *pitom* is the ambiguous one and
   can mean *shogeg*, *oness*, or *meizid*. The proofs are verbal: *petaim* in
   Mishlei 22:3 for deliberate sinners, *peti* in Mishlei 14:15 for the naive
   one whose folly leads to a mistake, and *pitom* in Bamidbar 12:4, where
   Hashem spoke suddenly to Moshe, Aharon and Miriam, a thing wholly outside
   their control. That *peta* is *shogeg* is learned from Bamidbar 35:22, since
   only the *shogeg* killer goes to an *ir miklat*.
2. **Sifrei to Bamidbar 6:9**, a machlokes among Tannaim: Rabbi Yoshiya holds
   *peta* is *shogeg* and *pitom* is *oness*, as the Gemara assumes; Rabbi
   Yonatan holds the reverse.
3. **Rashi to Bamidbar 6:9** reads on its face like Rabbi Yonatan. Rabbi David
   HaLevi Segal, the TaZ, in *Divrei David* there, shows how Rashi in fact holds
   with the Gemara and Rabbi Yoshiya; see also *Biur HaGra* to Mishlei 6:15.
4. **Rav Hirsch** (to Bamidbar 6:9): *peta* is a **subjective** surprise, one a
   person could have foreseen had he thought about where his course was
   heading, which is the profile of *shogeg*; *pitom* is an **objective**
   surprise, which no amount of forecasting would have caught.
5. **Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim** holds the exact opposite: *peta* is the surprise
   whose cause was not objectively visible, and *pitom* the one that those
   affected could not subjectively prepare for though its signs were plain.
6. **Ibn Ezra** (to Bamidbar 35:22) takes the two as one and the same thing, and
   Rabbi Avraham Menachem Rappaport (*Minchah Belulah* to Bamidbar 12:4) follows
   suit, since ayin and aleph interchange, so that פ-ת-ע and פ-ת-א(ם) are two
   forms of one word rather than two synonyms.

The Bottom line follows the Gemara and Rabbi Yoshiya, since the Mishnah in
Kerisus 2:2, that the Nazir brings his *asham* whether *shogeg*, *oness* or
*meizid*, rests on it: *peta* being fixed to *shogeg* forces *pitom* to reach
further. Ibn Ezra's identity reading is a real minority view.

## Distinctions

### peta (פתע)
Root פ-ת-ע. Seven appearances, four of them alongside *pitom* (Bamidbar 6:9;
Yeshayahu 29:5, 30:13; Mishlei 6:15) and two standing alone (Chavakuk 2:7;
Mishlei 29:1). Rashi (to Bamidbar 35:22, Mishlei 6:15, Makkos 7b) explains it as
closeness and immediacy: the thing follows so fast on what preceded it that no
care could have headed it off. Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenuro (*Amar Nakeh* to
Bamidbar 6:9) reaches *oness* by another route: peh interchanges with beis, so
פ-ת-ע may be read as ב-ת-ע, a permutation of ב-ע-ת, "startled" (Esther 7:6;
Yirmiyahu 8:15), making *peta* what catches a man wholly off guard. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim traces ב-ע-ת to the two-letter ב-ע, what reveals itself from below
the surface, whence *avabuos*, boils that bubble up (Shemos 9:9-10).

### pitom (פתאום)
Twenty-five appearances. Rabbeinu Efrayim ben Shimshon of Regensburg (to
Bamidbar 12:4) parses it as a compound of *peh*, mouth, and *to'em*, fits: what
happens *pitom* comes as fast as words leave the mouth. Rabbi David Golomb reads
the same ת-א-ם element differently, a man in a settled moment where everything
fits together, and then suddenly something breaks it.

### peti and pitui (פתי, פתוי)
Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 9:27 and Bamidbar 6:9) ties *pitui*, persuading or
enticing, to *pesach* and *patuach*, "open": one can be talked into a thing only
if he stands open to outside impressions, and the *peti*, the simple one, is
open in just that way. This is the family the Gemara leans on when it uses
*peti* and *petaim* to widen *pitom*; Bartenuro cites Yechezkel 45:20 for the
connection.

## Rendering guidance

- The couplet פתע פתאום (Bamidbar 6:9): render it as two words. "With sudden
  suddenness" is stiff but honest; "suddenly, all at once" reads better and
  still keeps two terms. A single "suddenly" destroys the drashah.
- Trap: English "suddenly" covers both words completely, so any passage that
  distinguishes them needs the difference carried in an added phrase, not in
  the adverb: *peta* "before he could take care," *pitom* "with no warning at
  all."
- Where a sefer is quoting Kerisus 9a, keep *shogeg*, *oness* and *meizid* in
  transliteration; "by mistake," "by accident" and "on purpose" are serviceable
  glosses but blur the halachic categories if used alone.
- פתי in Mishlei: "simpleton," not "fool"; the word is about being open and
  unguarded, not about stupidity.
