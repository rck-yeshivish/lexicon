# With: es (itto) / im (imo)

## Bottom line

Both render **with** in English, and no English preposition separates them. The
difference is whether the two parties stand as equals. The Vilna Gaon and
Malbim: *im* is "with" in the full sense, two equal and cooperative parties,
while *es* is the weaker "with," one party dominant and the other along for the
ride. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg hold the exact
reverse: *im* is circumstantial, they merely happened to be together, while *es*
is deliberate and full. Neither view is marginal; when a source darshens a switch
between the two words, find out which school it belongs to before translating,
because the two produce opposite readings of the same pasuk.

## Distinctions

### es (את) as a preposition
*Es* is the commonest word in Tanach, roughly twelve thousand appearances, and
usually carries no lexical meaning at all: it marks the object of a verb, and
Rabbi Nachman Marcuson likens it to an arrow pointing the reader to what the verb
acts on. Ibn Janach and Radak list the places where it does carry meaning: "to"
(Vayikra 13:49; Bamidbar 13:17), "from" (Bereishis 44:4; Bamidbar 35:26), "next
to" (Bereishis 40:4, 44:24), "instead of" (Vayikra 22:17; II Shmuel 12:6), "on
top of" (I Melachim 9:25), and "with." Suffixed it gives *itto*, *itti*, *itah*.
Rashi (to Bereishis 37:18) simply glosses *itto* as *imo*.

### im (עם)
Ayin-mem, "with," suffixed as *imo*, *imi*, *imah*.

### the Vilna Gaon and Malbim: im is the stronger
The Gra reads the Bilam narrative on this. Hashem first forbade him to go
*imahem* (Bamidbar 22:12), then told him to rise and go *itam* (22:20), and the
Torah reports that he went *im* Balak's men, whereupon Hashem's anger flared
(22:21-22). The Gra: *im* is "with" in the fullest sense, the joining of two
equal and cooperative parties; *es* carries no such equality. So Bilam was barred
from joining their effort (*im*), permitted only to travel alongside them (*es*),
and when he went *im* he broke the condition. The Gra and Malbim both derive this
from *es*'s ordinary grammatical use: in *achalta es halechem* the eater and the
bread are anything but equal, so *es* in the sense of "with" marks the unequal
pair, one active and one passive. Malbim applies it to Lot: he came to Eretz
Yisrael *itto* with Avraham (Bereishis 12:4), deferring to him, and by the
parting he is *imo* (13:14), asserting himself as an equal.

### Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: es is the stronger
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheishek Shlomo*) and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
(*HaKesav VeHaKabbalah*): *im* is "with" circumstantially, two who happen to be
together, and *es* is "with" deliberately and absolutely. Rabbi Mecklenburg reads
the same Lot verses the other way round: *itto* at the outset because Lot
attached himself to Avraham in order to learn from him and become his equal, and
*imo* at the parting because he never got there, which he takes as the basis of
the *Zohar* (Bereishis 78b). Two more of his cases: Besuel washed the feet of the
men who were *itto* with Eliezer (Bereishis 24:32) while he still took them all
for equals, and once Eliezer had explained his mission and emerged as the leader
the Torah says the men who were *imo* ate and drank (24:54). At Yaakov's burial,
Pharaoh's servants and the elders of Mitzrayim went up *itto* with Yosef
(Bereishis 50:7) as near-equals, while the chariots and horsemen, far below him
in rank, went up *imo* (50:9). On Bilam, though, Rabbi Mecklenburg's own reading
follows the Gra rather than his general rule.

## Rendering guidance

- Ordinary prose, either word: "with." No English preposition marks the
  distinction, and forcing one ("together with" against "alongside") reads
  strained if the source is not making the point.
- Where a source is darshening the switch, supply the sense in a bracket or a
  note rather than in the preposition: "with them [*imahem*, as full partners]"
  against "with them [*itam*, merely accompanying]."
- Trap: "with" absorbs both words completely, so a derashah built on the change
  from *itto* to *imo* simply vanishes in translation unless the Hebrew is kept
  visible. Where the pair turns up in one passage (Bereishis 12:4 and 13:14;
  24:32 and 24:54; 50:7 and 50:9), quote both Hebrew forms.
- Second trap: most appearances of את are the object marker and carry no meaning
  at all. Before reading "with" into one, check that the word is not simply
  pointing at the object.
- Never render the two by a single English word in a passage where the source
  reads them as opposites; and note which school the source belongs to, since
  the Gra and Rabbi Pappenheim assign the equality to different words.
