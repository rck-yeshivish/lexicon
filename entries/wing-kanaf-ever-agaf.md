# Wing: kanaf / ever / agaf

## Bottom line

*Kanaf* is the **wing**, and specifically its outer reach, which is why it also
means the **edge** or **corner** of a garment or a land and why it is the wing
that shelters. *Ever* is best rendered **pinion**, the outer wing with its
flight feathers, and in Chazal's Hebrew it widens to any **limb**. *Agaf* is the
wing where it joins the body, and by extension a **flank**; it appears only in
Yechezkel. Devarim 32:11 uses *kanaf* and *ever* in one pasuk, so they cannot
both be "wings" there.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| כנף | kanaf | wing; edge, corner | the outermost part of the wing (Wertheimer); hence periphery (Bedersi) |
| אבר | ever | pinion; limb | the outer wing with its flight feathers; any limb, and the strongest (Bedersi) |
| אגף | agaf | wing near the body; flank | the inner part, branching off the body (Wertheimer); only in Yechezkel |
| נוצה | notzah | feather | the feather itself, not the wing |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where ever comes from, and how it relates to avir.** Menachem Ibn Saruk
(*Machberes Menachem*), Rabbeinu Yonah Ibn Janach and Radak all trace *ever* to
the triliteral א-ב-ר and put *avir*, "strong," under the same root, leaving one
root with two unrelated senses. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini resolves it:
*ever* is any limb of a living creature, and it names the wing because that limb
musters the most strength. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 17:4) resolves it the other
way, that *avir* is the power that lifts a man higher, matching the *ever* that
lifts the bird. Rabbi Pappenheim rejects the triliteral reading: the aleph is
not root, the root being the biliteral ב-ר, "choosing," so the *ever* is the
choicest limb. The Bottom line follows Bedersi, which accounts for the wing and
for the Rabbinic *eiver* alike (Makkos 23b; Pesachim 22b).

**Where kanaf comes from.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:21, 7:14) compares *kanaf*
to *ganav*, thief, through the interchange of kaf with gimmel and peh with beis:
both hide, the wing covering part of the bird's body, or carrying it so high
that it is out of sight, as the thief works unseen. Rabbi Pappenheim (*Yerios
Shlomo*) assigns *kanaf* to the biliteral כ-פ but gives no thematic link, and
his treatment of כ-פ as "receptacle" in *Cheshek Shlomo* (whence *kaf*, palm,
and *kapayim*, the heavens draped like a dome) does not mention *kanaf* at all.

**What agaf is built on.** Rabbi Wertheimer and Menachem Ibn Saruk work from the
biliteral ג-פ, the aleph not being root, as in the biblical Aramaic *gapin*
(Daniel 7:4, 7:6); Menachem notes that *gaf* in Mishlei 9:3 means the edges of a
land exactly as *kanaf* does. Radak instead derives *agaf* from the Aramaic
*gadfin*, explaining the dagesh in the peh (the biblical forms are always
*agapav*, *agapecha*, never a soft feh) as standing in for a dropped dalet.
Rabbi Pappenheim gives ג-פ the core sense of outward protrusion, which fits a
wing, and puts *guf*, *negef* and *mageifah*, and *gefen* in the same family.

## Distinctions

### kanaf (כנף)
Root כ-נ-ף. Primarily the wing of a flying creature, and by extension birds
themselves (Bereishis 7:14). Bedersi: because the wing sits at the edge of the
bird, *kanaf* came to mean whatever lies at the periphery, the corner of a
garment (Devarim 22:12; Shmuel I 24:5) or the far edge of a place (Yeshayahu
11:12; Yechezkel 7:2; Iyov 37:3, 38:13). Rabbi Wertheimer adds the sun's rays,
called *kanaf* in Malachi 3:20, since they branch off the sun as wings branch
off a bird, and fixes the anatomy: *kanaf* is the outermost part, the part that
casts shade, which is why taking someone under one's wing is the Torah's own
image for protection (Devarim 23:1; Rus 2:12, 3:9).

### ever (אבר)
Root א-ב-ר, or ב-ר for Rabbi Pappenheim. Many render it "pinion" rather than
"wing," the whole outer section with its flight feathers, and in Rabbinic Hebrew
it widened to any limb. Its appearance beside *kanaf* in Devarim 32:11, where
Hashem spreads His *kanaf* and carries them on His *evrah*, forces them apart.

### agaf (אגף)
Five appearances, all in Yechezkel (12:14, 17:21, 38:6, 38:22, 39:4). Rabbi
Wertheimer: the part of the wing nearest the body, which appears to branch off
it, whence *agaf* for a small town branching off the main city (38:6).

### gadfa and notzah
The Aramaic *gadfa* runs across several senses: a feather, as in the one that
fell on Amimar and was brushed off by a litigant, moving him to recuse himself
(Kesubos 105b; Chullin 22b); the wing bone (Rashi to Yoma 84a); and the wing
itself (Rashbam to Bava Basra 73b). The Targumim render *misgeres* and *gvul* as
*gadnaf* (Shemos 25:25). Biblical Hebrew for a feather is *notzah* (Vayikra
1:16; Iyov 39:13).

## Rendering guidance

- כנף on a bird: "wing." On a garment or a land: "corner," "edge," or "hem," and
  never "wing," even where a drashah plays on both; put the play in a note.
- Devarim 32:11: render the two words differently. "He spreads His wings… He
  bears them on His pinions"; "wings" twice loses the pasuk's distinction.
- Trap: English "wing" will absorb *kanaf*, *ever* and *agaf* together. Where
  two of them appear, give *kanaf* "wing," *ever* "pinion," and *agaf* "flank."
- אבר in Chazal: "limb," not "wing," unless a bird is the subject.
