# Wing: kanaf / ever / agaf

## Bottom line

In Devarim 32:11 *kanaf* and *ever* stand in one pasuk (after Shemos 19:4,
"wings of eagles"); *agaf* is found only in Yechezkel. *Kanaf* is the **wing**.
Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer makes it specifically the outermost part,
farthest from the body, the part that casts shade and so stands for protection;
Bedersi explains that because the wing sits at the edge of the bird, *kanaf*
came to mean the **edge** or **corner** of a garment or a place. *Ever*, which
Rabbi Klein's own translation renders "wing," many prefer to translate
**pinion**, the whole outer part of the wing with its flight feathers; Bedersi
makes it any **limb**, the wing being the strongest, and in Rabbinic Hebrew it
means any limb. *Agaf* is, for Rabbi Wertheimer, the part of the wing nearest
the body, whence a town branching off a city. Dr. Chaim Tawil compares the three
to Akkadian *kappu*, *abru* and *aggapu*; such Semitic parallels, Rabbi Klein
notes, do not quite explain how the words differ. The Biblical word for a
feather is *notzah*; the Aramaic *gadfa* is a feather, a wing bone, or the wing
itself.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where ever comes from, and how it relates to avir.** The earliest
grammarians, Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970), Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach (990 to
1050) and Radak (1160 to 1235), trace *ever* to the triliteral א-ב-ר and put
*avir*, "strong," under the same root, which on the surface would suggest one
root with two entirely different senses. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini, a
thirteenth-century Spanish sage, relates them: *ever* is any limb of a living
creature, like the hands or legs, and in birds it names the wings because that
limb musters the most strength. Alternatively, Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 17:4)
explains *avir* as the power that lifts a person to a higher place, in a
spiritual or moral sense, on a par with the bird's *ever*, which lifts it
physically. Rabbi Pappenheim holds that the aleph of *ever* is not part of the
root: the root is the biliteral ב-ר, primarily "choosing" or "separating," and
the *ever* is the choicest limb of a bird because it is the strongest.

**Where kanaf comes from.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:21, 7:14; Vayikra
19:11) compares *kanaf* to *ganav*, thief, through the interchange of kaf with
gimmel and peh with beis: both conceal, the wing covering part of the bird's
body, or carrying it so high that it is out of sight, as the thief works
unseen. Rabbi Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) assigns *kanaf* to the biliteral
כ-פ but offers no thematic link, and his exposition of כ-פ as "receptacle" in
*Cheshek Shlomo* (whence *kaf*, the palm, and *kapayim*, the heavens draped over
the horizon like a dome) does not mention *kanaf* at all. Rabbi Klein surmises,
in the absence of an explanation from Rabbi Pappenheim, that *kanaf* would then
be the limb by which a bird reaches toward the *kapayim*.

**What agaf is built on.** *Agaf* seems to be cognate with the Biblical Aramaic
*gapin*, wings (Daniel 7:4, 7:6), which, Rabbi Klein infers, suggests a
biliteral root ג-פ with a non-essential aleph. Rabbi Pappenheim holds exactly
that: the aleph of *agaf*, as of *ever*, is not root. He gives ג-פ the core
sense of outward protrusion, apt for a wing, and puts in the same family *gap*
or *guf*, the body, a person's outward projection as against the soul within,
which, he also suggests, is a person's means of moving about as wings are a
bird's; *negef* and *mageifah*, disease that spreads outward; and *gefen*, the
cluster of grapes
spreading from its stem like wings. Menachem Ibn Saruk compares the triliteral
כ-נ-ף with the biliteral ג-פ: as *kanaf* means a wing and also the periphery of
a place, so *gaf* in Mishlei 9:3 means the edges of a land. Rabbi Klein
suggests that perhaps the two roots are related through the interchange of kaf
and gimmel and the often-disappearing nun. Radak, noting that the biblical
forms always have a dagesh in the peh (*agapav*, *agapecha*, *agapeha*,
*gapin*), claims the dagesh stands for a missing letter: *agap* derives from the
Aramaic *gadfin*, wings, the dagesh replacing the dalet. The Hebrew root ג-ד-ף
means to curse or blaspheme, and Rabbi Klein finds it intriguing to consider how
that relates to wings (see Rav Hirsch to Bamidbar 15:30).

## Distinctions

### kanaf (כנף)
Root כ-נ-ף. Primarily the wing of a flying creature, and by extension birds
themselves (Bereishis 7:14). Bedersi: because the wing sits at the edge of the
bird's body, *kanaf* came to mean whatever lies at the periphery or edge, of a
thing (Devarim 22:12; Shmuel I 24:5) or of a place (Yeshayahu 11:12, 24:16;
Yechezkel 7:2; Iyov 37:3, 38:13). Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935)
adds the sun's rays, called *kanaf* in Malachi 3:20, since they branch off the
sun as wings branch off a bird. He also fixes the anatomy: *kanaf* is the
outermost part of the wing, protruding farthest from the body, the part that
casts shade and so serves as a metaphor for protection; the idiom of taking
someone under one's wing comes from this Biblical usage (Devarim 23:1; Rus 2:12,
3:9).

### ever (אבר)
Root א-ב-ר, or ב-ר for Rabbi Pappenheim. Though Rabbi Klein's translation of
Devarim 32:11 renders it "wing," many prefer "pinion," the entire outer part of
the wing including its flight feathers. In Rabbinic Hebrew it widened to any
limb: the 248 *eivarim* (Makkos 23b) and *eiver min hachai* (Pesachim 22b). In
Devarim 32:11 it stands beside *kanaf*: Hashem spreads His *kanaf* and carries
them on His *evrah*.

### agaf (אגף)
Five appearances, all in Yechezkel (12:14, 17:21, 38:6, 38:22, 39:4). Rabbi
Wertheimer: *agaf* or *gaf* is the part of the wing nearest the body, which
seems to branch off it, whence *agaf* for a small town or suburb branching off
the main city (38:6). In Modern Hebrew *agaf* is a branch or department of the
central government.

### gadfa and notzah
The Aramaic *gadfa* has several senses: a feather, as in the one that fell on
the judge Amimar and was brushed off by a litigant, whose kindness moved Amimar
to recuse himself out of gratitude (Kesubos 105b; see also Chullin 22b); the
bone of the wing (Rashi to Yoma 84a); and the wing itself (Rashbam to Bava
Basra 73b). The Targumim render *misgeres* and *gvul*, "frame, border,
perimeter," as *gadnaf* (Shemos 25:25; Yechezkel 43:17; see also Rashi to Succah
20b), which also seems related to *gadfa*. The Biblical Hebrew word for a
feather is *notzah* (Vayikra 1:16; Yechezkel 17:3, 17:7; Iyov 39:13).

## Rendering guidance

- כנף on a bird: "wing." On a garment or a land: "corner," "edge," or "hem,"
  following Bedersi's periphery sense.
- Devarim 32:11: the column's own translation renders both words "wings."
  Those who prefer "pinion" for *ever* keep the two apart: "He spreads His
  wings… He bears them on His pinions."
- Trap: English "wing" will absorb *kanaf*, *ever* and *agaf* together. Where
  two of them appear, "wing" for *kanaf* and "pinion" for *ever* keep them
  distinct; for *agaf*, Rabbi Wertheimer's reading points to the inner wing or,
  by extension, an outlying town.
- אבר in Chazal: "limb," not "wing," unless a bird is the subject.
