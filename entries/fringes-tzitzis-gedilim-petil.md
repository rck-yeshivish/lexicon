# Fringes: tzitzis / gedilim / petil / tallis / kruspidin

## Bottom line

*Tzitzis* are the **fringes**, named for what protrudes and is therefore seen.
*Gedilim* are the same strings named, on the reading of Radak and Rav Hirsch,
for the **braided** third that is thickened by twisting. *Petil* is the single
**thread** of *techeiles*, named for snug twisting and, in Rav Hirsch's reading,
for its separateness. *Tallis* is not the fringes at all but the **four-cornered
garment** that carries them, and it is Rabbinic Hebrew only. *Kruspidin* is the
Targum's Aramaic for *tzitzis* and *gedilim*, a Greek loanword for the **hem or
fringe** of a garment. *Tzitzis* and *tallis* are not interchangeable.

## Distinctions

### tzitzis (ציצית)
The classical lexicographers of the triliteral school trace *tzitzis* to צ-י-ץ,
a root that yields "sprouting," "peeking," "branch, wing, fin," and *tzitz*, the
Kohen Gadol's forehead plate.

- Rashi (to Bamidbar 15:38) connects *tzitzis* both to the hair that juts from
  the side of the head (Yechezkel 8:3, the word's only other appearance in
  Tanach) and to "peeking."
- Rashi (to Yirmiyahu 48:9): anything that juts out from a body is a *tzitz*.
- Malbim (*Sefer HaCarmel*): a *tzitz* protrudes from the main body in a way
  that makes it highly visible. In the very same way the strings protrude from
  the garment.

In Mishnaic Hebrew the shreds of foreskin left after a milah are *tzitzin*
(Shabbos 19:6) for the same reason.

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces the word to the single letter צ, whose core
  sense is "exiting, outwardness." In *Yerios Shlomo* the path runs through
  "peeking": a sapling mid-sprout is only partly visible, and so is the eye of a
  man squinting to see better, and from there *tzitzis* and the Kohen Gadol's
  *tzitz* are highly visible ornaments that people end up looking at. In
  *Cheshek Shlomo* he takes the shorter path, that the *tzitzis* appear to
  "exit" the garment they are tied to.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) implies the same monoliteral root,
  although he does not actually list *tzitzis* as a declension of it.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 28:36), by his phonetic method, links צ-י-ץ to ש-י-ש
  (*sasson*, gladness as the outward showing of inner happiness) and to ז-י-ז
  (*ziz*, the mammary gland as the outward showing of the inner motherly
  instinct to provide nourishment), so that *tzitz* is what protrudes from a
  surface and becomes visible to all; he notes this is close to the "growth"
  sense, a plant flourishing outward from its seed.

A prominent nobleman of Yerushalayim in the generation before the Churban was
called Ben Tzitzis (Bereishis Rabbah 42:1, Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer ch. 3, Avos
D'Rabbi Nasan 6:3). Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) explains the name:
his *tzitzis* were so long that they trailed over pillows and blankets wherever
he went. (Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein understands this to mean that he was
prominent enough to have an entourage laying down the ancient equivalent of a
red carpet so the strings would not drag on the floor.)

Rabbeinu Bachaya (*Kad HaKemach* s.v. tzitzis, and to Bamidbar 15:38) draws the
mussar from the "peeking" sense: the wearer should be mindful that Hashem sees
him.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bamidbar 15:38) connects *tzitz*,
*tzitzis* and *tze'etza'im*.

### gedilim (גדילים)
Appears once for the mitzvah, "you shall make yourself *gedilim* on the four
corners of your garment" (Devarim 22:12), and once for a braided metal chain
(Melachim I 7:17). All the early lexicographers (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Ibn
Parchon, Radak) put it in ג-ד-ל, the root of *gadol*.

- Ibn Janach defines *gedilim* as *tzitzis*.
- Radak refines it to free-hanging *tzitzis*.
- Ibn Parchon defines it by *petil*; Ibn Saruk by *chut*, "string."

- Rav Hirsch (to Bamidbar 15:38): a braided rope is a *gedil* because braiding
  makes it thicker than a single string, so it appears to have "grown" and
  become "bigger." He notes parenthetically that the Rabbinic *gudal* ("thumb")
  names the thickest finger, not the longest.
- Radak's father, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (*Sefer HaGalui*), takes the other path:
  the strings "expand" the edge of the garment beyond the fabric itself.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein offers a third turn of the same idea, that *gedilim*
  are a mere extension or "overgrowth" of the garment, as the *gudal* is a sort
  of extension of the hand.

The difference from *tzitzis*: Menachos 39a requires a third of the strings'
length to be *gadil* (braided) and two thirds to hang loose like a branch
(*anaf*). Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) uses that Gemara to resolve why the Torah
sometimes says *gedilim* and sometimes *tzitzis*: one part of the string is a
branch, which is the "branch" sense of צ-י-ץ, and the other is a braid. Rav
Hirsch (to Bamidbar 15:38) reads it similarly. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein then
floats a further step of his own ("I was thinking that perhaps"): the mitzvah
appears in three pesukim, *tzitzis* in two of them (Bamidbar 15:38, 15:39) and
*gedilim* in one (Devarim 22:12), matching the Gemara's own two thirds branch
and one third braid.

### petil (פתיל)
The word belongs to *techeiles*. The Midrash (Bamidbar Rabbah 18:3) explains why
Korach's rebellion is set beside the parashah of *tzitzis*: Korach argued that a
*tallis* made entirely of *techeiles* is exempt from *tzitzis*, and Moshe held
it is obligated (see Menachos 41b).

The Torah requires one *petil* of *techeiles* among the strings (Bamidbar
15:38); Targum Onkelos there renders *petil* as *chut*, "string." The root פ-ת-ל denotes snug attachment or twistedness, as in *tzamid
pasil*, the tightly sealed vessel whose contents are saved from tumah (Bamidbar
19:15); a string is made by twisting fibers snugly together. Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 30:8, Bamidbar 15:38) reads the root as "separateness" instead,
comparing ב-ד-ל (*havdalah*), ב-ת-ל (*besulah*, a woman separated out from the
normal course of events), and ב-ט-ל (*batel*, excluded from the realm of the
relevant): the *techeiles* thread is bound into the *tzitzis* yet stands apart
from them, since it alone is dyed. The name Naftali is tied to this root
explicitly (Bereishis 30:8), Rachel saying she had now intertwined her own fate
with her sister Leah's. Yehudah's *petil* given to Tamar (Bereishis 38:18) is
his belt according to some commentators, such as Ibn Janach, Rabbi Avraham
Maimuni and Rashbam, or his cloak according to Radak; others read it as his
*tallis*/*tzitzis* (Rabbi Moshe HaDarshan, *Bereishis Rabbasi* to 38:25, and
Rabbeinu Chaim Paltiel to 38:18 in the name of Rashbam), which Ramban (to 38:18)
rejects. Some commentators explain that it was his *tefillin*, since *petil* can be
read as an anagram of *tefillin* (see *Paneach Raza* and *Baal HaTurim* to 38:18).
In Mishnaic Hebrew *petilah* is a lamp wick, in some ways just a string
fastened in place to hold a fire (Shabbos 2:4-5, Beitzah 4:4, Keilim 3:2), and
in Sanhedrin 7:2 it seems to mean a lead rod.

### tallis (טלית)
First occurs in Rabbinic Hebrew (Nedarim 3:5, 4:1, Kiddushin 4:14, Bava Metzia
1:1, Meilah 5:1, Keilim 28:7, Ohalos 8:5, Zavim 4:7, and more), and in context
it means the four-cornered garment itself, not its strings. The Mishnaic
*matlis*, something like a wash cloth, is derived from it (Shabbos 24:5, Bava
Kamma 10:10, Keilim 10:4, Negaim 11:5-6, Taharos 9:9). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur
(*Sefer HaTishbi*): in rabbinic usage it is the special four-cornered garment
with *tzitzis* worn at prayer; he, and Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi in *HaMadrich
HaMaspik*, hold the grammatically correct plural is *taliyos*, not *talitos* and
certainly not the popular Yiddish misnomer *talleisim*.

Etymologies:
- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) cites Rav Matzliach Gaon that the
  garment is "placed upon" the other clothes, relating *tallis* to *natal*,
  lifting (see Targum to Bereishis 50:13); alternatively he ties it to ט-ל-ל,
  *tillel*, "shade, covering" in Late Biblical Hebrew and Biblical Aramaic
  (Nechemiah 3:15, Daniel 4:9). Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological
  dictionary of Hebrew, follows that second approach, relating *tallis* to
  *telalim* and holding it cognate with *tzel*, on the interchange of tzadi and
  tes.
- Dr. Alexander Kohut (*Aruch HaShalem*), as is his habit, derives *tallis*
  from Persian, a theory that has not gained much traction.
- David Curwin of the *Balashon* blog brings a further theory, in the name of
  the late Rabbi Dr. Seth Mandel: that *tallis* and the Rabbinic *itztala* come
  from one Greek etymon, both borrowed from Latin *stola* and Greek στολή
  (*stolē*). Rashi (to Sanhedrin 44a, and see Bechoros 34b) does define
  *itztala* as *tallis*, and Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi likewise notes how close
  the two words are.
- Rabbeinu Bachaya reads *tallis* as alluding to Hashem being lifted and raised
  above all, on Daniel's *netilas min ar'a*, "lifted from the earth" (Daniel
  7:4).
- Rabbi Nosson Shapiro (*Matzas Shimurin* fol. 11b) offers a kabbalistic
  derivation from the Aramaic *talyusa*, which Targum Onkelos uses of a young
  person coming of age (Bereishis 8:21); Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says frankly
  that what Rabbi Shapiro says there is beyond his expertise.

### kruspidin (כרוספדין)
The Targum renders both *tzitzis* and *gedilim* as *kruspeda* (plural
*kruspidin*).

- Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Mussaf HeAruch*): an Aramaic loanword from the Greek
  *kraspedon* (κράσπεδον). *Kraspedon* is the fringe, edge, hem, or embroidered
  border of a garment, that is, the prominent extension added to a core frame,
  and it is also the standard Koine Greek word for *tzitzis* in the Christian
  Bible.
- Classicists trace *kraspedon* as a portmanteau of Greek *karé* ("head, top,
  peak") and *pédon* ("foot, bottom, edge"), and Rabbi Shaul Goldman draws that
  etymology out: the term itself implies that the most prominent feature of a
  four-cornered garment is neither its top nor its center but the strings
  protruding outward at its bottom edge.
- Dr. Alexander Kohut (*Aruch HaShalem*) rejects the Greek origin here too
  and prefers Persian, as he often does.

There is an Amora called Rav Kruspedai, cited four times in Bavli, most famously
that on Rosh Hashanah Hashem opens three books, of the wholly righteous, the
wholly wicked, and those in between (Rosh Hashanah 16a; also Sanhedrin 69a,
Kerisus 24a, Niddah 46a). And a story is told of Rabbi Avraham Genachovsky, who,
seated beside an immodestly dressed woman and unwilling to embarrass her, said
only, "I have *kruspidin*"; she took it for the name of a contagious disease and
fled the seat. He had told no lie. (Rabbi Zev Berlin, son-in-law of Rabbi Aharon
Leib Steinman, adds that he heard it happened in a hired van, that she left the
van altogether, and that Rabbi Genachovsky reimbursed the driver for the emptied
seat.)

## Rendering guidance

- *Tzitzis* stays transliterated; where an English gloss is wanted once,
  **fringes**, not "tassels."
- *Gedilim*: **braided cords**, and where the Gemara's ratio is in play, "the
  braided part." Do not render it "fringes" as well, or Radak's resolution of
  the two Torah words collapses.
- *Petil*: **thread** or **cord** (of *techeiles*), singular. Never "fringe."
- *Tallis*: **tallis**, glossed once as the four-cornered garment. Reserve
  "prayer shawl" for a translation aimed outside the frum readership, and even
  then only where nothing in the passage turns on the garment as such.
- Trap: English "fringes" will swallow *tzitzis*, *gedilim*, and *kruspidin* at
  once. When a passage sets *tzitzis* against *gedilim* (Menachos 39a and Radak
  on it), give the first as "fringes" and the second as "braided cords," and let
  *petil* stay a single thread inside them.
- Devarim 22:12 beside Bamidbar 15:38: keep the Torah's own switch of word
  visible in English. The whole derashah is that the Torah used two names for
  one mitzvah.
