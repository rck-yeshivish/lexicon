# Waking: yekitzah / oorah

## Bottom line

*Yekitzah* is **waking up**; *oorah* is **rousing** or **stirring**. How they
differ is disputed, and the article gives three views. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim:
*yekitzah* is sudden and complete, asleep one moment and awake the next, while
*oorah* is gradual and includes the half-asleep state (so "my heart is awake,"
Shir HaShirim 5:2). Rabbi Yechezkel Pannet seems to suggest that *orer* suits
waking from deep sleep and *yekitzah* waking one who merely dozed off. Malbim:
two stages of one process, *oorah* first (coming to one's senses), then
*yekitzah* (getting up and staying awake); and in Chavakuk 2:19 *yekitzah* is
the more responsive waking, *oorah* the most basic. The column adds its own
suggestion that *yekitzah* is the end of a dream or other false impression,
since most of its Biblical occurrences follow a dream or drunkenness. The trap
is that English "awake" and "awaken" cover both; where a text pairs them
(*HaMe'orer yesheinim v'haMeikitz nirdamim*), rendering them by two different
English verbs is our judgment.

## Distinctions

### yekitzah (יקיצה)
The triliteralists, Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and Radak, take it from the
triliteral י-ק-צ, "wake up." Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) instead
files it under the biliteral ק-צ, which also carries border, summer, pressure,
disgust, cutting and thorn; he does not intimate how these connect to waking,
but, the column says, some connections can be gleaned from other
commentators.

Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 9:24) ties *yekitzah* to *kutz* (harvest, cut off) and
*kayitz* (summer): when fruit has ripened it tends to disengage from its source
and is ready to be harvested, and a man who has slept his fill wakes on his own
the same way. To Bereishis 41:4 he adds the *katz* of revulsion: a man who has eaten
his fill is sickened by more food, and a body that has slept its fill likewise
rejects more sleep and wakes.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) takes the core of ק-צ as "end/edge,"
spatially (*ketzeh*, the edge of an area, Bereishis 23:9; Melachim II 7:8; the
article also gives Bamidbar 22:6, which has no such word, presumably for 22:36
or 22:41) and temporally (*keitz*, the end of a span, Bereishis 6:13;
Tehillim 119:96; Daniel 11:35, 12:13, and *mikeitz* from it, the mem added).
*Kayitz* is the end of the year, the last season before Rosh HaShanah.
*Ketzitzah* (Shemos 39:3; Devarim 25:12) is cutting off an end, typically in one
swift motion rather than the drawn-out work of sawing or slicing, and
that is what makes *yekitzah* what it is: a sudden, total waking, asleep one
moment and awake the next, with no half-and-half in between.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) offers a relatively straightforward
explanation: *yekitzah* is the *keitz*, the end, of one's sleep.

The column offers an alternative of its own: "we may explain" that *yekitzah*
is the end of a dream or other false impression of reality, since most times
its cognates appear in Tanach they concern waking from a sleep with dreams
(Bereishis 28:16, 41:4, 41:7; Melachim I 3:15; Tehillim 73:20; Yeshayahu 29:8)
or a drunkard waking from his stupor (Bereishis 9:24; Mishlei 23:35; Tehillim
78:65; Yoel 1:5).

### oorah (עורה)
"Wake up (*urah*)! Why do You sleep?" (Tehillim 44:24). Root ע-ו-ר. *Shoresh
Yesha* relates all derivatives of ע-ו-ר to revealing and exposing: *ohr* (skin)
is the part of the body left exposed, *ervah* is the exposing of what should be
covered, and a sleeper, eyes shut, has nothing revealed to him, so waking is
*oorah*, an entire world now revealed to him. A blind
man is an *iver* because he sleeps a permanent sleep, or (a second suggestion
there) because a layer of skin sits over his eyes. Pappenheim works the same
biliteral ע-ר and reaches "revealing" too, but applies it differently: the
sleeper's powers are hidden, he lies inert and motionless, and waking reveals
them; on that basis he explains *noer*, a lion's roar, as an outburst that
reveals what was in him.

Pappenheim contrasts it with *yekitzah*: *oorah* is the more gradual waking, a
man slowly passing from asleep to awake through an intermediate stage of
dozing. He grounds it in
*ani yesheinah v'libi er*, "I am asleep but my heart is awake" (Shir HaShirim
5:2): a man can be in *oorah* while he is still, technically, asleep.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865) explains that certain angels are
called *irin* from *er*, "awake," since they are always awake and cognizant of
man's deeds.

The Targumim always render cognates of *yekitzah* by cognates of *oorah*, so
Aramaic collapses the pair; *vayikatz* becomes *v'isar*, the Aramaic TAV
standing in for the Hebrew YOD as the reflexive. The Gemara's term for one
half-asleep is *nim v'lo nim, tir v'lo tir*, "asleep and not asleep, awake and
not awake" (*Pesachim* 120b; *Taanis* 12b). Rashi (to *Taanis* 12b, and see
Rashi to *Bava Kama* 117b) derives *tir* from the Targumic *v'isar* (as though,
the article notes, the tav were part of the root) and reads it as a sleeper on
the edge of waking; Tosafos (to *Taanis* 12b, and see Tosafos to *Niddah* 63a)
explain it the other way, the wakefulness of one about to fall asleep.

### the two together
*Nishmas* has *HaMe'orer yesheinim v'haMeikitz nirdamim*, and pairs two words
for sleeping with the two for waking. Rabbi Yechezkel Pannet of Karlsburg (1783
to 1845), Chief Rabbi of Transylvania, seems to suggest a match: *orer* suits the deep sleeper (*yesheinim*), dreams and
all, and *yekitzah* suits one who merely dozed off (*nirdam*) and never went
under. Alternatively, Malbim explains the two terms as two stages of a gradual
process: *oorah* first, coming to one's senses, then *yekitzah*, actually
getting up from bed and remaining fully awake.
Malbim also reads Chavakuk 2:19 by the sentience of the object: the navi says
*hakitzah* to a wooden idol and *uri* to a stone one, because on the scale of
*domem*, *tzomeach*, *chai* and *medaber* (from "Kabbalistic and philosophical
traditions," the column says), wood ranks above stone, and
*yekitzah* is the more responsive wakefulness while *oorah* is the most basic.

## Rendering guidance

These are our rendering judgments, built on the article's views.

- Default renderings: *vayikatz* "he awoke"; *hekitz / meikitz* "wakes,"
  "rouses from sleep"; *oorah / uri* "awake!" or "rouse yourself"; *me'orer*
  "who rouses"; *er* "awake" as an adjective; *hisorerus* "arousal" or
  "stirring," never "wakefulness."
- Trap: English "awaken" will absorb both roots. Where they stand in one phrase
  (*Nishmas*; Chavakuk 2:19), split them, for example "who rouses the sleeping
  and wakes those who have dozed off." A single verb used twice destroys both
  Rabbi Pannet's reading and Malbim's.
- When *yekitzah* follows a dream or a drunken sleep (Bereishis 28:16, 41:4,
  41:7; Bereishis 9:24; Yoel 1:5), "awoke" is enough; do not add "suddenly."
  Pappenheim's suddenness, for those who follow him, is carried by the word
  itself, and spelling it out in English overtranslates.
- *Hisorerus*, the later term of art for an inner stirring, is not discussed in
  the article. Our suggestion is to keep it transliterated and gloss it once.
- The ק-צ family (*keitz*, *ketzeh*, *kayitz*, *ketzitzah*, *kotz*) is shared
  with the "corner" cluster; see `corner-peah-keren-zavit-pinah.md`, where
  Pappenheim runs the same root through *katzeh*.
