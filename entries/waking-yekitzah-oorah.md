# Waking: yekitzah / oorah

## Bottom line

*Yekitzah* is **waking up**, one clean break from sleep to waking, and it is the
Torah's word for coming out of a dream or a stupor. *Oorah* is **rousing** or
**stirring**, a gradual coming-to that can begin while a man is still asleep.
So *yekitzah* is intransitive and complete; *oorah* is the word for waking
someone else, and for the half-state in between. The trap is that English "awake"
and "awaken" cover both; where a text pairs them (*HaMe'orer yesheinim
v'haMeikitz nirdamim*), the two must be rendered by two different English verbs.

## Distinctions

### yekitzah (יקיצה)
Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and Radak take it from the triliteral י-ק-צ, "wake up,"
and stop there. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) instead files it under
the biliteral ק-צ, which also carries border, summer, pressure, revulsion,
cutting and thorn; he does not say how these join, but the later commentators
supply the link.

Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 9:24) ties *yekitzah* to *kutz* (harvest, cut off) and
*kayitz* (summer): when fruit has ripened it disengages from its source and is
ready to be taken off, and a man who has slept his fill wakes on his own the
same way. To Bereishis 41:4 he adds the *katz* of revulsion: a man who has eaten
his fill is sickened by more food, and a body that has slept its fill likewise
rejects more sleep and wakes.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814) takes the core of ק-צ as "end/edge,"
spatially (*ketzeh*, the edge of a field, Bereishis 23:9; Bamidbar 22:6;
Melachim II 7:8) and temporally (*keitz*, the end of a span, Bereishis 6:13;
Tehillim 119:96; Daniel 11:35, 12:13, and *mikeitz* from it). *Kayitz* is the
last season before Rosh HaShanah. *Ketzitzah* (Shemos 39:3; Devarim 25:12) is
chopping off an end in one stroke rather than the drawn-out work of sawing, and
that is what makes *yekitzah* what it is: a sudden, total waking, asleep one
moment and awake the next, with no half-and-half in between.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) reaches the same place more simply:
*yekitzah* is the *keitz*, the end, of one's sleep.

A usage point worth holding: nearly every cognate of *yekitzah* in Tanach is
waking from a dream (Bereishis 28:16, 41:4, 41:7; Melachim I 3:15; Tehillim
73:20; Yeshayahu 29:8) or from drink (Bereishis 9:24; Mishlei 23:35; Tehillim
78:65; Yoel 1:5). It is the end of a false picture of reality, not merely the
end of sleep.

### oorah (עורה)
Root ע-ו-ר. *Shoresh Yesha* reads every derivative of ע-ו-ר as revealing and
exposing: *ohr* (skin) is the part of the body left exposed, *ervah* is the
uncovering of what should stay covered, and a sleeper, eyes shut, has nothing
revealed to him, so waking is *oorah*, a whole world revealed at once. A blind
man is an *iver* because he sleeps a permanent sleep, or (a second suggestion
there) because a layer of skin sits over his eyes. Pappenheim works the same
biliteral ע-ר and reaches "revealing" too, but applies it differently: the
sleeper's powers are hidden, he lies inert and motionless, and waking reveals
them; on that basis he explains *noer*, a lion's roar, as an outburst that
reveals what was in him.

Pappenheim's contrast with *yekitzah* is the operative one. *Oorah* is the slow
transition, the man in the intermediate stage of dozing. He grounds it in
*ani yesheinah v'libi er*, "I am asleep but my heart is awake" (Shir HaShirim
5:2): a man can be in *oorah* while he is still, technically, asleep.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865) explains the angels called *irin*
from *er*, "awake," since they never sleep and are always aware of what a man
does.

The Targumim always render *yekitzah* by cognates of *oorah*, so Aramaic
collapses the pair; *vayikatz* becomes *v'isar*, the Aramaic TAV standing in for
the Hebrew YOD as the reflexive. From that comes the Gemara's *nim v'lo nim,
tir v'lo tir*, "asleep and not asleep, awake and not awake" (*Pesachim* 120b;
*Taanis* 12b). Rashi (to *Taanis* 12b, and see *Bava Kama* 117b) takes *tir*
from *v'isar* and reads it as a sleeper on the edge of waking; Tosafos (to
*Taanis* 12b, and to *Niddah* 63a) read it the other way, a waking man on the
edge of sleep.

### the two together
*Nishmas* has *HaMe'orer yesheinim v'haMeikitz nirdamim*, and pairs two words
for sleeping with the two for waking. Rabbi Yechezkel Pannet of Karlsburg (1783
to 1845) matches them: *orer* suits the deep sleeper (*yesheinim*), dreams and
all, and *yekitzah* suits one who merely dozed off (*nirdam*) and never went
under. Malbim reads the same phrase as two stages of one process: *oorah* first,
coming to one's senses, then *yekitzah*, actually getting up and staying up.
Malbim also reads Chavakuk 2:19 by the sentience of the object: the navi says
*hakitzah* to a wooden idol and *uri* to a stone one, because on the scale of
*domem*, *tzomeach*, *chai* and *medaber*, wood ranks above stone, and
*yekitzah* is the more responsive wakefulness while *oorah* is the most basic.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *vayikatz* "he awoke"; *hekitz / meikitz* "wakes,"
  "rouses from sleep"; *oorah / uri* "awake!" or "rouse yourself"; *me'orer*
  "who rouses"; *er* "awake" as an adjective; *hisorerus* "arousal" or
  "stirring," never "wakefulness."
- Trap: English "awaken" will absorb both roots. Where they stand in one phrase
  (*Nishmas*; Chavakuk 2:19), split them, for example "who rouses the sleeping
  and wakes those who have dozed off." A single verb used twice destroys both
  Rabbi Pannet's reading and Malbim's.
- When *yekitzah* follows a dream or a drunken sleep (Bereishis 28:16, 41:4,
  41:7; Bereishis 9:24; Yoel 1:5), "awoke" is enough; do not add "suddenly."
  Pappenheim's suddenness is in the word, and spelling it out in English
  overtranslates.
- In mussar and chassidus, *hisorerus* is a term of art for an inner stirring.
  Keep it transliterated and gloss it once; "awakening" reads as an event, and
  *hisorerus* is a process, which is exactly Pappenheim's point.
- The ק-צ family (*keitz*, *ketzeh*, *kayitz*, *ketzitzah*, *kotz*) is shared
  with the "corner" cluster; see `corner-peah-keren-zavit-pinah.md`, where
  Pappenheim runs the same root through *katzeh*.
