# Maybe: ulai / pen

## Bottom line

*Ulai* is **maybe** and *pen* is **lest**. On the view of Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur
and Rabbi Immanuel Benevento, Biblical Hebrew has no neutral "maybe" at all:
*ulai* introduces an outcome the speaker wants, and *pen* one he hopes will not
happen. Malbim writes similarly that *ulai* shows the speaker wants the outcome,
or at least thinks it the likelier. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino holds the opposite,
that *ulai* is neutral, and Rabbi Klein leaves the machlokes standing, noting a
significant problem with HaBachur's and Benevento's view. Two other readings
turn on likelihood: Rabbi Nissim of Marseilles makes *pen* a remote possibility
and *ulai* a likelier one, and *Sefer HaChochmah*, ascribed to the Rokeach,
takes *pen* as what will surely happen, as though it were *ken*. Aramaic *shema*
and *dilma* are neutral on all accounts.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether ulai is neutral.**

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur and Rabbi Immanuel Benevento: no; every *ulai* carries
  the speaker's hope, as every *pen* his fear.
- Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino: *ulai* is neutral, like English "maybe."
- Rabbi Klein leaves the machlokes standing, noting that Yaakov's *ulai*
  (Bereishis 27:12) is a significant problem for the first view.

**Whether pen and ulai differ in likelihood.**

- Malbim: *ulai* is what the speaker thinks the likelier.
- Rabbi Nissim of Marseilles (*Maaseh Nissim* p. 270): *pen* is a remote
  possibility and *ulai* a likelier one, so Hashem's concern that Adam might eat
  from the *Eitz HaChaim* (Bereishis 3:22) is said with *pen*.
- *Sefer HaChochmah*, ascribed to the Rokeach (Bereishis p. 19): *pen* is said of
  what will certainly happen, as though it were *ken*. Rabbi Klein does not know
  the basis, since peh and kaf do not usually interchange.

## Distinctions

### ulai (אולי)
- Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino, a sixteenth-century Italian scholar, writes in *Ohel
  Moed*, his lexicon of Hebrew synonyms, that *ulai* carries a neutral charge;
  English "maybe" is neutral, and so is Aramaic *dilma*/*shema*.
- Two other Italian grammarians of the same generation disagree: Rabbi Eliyahu
  HaBachur (1469-1549) in *Sefer HaTishbi* and Rabbi Immanuel Benevento in
  *Livyat Chen* hold that Biblical Hebrew has no neutral word for "maybe" at
  all, and that *ulai* introduces a possibility the speaker desires.
- Malbim writes similarly: *ulai* implies either a certain desire for that
  outcome or the recognition that it is the likelier of the two to materialize.

To illustrate: "I bought a lottery ticket because *maybe* I will
win" takes *ulai*; "I wear a seatbelt because *maybe* I will get in an
accident" takes *pen*.

Rabbi Klein finds the distinction also somewhat supported by linguists.
Some experts explain *ulai* as a portmanteau of *oh lo* ("or not"), or relate
it to Hebrew *lu* or Aramaic *levai* ("if only").

### pen (פן)

- HaBachur and Benevento: *pen* introduces a possibility the speaker raises in
  order to guard against it, hoping it will not come about.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg ties *pen* to *panah*, "turned away," since the
  speaker hopes his listener will turn away from the possibility he has just
  named.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo* s.v. פן): *pen* is from *pniyah*,
  turning, a doubt as to which way a thing will turn, and not the full doubt of
  *ulai*. *Pen yechereh af Hashem* (Devarim 6:15) cannot mean "perhaps," since
  Hashem's anger at avodah zarah is certain; it is a condition said of what has
  two faces, and it is mostly used of the unworthy face, which is why Chazal
  (*Eruvin* 96a) count *pen* as a negative commandment. It also appears of the
  worthy face (Yeshayahu 6:10), which may be said from the people's own corrupt
  point of view.
- Rabbi Nissim of Marseilles (*Maaseh Nissim* p. 270): *pen* and *ulai* differ
  in likelihood. *Pen* is a remote possibility and *ulai* a likelier one, so
  when Hashem is concerned that Adam might also eat from the *Eitz HaChaim*
  (Bereishis 3:22), a remote chance, the pasuk says *pen*.
- *Sefer HaChochmah*, ascribed to Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms (Bereishis p.
  19): *pen* is said of what will certainly happen, as though it were *ken*.
  Rabbi Klein does not know the basis, since peh and kaf do not usually
  interchange, though they look alike.

### the test case: Bereishis 24:39 and 27:12
The Vilna Gaon made the idea famous by applying it to exegesis, and to Rashi in
particular. Eliezer says *ulai lo seilech
ha'ishah acharai*, "maybe the woman will not follow me" (Bereishis 24:39);
Rashi reads a hidden hope there, that Eliezer wanted his own daughter for
Yitzchak. The Gaon locates Rashi's proof in the word: had Eliezer genuinely
wanted the mission to succeed he would have said *pen*.

Yaakov's *ulai yemusheini avi*, "maybe my father will feel me" (Bereishis
27:12), is what Rabbi Klein calls a significant problem with the way HaBachur,
Benevento and the Vilna Gaon explain *ulai*, since Yaakov would seemingly not
want to be caught.
- A bevy of commentators, among them Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel (1760-1828), Rabbi
  Tzvi Hirsch Chayes (1805-1855), Rabbi Seligman Baer (Yitzchok Dov) Bamberger
  of Würzburg (1807-1878), Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866-1935) and Rabbi
  Zalman Sorotzkin (1881-1966), answer that Yaakov is the one most associated
  with commitment to truth, the navi Michah (7:20) associating him with *emes*
  as he associates Avraham with *chesed*, and that he therefore really did want
  his father to feel him and put an end to the deception; he was so bound to
  truth that passing a lie to his father made him uncomfortable even where it
  was justified.
- Mecklenburg too holds that Yaakov *wanted* his father to feel him, but not so
  as to expose the ploy: he wanted his father's hands laid on his head for the
  *berachah*.
- Alternatively, Rabbi Klein suggests, on Malbim's view no answer is needed,
  since *ulai* need not mean Yaakov wanted it, only that he felt it most likely
  to happen; Rabbi Wertheimer uses a similar argument for the *ulai* of
  Bereishis 18:28.

## Rendering guidance

- אולי: "perhaps" or "maybe." Where a derashah turns on the word, add the
  leaning in a bracket: "perhaps [as he hoped] the woman will not follow me."
- פן: "lest," or "in case," never a bare "maybe." "Lest he stretch out his
  hand" preserves what "maybe he will stretch out his hand" destroys.
- Trap: English "maybe" absorbs both, and a translation that uses it twice in
  one passage erases the whole distinction. If *ulai* and *pen* stand near
  each other, render them with different English words on purpose.
- Aramaic שמא / דלמא in a Gemara passage: "perhaps," neutral. Do not import
  the *ulai* leaning into them.
- Pronunciation of שמא: *shema*, with a kamatz under the mem, is the
  traditional form; Rabbi Meir Mazuz, a contemporary Sephardic grammarian in
  Bnei Brak, claims on the basis of HaBachur's vowelization and other sources
  that the correct pronunciation is *sheme*, with a segol; and the Kaufmann
  Manuscript, the oldest vowelized manuscript of the Mishnah, consistently
  vowelizes *shemay*, with a tzeirei.
