# Dry: yavesh / chorev / negev

## Bottom line

*Charev* is **dried** in the partial sense: the surface has dried but moisture
remains underneath. *Yavesh* is **dry** outright, drained through and through.
The order is fixed: wherever the two stand together, *charev* comes first and
*yavesh* second (Malbim), so a translation must show a progression, not repeat
one English word. *Negev* is **arid** or **parched**, closer to *charev* than to
*yavesh*, and in Tanach it usually means "south" rather than "dry" at all.

## Distinctions

### charev (חרב), chareivah / charavah (חרבה)
The root ח-ר-ב appears over 500 times in Tanach and carries several senses,
among them "sword," "destruction, desolation," and "dry." The Vilna Gaon (to Yeshayahu 8:23): *chorev* means most of the liquid has gone
but some is left; *yavesh* means totally dry. His raya is the drying of the
world after the Mabul, where the two words mark two stages: *charvu hamayim*
and *charvu penei ha'adamah* on the first of the first month, then *yavshah
ha'aretz* on the twenty-seventh of the second (Bereishis 8:13-14). Rashi there
already has it: at *charvu* the ground was "like mud whose upper surface crusted
over," at *yavshah* it was dry land as it ought to be. *Sefer HaChochmah*
(ascribed to Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms) writes that in general *yabashah* is
a place truly dry, while *chareivah* is a muddy place still dripping with
moisture.

The Malbim reaches the same reading and adds the ordering proof: in every pasuk
where the two appear side by side, *charev* precedes *yavesh* (Yeshayahu 19:5,
Iyov 14:11), which shows *yavesh* to be the more intense.

Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer applies it to a halachic difficulty: the Torah
distinguishes a minchah mixed with oil from one that is *chareivah* (Vayikra
7:10), yet no minchah is bone dry. On this reading *chareivah* means only
"oil-free," drier than its neighbor but not absolutely dry, and the Torah's
choice of *chareivah* over *yeveishah* says exactly that. Against this, the
column enters a caution of its own: Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi's lexicon of
rabbinic Hebrew equates *chareivah* in Mishlei 17:1 with *yavesh*, which leaves
open the possibility that the two terms are indeed synonymous.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives two accounts. In *Cheshek Shlomo* and in one place
in *Yerios Shlomo* he takes the difference as quantitative, as above. Elsewhere
in *Yerios Shlomo* he makes it qualitative, keyed to the two kinds of moisture he
distinguishes under *ratuv* and *lach*: *chorev* is dryness on the outside,
*yavesh* is an interior empty of liquid, and in either case it does not matter
whether the thing was ever wet before. A marsh can be called *charavah*, its
surface firm enough to walk on, and can never be called *yavesh*, its inside
still saturated. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg, commenting on Shemos 14:21-22,
where the sea is first made *charavah* by the east wind and Bnei Yisrael then
come into it *bayabashah*, quotes Pappenheim's second explanation and adds
nothing of his own. The reading that the seabed dried in stages, firm on the
surface first and only afterward dry within, is the column's inference about
what Mecklenburg intends, not something Mecklenburg says.

On the root: *Shoresh Yesha* takes ח-ר-ב at core as destruction, the opposite of
living and thriving, which yields both *cherev* (the instrument of destruction)
and dryness (what withers for lack of juice); Pappenheim says something similar.
Rabbi Avraham Maimuni (to Bereishis 31:40) reads *chorev* as dryness caused by
heat, which fits Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim* on ח-ר-ה, where heat is the core
and anger the result. On that footing the column offers two speculations of its
own, both flagged as such. First, that ח-ר-ב may somehow be an offshoot of
ח-ר-(ה), the "destruction" sense alluding to the eventual consequence of
uncontrolled anger and the "sword" being the tool of that destruction; the
column names the downside itself, that most grammarians and lexicographers agree
a *beis* cannot serve as a radical added to a biliteral root. Second, that
ח-ר-ב may be a portmanteau of ח-ר, hole, as in *chor*, and ר-ב, many, as in
*rav*, *harbeh*, *rabbim*: as a thing dries it has less moisture holding it
together and may develop many holes or cracks, and a thing full of holes is that
much more open to destruction, whence also "destruction" and "sword."

### yavesh (יבש), yabashah (יבשה)
Pappenheim: the root is ב-ש, delay or withholding, as in *boshesh Moshe laredes*
(Shemos 32:1), Moshe's return withheld from the people, and as in *bushah*, where
a man withholds his face from the public. *Yavesh* is accordingly the withholding
of the flourishing a thing should have shown; in plants that comes of dryness,
and from there the word covers anything drained of its life-giving liquid. So
Yeravam's hand at the mizbeach in Beis El, *vativash yado* (Melachim I 13:4),
shriveling rather than literally drying.

Rabbi Aharon Marcus writes that the core meaning of ב-ש is finishing or
completing a project, and claims that this original sense is still known to us
through Akkadian. It shows, he says, in two Hebrew roots: י-ב-ש, dryness, the
end or completion of the drying process, and ב-ש-ל, giving *bishul* as cooking
and *bishul* as ripening (Bereishis 40:10), the completion of preparing food.
He also supposes that the *shin* of ב-ש stands for *aish*, fire, alluding to the
part heat plays in cooking, ripening and drying alike. His completion sense
dovetails with the Vilna Gaon and Malbim on *yavesh* as completely dry.

### negev (נגב)
Of its 110 appearances in Tanach nearly all mean "south"; only a few can mean
"dry" (Yehoshua 15:19, Shoftim 1:15, Yeshayahu 21:1, Tehillim 126:4). In
Mishnaic Hebrew, however, its cognates are the ordinary verb for drying
something off (Chagigah 3:1, Avodah Zarah 2:11 and 5:11, Menachos 3:3 and 8:4,
Keilim 25:6, Taharos 2:1, Machshirin 3:5, Mikvaos 10:4, Tvul Yom 3:6, Parah 5:2,
and elsewhere).

Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) observes an asymmetry in Targum: it may
leave *charev* as *charev* (Yeshayahu 19:5) and *yavesh* as *yavesh* (Bereishis
8:14, Shemos 14:22, Yeshayahu 19:5, Iyov 14:11, Tehillim 102:12), and it may
render *charev* as *negev* (Bereishis 8:13, Tehillim 106:9, Iyov 14:11) or as
*yavesh* (Bereishis 7:22, Shemos 14:21), but it never renders *yavesh* as
*negev*. Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev (*Me'at Tzari* to Bereishis 8:13) offers a partial
resolution: *negev* cannot carry the total dryness of *yavesh*, only the partial
dryness of *charev*.

Why *negev* is also "south": Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter holds that in
Hebrew it means only "south" and that the region called the Negev (Bereishis
12:9) is named for the direction, dryness being an Aramaic sense alone; the
column notes that he says this explicitly and adds that he is probably basing
himself on Ibn Ezra there. The column itself calls it most plausible that the
core meaning is indeed "dry," the arid southern region being named for that.
Various explanations are then given for why *negev* also means the direction,
most of them assuming the south always gets more light and heat from the sun
(Ibn Ezra to Bereishis 12:9; *Peirush HaRokeach* there; Rav Hirsch there), but
the column thinks the most reasonable is that once the southern part of Eretz
Yisrael was called the Negev on account of its dryness, the word was borrowed
for the southern direction everywhere.

## Rendering guidance

- Bereishis 8:13-14 and Shemos 14:21-22 both stage the drying. Render the first
  "the waters had drained" or "the surface had dried" and the second "the land
  was dry." Using "dry" twice erases the whole reading of the Vilna Gaon and the
  Malbim, and the staged reading the column draws out of Mecklenburg.
- *Yabashah* as a noun opposite the sea: "dry land." *Charavah* in the same
  role: "dry ground," with the sense of ground firm enough to cross.
- Vayikra 7:10 *chareivah*: "dry," meaning without oil. If the passage is citing
  Wertheimer, say "unmixed with oil" and note the word, since a plain "dry"
  invites the very objection he is answering.
- Trap: English "dry" absorbs all three. When two appear in one pasuk, the first
  must be weaker than the second; when *negev* means the region, leave it as the
  proper name Negev and do not translate it "the dry land," which would collide
  with *yabashah*.
- *Vativash yado* (Melachim I 13:4): "his hand withered." Not "dried," which
  reads literally where the pasuk is figurative.
