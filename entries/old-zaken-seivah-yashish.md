# Old age: zaken / seivah / yashish / yashan / atik / vatik

## Bottom line

The three age words run in ascending order: *zaken* is **an elder** (sixty),
*seivah* is **old age** proper (seventy), *yashish* is **the aged**, older still.
*Yashan* is **old** of plants and produce, not of people ("aged," "last year's").
*Atik* is **ancient**, old by remoteness in time. *Vatik*, on the explanation
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein judges more plausible (that of Rabbi Ernest Klein), is
not an age word at all but means **veteran** or **senior**, of standing and
reliability, not "old."

## Distinctions

### zaken (זקן)
The Mishnah's stage list (*Avos* 5:24) sets *zaken* at sixty and *seivah* at
seventy.

- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini concludes from this, and from two pesukim which
  imply that a man is a *zaken* before he reaches *seivah* (Shmuel I 12:2;
  Yeshayahu 46:4), that *seivah* is the later stage.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (*HaRechasim LeVik'ah*): *zaken* belongs
  to people and animals, not to plants; Iyov 14:8, which calls a plant's root
  *zaken*, is a borrowed usage and not the word's primary sense.
- The Targumim tend to render *zaken* either *kashish* or *sav*.

### seivah (שיבה)
Old age at seventy. Rabbi Eliezer Reines
(*Maftechos HaDamesek*) reads *seivah* as a contraction of *seva yamim*,
"satisfied with days," the phrase Rashi and Bartenura attach to seventy in
*Avos*. Aramaic *sav* (with a samech) is its cousin, and the two stand behind
*saba*, an old man or a grandfather.

### yashish (ישיש)

- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) places *yashish* after *seivah* on the strength of
  Iyov 15:10, so *yashish* marks a more advanced age than *seivah*.
- Bedersi suggests it may name the Mishnah's next stage, ninety, "bent over."
- Radak derives it from *yesh*: one who has existed a very long time.

The word appears only four times in Tanach, all in Iyov (12:12, 15:10, 29:8,
32:6).

### kashish (קשיש)
The Aramaic equivalent, the Targum's standing rendering of *yashish* and one of
its two usual renderings of *zaken*.

- Rabbeinu Chananel (Bava Metzia 109b) and Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer
  HeAruch*): Aramaic *kash* derives from *kashish*, which carries the same sense
  as *yashish*.
- As for how the two words connect etymologically, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
  reports that the only idea he could find is an unlikely one, proposed by Rabbi
  Mordechai Tzvi Herzka (*Divrei Chachamim*): that aleph, yud and kuf can
  interchange because in gematria they are the first number of the ones, the
  tens and the hundreds respectively, so the yud of Hebrew *yashish* becomes the
  kuf of Aramaic *kashish*.

### yashan (ישן)
Old of plants rather than of persons. Shapira-Frankfurter pairs it against
*zaken*: *zaken* for people and animals, *yashan* for plants and other flora.

### atik (עתיק)
Root ayin-tav-kuf, whose primary sense in Tanach is movement in space: Avraham
and later Yitzchak move camp (*vayaatek*, Bereishis 12:8 and 26:22), and a child
is *atik* when "moved away" from nursing (Yeshayahu 28:9). In later usage the
root means to copy or transcribe, as though one took the information from one
document and moved it to another. Because Hebrew presumes a connection between
time and space, *atik* also names what is far removed in time, hence Daniel's
*Atik Yomin*, Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:9 and 7:22). Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
used to think the English *antique* was a corruption of *atik*, until
Rabbi Shaul Goldman told him that linguists parse it otherwise, as the
Proto-Indo-European *ant*, "front, before," plus *okw*, "to see."

### vatik (ותיק)
A *talmid vatik* in the Gemara is a veteran or experienced disciple; *vatikin*
are those who rise to daven at the earliest possible time; and *Yedid Nefesh*,
ascribed to Rabbi Elazar Azikri, addresses Hashem as *Vatik*, which one might
connect with His being called *Atik*. In all these one might be tempted to hear
"old," or at least "old-timer." Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein judges another
explanation more plausible: Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionary,
relates *vatik* to the
Arabic *wathuqa*, "firm, solid," seniority and distinction being what a man
earns by acting dependably and reliably. On that account *vatik* refers to
dependability and standing in a particular position, not to an age.

A popular folk etymology connects *vatikin* to the Vatican, the home of the
Papacy and the Holy See, on the grounds that its leaders too claim to be
dependable and reliable. Serious linguists reject it, and derive Vatican from
the Latin *vates*, soothsayer or prophet, and *cano*, to sing or chant, the site
having housed a pagan oracle before it became the center of Christianity.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "old" absorbs *zaken*, *seivah*, *yashish*, *yashan*, and *atik* at
  once. Where a passage counts stages or builds a series, keep them apart:
  "elder ... old age ... the aged."
- Where זקן and שיבה stand together, render them as an ascent, not as a synonym
  pair; on Bedersi's reading the second is the later stage.
- *Yashan* of produce is "aged" or "last year's." Never use it of a person;
  Shapira-Frankfurter's whole point is that people take *zaken*.
- *Vatik* / *vatikin*: "veteran," "senior," "the punctilious." Writing "the old
  ones" for *vatikin* imports an age the word does not carry.
- *Atik*: "ancient." Reserve it for remoteness in time; where the root is doing
  spatial work, translate the movement ("moved on," "was weaned").
