# Balsam: panag / tzari (nataf, ketaf) / bosem / afarsemon

## Bottom line

*Tzari* is the balsam resin of Tanach and of the *ketores*: render it **balm**
or **balsam**, and keep it distinct from the incense's own biblical name for the
same ingredient, *nataf*, literally **the dripping one**. *Bosem*/*besamim* is
the wide word, **fragrance, spices**, never balsam specifically in Tanach.
*Afarsemon* is the Rabbinic word for **balsam oil**. *Panag* is a *hapax
legomenon* at Yechezkel 27:17; *Yossiphon* reads it as balsam and Rashi cites
that, presumably endorsing it, but
Radak objects that Yechezkel lists *panag* and *tzari* separately, so leave it
untranslated or gloss it. The trap: "balsam" will swallow *tzari*, *panag*,
*bosem* and *afarsemon* at once, flattening a whole disagreement.

## Distinctions

Balsam is a sweet-smelling oil once bound up with Eretz Yisrael. The exact plant
is not known today, though some identify it as *Commiphora gileadensis*, a shrub
native to the land; in ancient times it was famous.

### panag (פנג)
One occurrence, Yechezkel 27:17, among the goods Tyre bought from Eretz Yisrael:
wheat of *Minis*, *panag*, honey, oil and *tzari*. Neither the word nor its root
פ-נ-ג occurs anywhere else, so its sense is not readily
recoverable. *Yossiphon* (I ch. 38), which
calls Yericho *Ir HaReiach*, the City of Scent, for its balsam groves, says
outright that balsam oil
is also called *panag* oil; Rashi cites this (to Yechezkel 27:17, Melachim II
20:13,
Yeshayahu 39:2, *Berachos* 43a), presumably endorsing it, as do the French
exegetes Rabbi Yosef Kara and Rabbi Eliezer of
Beaugency, and Radak (to Yechezkel there and *Sefer HaShorashim*). The column
adds that modern scholars agree *Yossiphon* was probably written in Italy in the
tenth century as a Hebrew adaptation and abridgement of the Jewish Roman
historian Flavius Josephus (37 to 100), and that Josephus himself mentions
Yericho in connection with balsam trees (*Antiquities of the Jews* IV ch. 6 and
XIV ch. 4, *War of the Jews* I ch. 6 and ch. 18) but says nothing about *panag*
in Yechezkel meaning balsam oil.

Against it, Radak himself notes that if *tzari* is balsam
oil then *panag* is unlikely to be, since Yechezkel lists them separately; Rabbi
Yosef Teomim-Frankel (*Iggros Pri Megadim* letter 2) takes that as support for
reading *tzari* as something else (Rashi's "theriaca"). Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi
Ashkenazi and Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto, in their commentaries to Yechezkel,
tie *panag* to *pinuk*, pleasure or indulgence, by the interchange of gimmel and
kuf; the column notes the irony that for all their opposition to Kabbalah,
the Zohar (*Bereishis* 47b, 235b, 245b-246b) corroborates them by likewise
linking *panag* to
*tafnukim*. *Shoresh Yesha* connects it to *oneg*, also pleasure or delicacy,
presumably (the column's word) because ayin and pei are consecutive letters and
so somehow interchangeable. Dr. Zohar Amar of Bar Ilan University, in *Tzimchei
HaMikra*, gathers a further list of explanations from the commentators:
cassia, wax (making *panag* a synonym of *donag*), honeycomb, edible grass,
rice, millet, some sort of pastry or baked good, a kind of
wheat, and a place name (so that the pasuk would mean wheat from there).

### tzari (צרי), nataf (נטף), ketaf (קטף)
*Tzari* (also *tzori* at Yechezkel 27:17, *tzri* at Bereishis 37:25) appears six
times in Tanach: Bereishis 37:25 and
43:11, Yirmiyahu 8:22, 46:11 and 51:8, Yechezkel 27:17. A scented potion used
medicinally. The column adds that it is also possible that the name Tzeruyah,
borne by David's sister, and the name Tzri (Divrei HaYamim I 25:3) derive from
*tzari*. It heads the *ketores* list at *Kerisus* 6a and *Yerushalmi Yoma*
4:5 and so entered the liturgy. Rambam (*Hilchos Klei HaMikdash* 2:4) renders it
in Arabic *al-balasan*, balsam. Its name in the incense passage is *nataf*
(Shemos 30:34), "the one that drips," which suits balsam, whose so-called oil is
sap dripping from the tree; Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, appended to the *ketores*
teaching, says *tzari* is what drips (*notef*) from the *ketaf* tree, and
Rambam's Mishnah commentary at *Sheviis* 7:6 glosses *ketaf* as the balsam tree.
Targum Onkelos renders both *tzari* and *nataf* as *ketaf*; Rabbi Saadia Gaon
and Rabbi Avraham Maimuni render *nataf* as *al-balasan*.

Whether Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel explains the earlier teaching or argues with
it: Rashi (to Shemos 30:34 and *Kerisus* 6a) reads him as explaining; Ramban
(there) reads Rambam as taking him to disagree, which would make *tzari* a piece
of the tree itself rather than its oil (see *Mahari Kurkus* and *Kesef Mishneh*).
*Bereishis Rabbah* 91:11 identifies the *tzari* Yaakov sent to Mitzrayim
(Bereishis 43:11) as *balsam*. That Rabbinic Hebrew word, like the English
*balsam*, comes from the Greek *balsamon* and the Latin *balsamum*; the *Oxford
English Dictionary*, the column adds, derives the English *balm*, an aromatic
resinous substance, and *balmy*, mildly fragrant, from the same terms.

### bosem / besamim (בושם)
The general word, thirty occurrences, for fragrant goods: perfumes, spices,
sweet-smelling things, not balsam in particular. It stands behind the names
Basmas, borne by two of Esav's wives (Bereishis 26:34, 36:3) and by a daughter
of Shlomo (Melachim I 4:15), and Yivsam (Divrei HaYamim I
7:2). It is, the column reports, widely held among linguists that the Greek
*balsamon* derives from *bosem* itself, or at least from a cognate of *bosem* in
another Semitic language (Arabic *basham*, Aramaic *busma*), as many other Greek
plant names are of Semitic origin; the generic Hebrew word for sweet-smelling
goods was seemingly borrowed by the Greeks for one particular sweet-smelling
product, balsam oil.

Ramban (to Shemos 25:6 and 30:34) denies a root ב-ש-ם and reads *bosem* as
a portmanteau of *bo*, in it, and *sam/samim*, potion or elixir. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim similarly
traces it to the biliteral ס-ם, putting a thing in its place, since such tonics
and potions are commonly stored in specifically designated places (the column
notes that the bet is usually treated as a radical in Pappenheim's system of
roots). Either way, when Chazal said one should become *besumei* on Purim
(*Megillah* 7b), this seems to refer to intoxication from wine, the wine serving
as the elixir that brings one to that state; others, including Rabbi Moshe ben
Yekutiel of Rome (*Sefer HaTadir*) and Rabbi Yosef of Saragossa, a talmid of
Rabbeinu Nissim of Gerona, explain it of drinking a quantity of scented (*bosem*)
wine.

On how *bosem* became *balsamon*, the column reports three attempts. Marcus
Jastrow notes that an extra lamed was added to *bosem* to produce *balsamon* but
does not explain where that consonant came from. Dr. Richard C. Steiner of
Yeshiva University accounts for it by arguing that the Hebrew and Semitic sin was
originally pronounced in a way resembling an *l* sound, or was at least
sometimes so perceived by Greeks, hence the *ls* in *balsamon*. Others surmise
that in some unknown early Semitic language *bosem* itself had a lamed before
the sin. Earlier, before the derivation from *bosem* became widely accepted,
Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Mussaf HeAruch*) speculated that Greek *balsamon* came
from the Hebrew *baal shemen*, master of oil, presumably invoking the
interchange of shin and sin; Dr. Alexander Kohut (*HeAruch HaShaleim*) dismissed
that on the ground that *baal shemen* appears nowhere else, and argued instead
for the Hebrew *boles*, cutting, which occurs once in Tanach (Amos 7:14),
together with *shemen*, oil.

### afarsemon (אפרסמון) and opopalsamun
*Afarsemon* (also *afarsema*) is the commonest Rabbinic word for balsam oil. It
is absent from the
Mishnah but appears with some frequency in Gemara: Jewish kings not anointed
with the formal *shemen hamishchah* of the Torah (Shemos 30:22-33)
were anointed with *afarsemon* oil (*Horayos* 11b-12a, *Kerisus* 5b, *Yerushalmi
Shekalim* 6:1 and *Sotah* 8:3); a special berachah on it (*Berachos* 43a); its
being highly flammable
(*Shabbos* 26a); the Daughters of Tzion using it to entice men to sin (*Shabbos*
62b, *Yoma*
9b); its medical use (*Shabbos* 140a, *Avodah Zarah* 30a); and the dream in
which Rabbi Elazar ben Pedas was shown that Hashem wishes to give him thirteen
rivers of balsam oil as his reward in the World to Come (*Taanis* 25a, and see
*Yerushalmi Avodah Zarah* 3:1). It appears in the Midrash too (*Bereishis
Rabbah* 30:8, 61:2; *Vayikra Rabbah* 16:1; *Eichah Rabbah* 4:18). Rashi (to Amos
6:6) calls
it the foremost sort of oil, so much so that an unspecified "oil" is sometimes
read as
*afarsemon* (*Bava Basra* 80b and *Rosh HaShanah* 23a on Yeshayahu 41:19; Rashi
to Mishlei 27:9).

On where *afarsemon* itself comes from, the column reports that scholars hold
the Rabbinic Hebrew *balsam* to have been borrowed straight from Greek, while
the Talmudic *afarsemon* was borrowed from Old Persian, which took it from
Greek: drop the initial alef (often added to foreign words taken into Hebrew),
swap the bet for a pei and the reish for a lamed, both interchangeable pairs,
and *afarsemon* and *balsam* show as the same etymon. Alternatively, the column
allows, *afarsemon* may have come into Persian directly from the Hebrew *bosem*,
with bet for pei and an added reish. Rabbi Yosef Teomim-Frankel roots the word
instead in the Hebrew *pirsum* or *mefursam*, publicized, since
balsam's good scent made it well known throughout the ancient world. In Modern
Hebrew *afarsemon* means
persimmon, a neologism that does not reflect the Rabbinic word: persimmons were
unknown in ancient times, having first been found in North America, and the
English *persimmon* comes from the Algonquin *pasimenan*, dried fruit.

*Opopalsamun* is a third
Rabbinic term, in *Bereishis Rabbah* 39:1 (Avraham likened to *opopalsamun*
sealed in a container in the corner of a room, giving off no scent until someone
moves it about, just as Avraham gave off nothing of his teaching while he stayed
bottled up in Mesopotamia and spread it once Hashem sent him traveling) and
27:3 (its strong smell used to catch and identify a thief who had been breaking
into houses at night). The word is most likely a loan from the Greek
*opobalsamum*, flowing fragrant, and so is bound up in the end with *balsamon*
as well.

## Rendering guidance

- צרי in Chumash and Neviim: "balm" (Bereishis 37:25, 43:11; "is there no balm
  in Gilad," Yirmiyahu 8:22). In the *ketores* list: "balsam," or *tzari* with a
  gloss, since Rashi's theriaca and Rambam's *al-balasan* differ.
- נטף: "the dripping resin," or "*nataf*, the sap that drips." Do not render it
  "balsam"; the word names the manner, and the Targum's *ketaf* names the tree.
- בושם/בשמים: "fragrance," "spices," "perfumes." Never "balsam" in a pasuk.
- אפרסמון: "balsam oil." Never "persimmon."
- Trap: "balsam" absorbs all four. Yechezkel 27:17 has *panag* and *tzari* in
  one list, which is Radak's objection; rendering both "balsam" silently answers
  a question the meforshim left open. Transliterate *panag*.
- Where *bosem* and *afarsemon* stand together, keep "spices" against "balsam
  oil": the *shemen hamishchah* recipe uses *besamim* and did not call for
  balsam, while its informal counterpart did.
