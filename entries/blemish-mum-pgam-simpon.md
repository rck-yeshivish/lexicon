# Blemish: mum / pgam / simpon

## Bottom line

All three are commonly taken to mean **blemish**, and all three mean something
else at root. *Mum* is a **something**, from *meumah*: a small something present that
should not be, or absent that should be, which is why it covers both a lack and
an excess. *Pgam* is a **nick** or **deficiency**, a groove taken out of
something, and by extension a loss of worth or standing. *Simpon* does not mean
blemish at all; it is a **cancelling condition**, whatever can void an
agreement, and a physical defect is only one instance of it. In *Kesubos* 57b *simpon* belongs
to a rule about voidable kiddushin, not a rule about bodies.

## Distinctions

### mum (מום), meumah (מאומה)
The Torah's word for the defect that disqualifies a kohen from serving or an
animal from the mizbeach, with its two lists at Vayikra 21:16 to 23 and 22:17 to
25. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814): the source is the biliteral מ-מ, the
smallest quantity, which gives *meumah*, "something," almost always in the
negative, *lo meumah*, nothing at all (Bereishis 30:31, 39:23; Melachim I
10:21). A *mum*, then, is a "something" whose presence or absence leaves the
thing short of perfect: either lacking what completes it or carrying an extra
that puts it past complete, which is equally a defect.

Rabbi Ernest Klein: *mum* without an aleph likely comes from *mum* with one
(Iyov 31:7; Daniel 1:4) or from *meumah*, a something or a point, first a speck
on an otherwise clean surface and then any flaw. Rashi (to Bereishis 22:12)
already links *mum* and *meumah* by derash. Rav Hirsch reads *meumah* as the
smallest possible scrap of existence, a something barely larger than nothing,
from the root א-מ, which gives *eim*, mother, the source of existence, and *im*,
"if," the precondition for anything existing at all.

Beyond the physical, *mum* is used for a defect from sin (Devarim 32:5; Mishlei
9:7), and in the Gemara for what disqualifies a man from sitting as a dayan,
where Rashi (to *Sanhedrin* 36b) explains it as a family *pgam*, a more abstract
use than the biblical one.

### pgam (פגם)
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur: the literal sense is a groove or crevice. So the *pgam*
of the moon, the darkened part visible at certain points in its cycle (*Rosh
HaShanah* 23b), and the nick in a knife that makes it unfit for shechitah
(*Chullin* 10a, 17a). In that light, Rabbi Ernest Klein's contention that *pgam*
is probably a cognate of the Arabic *fajama*, to break off a bit, makes good
sense to Rabbi Klein. (Neither word, he adds, has anything to do with the English
"pajama," which comes from the Persian *pay*, leg, and *jameh*, garment.)
Pappenheim traces it instead to the biliteral פ-ג, weakening: *vayafag libo*,
Yaakov's heart went weak when told Yosef was alive (Bereishis 45:26), and *pag*,
the unripe fig whose sweetness is still weak (Shir HaShirim 2:13); in modern
Hebrew *pag* is a premature baby, and *pag* also gives the expiration date of, say,
a coupon. On that reading every rabbinic use of *pgam* is a weakening of one kind
or another, in taste, in a knife's blade, or in a girl's worth.

The rabbinic uses all sit on "lacking" rather than "blemish":

1. *Yoma* 70a: the Kohen Gadol reads the second passage by heart rather than
   take out a second sefer Torah, lest people suspect the first had a *pgam*.
   Rashi: they would think it lacked what makes a sefer fit.
2. *Kesubos* 84a: a family *pgam* is something shameful that detracts from a
   family's standing.
3. *Sanhedrin* 73a: the rapist has caused the betrothed woman a *pgam*; Rashi,
   he has shamed her and cheapened her. The Mishnah (*Kesubos* 3:4) lists *pgam*
   among the payments due, and (3:7) fixes it by comparing a besulah's
   theoretical price on the slave market with a non-besulah's, the drop in value
   paid as damage.
4. *Pesachim* 106a: a cup of wine that is less than full is *pagum* and unfit for
   kiddush.
5. *Kesubos* 9:7 to 8 and Tosefta *Shevuos* 6:5: one who takes partial payment
   on a loan document is *pogem* it, reducing what it is worth.
6. *Avodah Zarah* 67a: *taam lifgam*, a forbidden taste that detracts from the
   permitted food rather than improving it, does not prohibit it.

### simpon (סימפון)
The Mishnah (*Kesubos* 5:3) records that originally a woman betrothed to a kohen
could eat terumah at once, and that later courts decreed she may not until the
marriage is complete. The Gemara (*Kesubos* 57b) gives as one of the reasons for
that decree the suspicion of a *simpon* that would void the kiddushin
retroactively. From this comes the common misconception that *simpon* means
blemish. It does not. The word carries three distinct senses, all from Greek, and Rabbi
Binyamin Mussafia (1606 to 1675) notes that Greek terms had entered Aramaic
already in the days of Daniel, citing *sumponia* there (Daniel 3:5, 3:10, 3:15).

1. A bronchial vessel branching from the lung (Mishnah *Chullin* 3:1), from
   *siphon*, a pipe.
2. A musical instrument of some sort, possibly a bagpipe or a virginal (Daniel;
   Mishnah *Keilim* 11:6), from *symphonia*; the *Sefer HaAruch* describes it as
   a wind instrument built on a hollow pipe, which joins this sense to the first.
3. Additional clauses appended to a legal document (Mishnah *Bava Metzia* 1:8),
   from *symphoneo*, agreement, and from there any stipulation, even one not
   written into the document, that can invalidate it.

Rashi (to *Kiddushin* 10b, *Kesubos* 57b, *Bava Metzia* 20a): *simpon* means to
cancel, and covers any clause capable of cancelling a deal, express or implied.
The early commentary on Onkelos ascribed to Rabbi Yaakov Dienna (printed as
*Patshegen*, *Tzintzenes HaMan*, and *Sefer HaYair*) offers a Semitic derivation
instead, from ס-י-מ, erasing or destroying, with *pon* somehow pointing back to
something from the past (the column flags that step as unexplained), and reaches
the same result as Rashi: retroactive cancellation. On either
account a physical defect in a kallah is one thing that can trigger a *simpon*,
not what the word means.

## Rendering guidance

- Default renderings: *mum* "blemish" (and "defect" for a kohen or an animal);
  *pgam* "flaw," "nick," "deficiency," or "loss of standing" by context;
  *simpon* "voiding condition," "cancellation," or the transliteration with a
  gloss.
- Trap: English "defect" will take *mum* and *pgam* both. Where a source glosses
  one by the other (Rashi to *Sanhedrin* 36b explaining *mum* as a family
  *pgam*), keep two English words and let the gloss do its work: "a *mum*, that
  is, a family flaw."
- Second trap, the important one: never render *simpon* "blemish." In *Kesubos*
  57b the concern is a condition that unwinds the kiddushin retroactively.
  "A defect that would void the betrothal" is right; "a blemish" is not.
- *Pagum* of a cup is "used" or "diminished," not "blemished," and *taam
  lifgam* is "a taste that detracts," a settled halachic idiom worth keeping
  transliterated with that gloss.
- *Meumah* in a pasuk is "anything," almost always inside a negative: *lo
  chasarta meumah*, "you lacked nothing." Do not render it "a blemish" even
  where a derashah connects the words; give the derashah in a note.
