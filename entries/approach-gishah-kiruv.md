# Approach: gishah / kiruv

## Bottom line

*Gishah* is **to approach** in the strong sense: coming right up to something
(Pappenheim, Wertheimer), and usually in awe of it or across a gap of standing
(Shapira-Frankfurter, Malbim). *Kiruv* is **to draw near** or **bring closer**,
a step taken while still on the way (Pappenheim, Wertheimer), between parties on
a level (Malbim). So when Yehudah *vayigash* to the viceroy (Bereishis 44:18),
it seems to Rabbi Klein that he came up close and personal rather than merely
nearer, perhaps even in a threatening way, as certain midrashic sources imply.
In line with Rav Hirsch, for whom *gishah* is a move toward a specific goal, it
also seems to Rabbi Klein that Yehudah approached very deliberately, as a
further step in his campaign for Binyamin's release. In Aramaic all of this is
lost: Targum typically renders both words by *kiruv* cognates.

## Where the authorities disagree

Three accounts of the same contrast, and they do not reduce to one.

1. **Awe and standing.** Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743-1826),
   Rav Hirsch's great-uncle: *gishah* is
   approaching with trepidation, as one approaches a king or a dignitary. This
   explains why a
   litigant *nigash* before a judge (Devarim 25:1) while Hashem coming to judge us
   is *kiruv* (Malachi 3:5): He approaches without fear. Malbim says the same in
   other terms, that *gishah* fits where there is a gap in power between the two.
2. **How far along the approach is.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and Rabbi Shlomo
   Aharon Wertheimer: *kiruv* is coming closer, *gishah* is arriving as close as
   possible. Rabbi Pappenheim's illustration is Vayikra 2:8, where the owner
   *hikrivah* the minchah to the kohen and the kohen *higishah* it to the
   mizbeiach: since the donor's goal is the mizbeiach, the first step brings it
   nearer the goal with a way still to go, and the second reaches it. Rabbi
   Wertheimer takes up the same approach and offers various proof-texts of his own
   from Tanach.
3. **Physical against abstract, and who can do it.** Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi
   Mecklenburg: *gishah* is bodily closeness and *kiruv* a meeting of minds, so a
   litigant "brought near" to the court (Shemos 22:7) is composing himself to
   stand there, and a *korban* brings a man's will into line with Hashem's, since
   no physical approach to Him is possible, since He is incorporeal. Rav Hirsch
   (to Vayikra 21:17) takes the opposite line: *kiruv* is absolute nearness, the
   antonym of *richuk*, absolute farness, so that *karov* names the mathematical
   difference between one location's coordinates and another's and *kiruv* is
   simply bridging that distance, which is why the verb is used of animals as well
   as people; *gishah*, by contrast, appears in Tanach only of human beings,
   because it is a step forward toward a specific goal, which only a being able to think and decide for itself can take
   (see also Abarbanel to *Moreh Nevuchim* 1:18).

Rav Hirsch's point about purpose is what, as it seems to Rabbi Klein, explains
the deliberateness of Yehudah's *vayigash*.
Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg's abstract/physical axis runs against Rav Hirsch.

## Distinctions

### gishah (גישה)
Root נ-ג-ש. Avraham *vayigash* to argue for Sedom (Bereishis 18:23); Yehudah
*vayigash* to Yosef (Bereishis 44:18); the litigant approaching the court
(Devarim 25:1). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ג-ש, closeness
so complete that the parties touch, as in *nigsheshah*, the blind man groping for
what he cannot see (Yeshayahu 59:10). Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg follows this
into contact: Targum Yerushalmi renders an ox goring as *yigash* (Shemos 21:28),
and a boat scraping the seabed in shallow water is *goshesh* (Bava Kamma 116b;
Bava Metzia 79b). The same root gives *gush*, a compacted clod of earth (Iyov
7:5), which Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim suggests may lie behind the name Goshen,
the one part of Egypt with packed soil rather than sand, and behind *geshem*: in
medieval usage any solid whose particles are bound tight, hence *gashmiyus*, and
in the sense of rain, the thick drops that fall from a dense cloud. Rabbi Yehudah
Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*) ties *geshem* to *gishah* the other way:
the drops never do approach one another as they fall.

### kiruv (קירוב)
Root ק-ר-ב, with *karov* "near" and *korban* the offering. The root also gives
*krav*, war. The classical lexicographers (Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak)
present approaching, innards and war as distinct meanings of ק-ר-ב, but Rabbi
Klein finds the line blurrier: the closer one approaches something, the nearer one
comes to entering its perimeter and reaching its innards, and in battle the
attackers approach while the defenders guard what lies within their line. Some
verbs of ק-ר-ב that literally mean approaching refer in context to potential
battle (Shemos 14:10; Devarim 2:19, 20:2-3, 20:10). Its range in Tanach
includes approaching food in order to eat it (Vayikra 22:3), approaching a person
in order to harm him (Tehillim 32:9), and approaching a woman for intimacy
(Bereishis 20:4), which Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg allows as *kiruv* standing
in for the goal it aims at. Ramban (in his notes to the Rambam's *Sefer
HaMitzvos*, lo saaseh 353) observes that both words are used of intimacy: *kiruv*
at Devarim 22:14 and Yeshayahu 8:3, *gishah* at Shemos 19:15. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi
Mecklenburg reads the choice of *kiruv* in the arayos parashah (Vayikra 18:6) as
load-bearing: the Torah forbids any coming nearer, the closest approach included, which is the Rambam's source that hugging and kissing are forbidden
mid'oraisa.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: גישה "approached," קרב "drew near" or "brought near." Where the two
  stand in one pasuk, as at Vayikra 2:8, the English must show two stages: "he
  brings it near to the kohen, and the kohen brings it up to the mizbeiach."
- Trap: English "approach" and "come close" will each cover both words. If a
  passage argues from the Torah's choice of one over the other (Devarim 25:1
  against Malachi 3:5; Vayikra 18:6), the rendering has to keep them apart or the
  argument evaporates.
- ויגש in Bereishis 44:18 is "then Yehudah came right up to him," not "approached
  him," if the derashos on his boldness are in play.
- קרבן stays "*korban*" or "offering," never "sacrifice of drawing near"; the
  etymology belongs in a note.
- Do not import the contrast into Aramaic. Targum renders both by *kiruv*
  cognates (Bereishis 18:23, 33:6, 44:18 for *gishah*; Bereishis 20:4, Shemos
  14:10, Vayikra 1:2 for *kiruv*), so a Targum-based argument cannot carry it.
- Modern *kiruv*, "outreach," is a borrowed use; keep it transliterated and do not
  read it back into a pasuk.
