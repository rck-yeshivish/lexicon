# Theft: genevah / gezeilah / chamas / listim

## Bottom line

*Genevah* is theft **by stealth**: the *ganav* hides himself, so render it
**stealing** and the actor **thief**. *Gezeilah* is taking **openly**, in public
or by force, so render it **robbery** and the actor **robber**. The distinction
is not severity but visibility, and the halachah runs the opposite way to
intuition: the Torah imposes extra fines on the *ganav* (double, and four or
five times for a slaughtered or sold animal) and none on the *gazlan*, because
the *ganav* shows more respect for man than for Hashem (Bava Kamma 79b).
*Chamas* appears in Tanach as theft (Rashi to Bereishis 6:13) but not always
exclusively so, sometimes as general **violence** or **injustice**; in Chazal a
*chamsan* is a coercive buyer who does pay, a wrongdoer but not technically a
thief or robber. *Listim* is a Greek loanword for a **robber** or brigand, and
whether an armed *listim* counts as *ganav* or *gazlan* is disputed among the
authorities.

## Distinctions

### genevah (גניבה)
The Torah's *lo tignovu* (Vayikra 19:11) forbids this class, and it mandates
repayment by the *ganav* at Shemos 22:3. The two prohibitions carry different
rules: the Gemara's list of twenty-four kinds of damage counts *genevah* and
*gezeilah* as two items (Bava Kamma 4b), and Rambam's *Sefer Nezikin* gives
Hilchos Geneivah and Hilchos Gezeilah separate sections. Bava Kamma 79b: the
*ganav* conceals himself from his victim and takes in a stealthier, sneakier
way. The *S'ma* (to Choshen Mishpat 348:7) adds that even if the victim ends up
seeing him, the fact that he *tried* to hide is enough to make him a *ganav*.
Rambam (Hilchos Geneivah 1:3): he takes another's property clandestinely so that
the owner does not know, as a pickpocket does; if he took it openly, with
violence or by force, he is not a *ganav* but a *gazlan*.

The *ganav* alone bears the fines (the article cites Shemos 21:37): besides
returning the value he pays an extra penalty of the same value, double in all,
and for a kosher animal slaughtered or sold, five times for cattle and four for
sheep or goats. Bava Kamma 79b gives the reason, and it is the heart of this
cluster: the *ganav* hides his theft from people but does not care to hide it
from Hashem, so he shows he respects man more than Hashem and cares more what
people think. The *gazlan* does not care what anybody thinks, but at least he
does not give man more respect than Hashem, and so he is exempt from the
penalties. (By Torah tradition the *lo tignov* of the Aseres HaDibros, Shemos
20:13 and Devarim 5:17, is kidnapping, not ordinary theft.)

### gezeilah (גזילה)
*Lo tigzol* (Vayikra 19:13), with repayment at Vayikra 5:23. Bereishis Rabbah
54:3: *gezeilah* is taking in public, out in the open; one opinion requires that
he take it in front of ten people, and if only nine he is "only" a *ganav*;
another opinion requires that he come face to face with the victim and grab the
item from his hand. Rambam (Hilchos Gezeilah 1:3) illustrates: grabbing movable
goods from a man's hand (see Bava Basra 34a), entering his property without
permission and taking (see Shevuos 44b), overpowering his slaves or animals and
making them work (see Bava Kamma 97a), going into his field and eating his
produce (see Bava Basra 38a).

Ernest Klein (no relation to the columnist) relates *gezel* to the Arabic
*jazala*, "cut off," a violent ripping of the object out of its rightful
owner's hands. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim and others give the two-letter ג-ז the
sense of shaving or trimming, a cut that leaves some parts attached and some
detached: *geiz* (the grass left after trimming, Tehillim 72:6), *gozez*
(shearing, Bereishis 31:19, 38:12), *gazam* (a locust that eats some produce and
leaves the rest), *geza* (a tree with its top cut off), *gazis* (hewn stone).
Rabbi Pappenheim does not connect *gezel* to this root, since the lamed does not
fit his theory; Rabbi Klein argues it fits all the same, robbery taking one
possession while leaving the rest intact. Similarly, Rav Hirsch (to Shemos
23:19): *gozel*, a fledgling, is cognate with *gezel*, because taking the bird
from the nest severs its bond with its mother as theft severs the bond between
an object and its owner.

Mnemonic, from Rabbi Klein's fifth-grade rebbi, Rabbi A. Y. Berman: the *gaNav*
steals at **N**ight, when nobody is looking, the *gazLan* in the **L**ight.

### chamas (חמס)
*Chamas* also appears in Tanach in the sense of theft: the generation of the
Flood was destroyed because "the land was filled with *chamas*" (Bereishis
6:13), which Rashi, following Sanhedrin 108a, explains as theft. But the word
and its cognates, some sixty times in Tanach, do not always refer exclusively to
stealing; sometimes they are general **violence** and **injustice**. Indeed,
Dr. Chaim Tawil relates Hebrew *chamas* to the Akkadian *hamasu*, "to oppress"
or "to do wrong." Bava Kamma 62a defines the *chamsan* narrowly: he is a
coercive buyer, who takes the object from his victim but gives him money,
still a wrongdoer yet not technically a robber or a thief. Rabbi Klein adds, in
passing, that in Arabic the word is related to the Hebrew *chamesh*, five, which
might allude to the thief's proverbial "five-finger discount," and that
*chamas* is not etymologically connected to the name of the terrorist
organization Hamas.

Rashi (to Yoma 39a and Tehillim 71:4) and Radak (to Tehillim 71:4 and in
*Sefer HaShorashim*) equate *chometz*/*chamtzan* with *gazlan* and *chamsan*,
Radak explaining it by the interchange of tzadi and samech. The Netziv writes
that *chamtzan* does not technically mean a thief; rather he is akin to a thief,
one who took from something he rightfully deserved but took more than his due.
(In Modern Hebrew *chamtzan* is oxygen, for unrelated reasons.)

### listim (לסטים)
Mishnaic Hebrew from the Greek *leistes*, "robber." *Listim*/*listin* is the
singular, but since its ending resembles a plural suffix it was also borrowed
for multiple robbers, and Rabbinic Hebrew derived verbs from it (*lilastem*, to
rob). (The Greek *-lestes* serves in English as a suffix in the scientific names
of predatory animals.) Based on an uncertainty in Bava Kamma 57a, the
authorities dispute whether an armed *listim* is a *ganav* or a *gazlan*
(*Kesef Mishneh*, *Lechem Mishneh*, *Even HaAzel* to Hilchos Geneivah 1:3): he
takes openly and the victim knows, like a *gazlan*, but he carries a weapon,
which suggests he is afraid of being caught, like a *ganav*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Genevah* / *ganav*: **stealing**, **theft**, **thief**. *Gezeilah* /
  *gazlan*: **robbery**, **robber**. Hold this pairing everywhere; the fines,
  the derashos, and the mussar all hang on it.
- *Chamas* in a pasuk: decide by context, "robbery" or "theft" where it is fixed
  on stealing (Bereishis 6:13 with Rashi), **violence** or **injustice** where
  it is not (a judgment call). The Rabbinic *chamsan* is neither thief nor
  robber: **a coercive buyer**, or leave it transliterated.
- *Listim*: **brigand** or **armed robber**; "bandit" also works. Do not resolve
  him into *ganav* or *gazlan* in the English, since the authorities dispute it.
- Trap: English "theft" and "stealing" will absorb *genevah* and *gezeilah*
  together, and the whole passage in Bava Kamma 79b becomes unreadable. When
  both stand in one sentence, take "steal" for the first and "rob" or "seize by
  force" for the second, and never let "steal" do both jobs.
- Trap: "robbery" is also the natural English for *chamas* in Bereishis 6:13,
  which then collides with *gezeilah*. If both words are live in the passage,
  render *chamas* "lawlessness" or "violent wrongdoing" and reserve "robbery"
  for *gezeilah*.
