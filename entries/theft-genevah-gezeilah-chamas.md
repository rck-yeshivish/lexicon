# Theft: genevah / gezeilah / chamas / listim

## Bottom line

*Genevah* is theft **by stealth**: the *ganav* hides himself, so render it
**stealing** and the actor **thief**. *Gezeilah* is taking **openly and by
force**, so render it **robbery** and the actor **robber**. The distinction is
not severity but visibility, and the halachah runs the opposite way to
intuition: the Torah fines the *ganav* double and the *gazlan* not at all.
*Chamas* is broader, **violence** or **injustice**, sometimes theft; in Chazal a
*chamsan* is a coercive buyer who does pay, so he is a wrongdoer but not a
thief. *Listim* is a Greek loanword for an armed **brigand**, and whether he
counts as *ganav* or *gazlan* is left open.

## Distinctions

### genevah (גניבה)
The Torah's *lo tignovu* (Vayikra 19:11) forbids this class, and the Torah
mandates repayment for the *ganav* at Shemos 22:3. Bereishis Rabbah 54:3 and
Bava Kamma 79b: the *ganav* conceals himself from his victim and takes in a
sneaky way. The *S'ma* (to Choshen Mishpat 348:7) adds that even if the victim
ends up seeing him, the fact that he *tried* to hide is enough to make him a
*ganav*. Rambam (Hilchos Geneivah 1:3): he takes another's property clandestinely
so that the owner does not know, as a pickpocket does.

The *ganav* alone bears the fines: double the value of what he stole (Shemos
21:37), and four or five times over for a slaughtered or sold animal. Bava Kamma
79b gives the reason, and it is the heart of this cluster: the *ganav* hides
from people but not from Hashem, so he shows he fears man more than his Creator.
The *gazlan* fears nobody, but at least he does not honor man above Hashem, and
so he goes free of the fines. (By Torah tradition the *lo tignov* of the Aseres
HaDibros, Shemos 20:13 and Devarim 5:17, is kidnapping, not ordinary theft.)

### gezeilah (גזילה)
*Lo tigzol* (Vayikra 19:13), with repayment at Vayikra 5:23. Bereishis Rabbah
54:3: *gezeilah* is taking in public, out in the open; one opinion requires that
he take it in front of ten people, and if only nine he is merely a *ganav*;
another opinion requires that he come face to face with the victim and snatch
the item from his hand. Rambam (Hilchos Gezeilah 1:3) illustrates: snatching
movable goods from a man's hand (Bava Basra 34a), entering his property
uninvited and taking (Shevuos 44b), overpowering his slaves or animals and
making them work (Bava Kamma 97a), going into his field and eating his produce
(Bava Basra 38a).

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives the two-letter ג-ז the sense of shaving or
trimming, a cut that leaves part attached and part detached: *geiz* (the grass
left after mowing, Tehillim 72:6), *gozez* (shearing, Bereishis 31:19, 38:12),
*gazam* (a locust that eats some and leaves the rest), *geza* (a stump), *gazis*
(hewn stone). He does not extend this to *gezel* himself, since the lamed does
not fit his theory; Rabbi Klein suggests it fits all the same, robbery severing
one possession while leaving the rest. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 23:19) makes the
same move from the other side: *gozel*, a fledgling, is cognate with *gezel*
because taking the bird from the nest severs its bond with its mother as theft
severs the bond between an object and its owner. Rabbi Ernest Klein takes *gezel*
as related to a root meaning "cut off," a violent ripping of the object out of
its owner's hands.

Mnemonic, from Rabbi A. Y. Berman: the *gaNav* steals at **N**ight, the
*gazLan* in the **L**ight.

### chamas (חמס)
Some sixty appearances in Tanach, and not always theft; often plain **violence**
or **injustice**. The generation of the Flood was destroyed because "the land was
filled with *chamas*" (Bereishis 6:13), which Rashi, following Sanhedrin 108a,
takes as robbery. But Bava Kamma 62a defines the Rabbinic *chamsan* narrowly: he
is a coercive buyer, who takes the object against the owner's will but pays him
money. A wrongdoer, yet not technically a thief or a robber.

Rashi (to Yoma 39a and Tehillim 71:4) and Radak (there and in *Sefer
HaShorashim*) equate *chometz*/*chamtzan* with *gazlan* and *chamsan*, Radak
noting the interchange of tzadi and samech. The Netziv narrows it differently: a
*chamtzan* is not a thief at all but one who took something he was genuinely
owed and took more than his due.

### listim (לסטים)
Mishnaic Hebrew from the Greek *leistes*, "robber." The singular form ends like
a plural, so it was pressed into service for a band of robbers too, and Rabbinic
Hebrew derived verbs from it (*lilastem*, to rob). Whether an armed *listim* is
a *ganav* or a *gazlan* turns on an unresolved question in Bava Kamma 57a and is
disputed by the authorities (*Kesef Mishneh*, *Lechem Mishneh*, *Even HaAzel* to
Hilchos Geneivah 1:3): he takes openly and the victim knows, like a *gazlan*,
but he carries a weapon, which shows he fears being caught, like a *ganav*.

## Rendering guidance

- *Genevah* / *ganav*: **stealing**, **theft**, **thief**. *Gezeilah* /
  *gazlan*: **robbery**, **robber**. Hold this pairing everywhere; the fines,
  the derashos, and the mussar all hang on it.
- *Chamas* in a pasuk: **violence** unless the context (Bereishis 6:13 with
  Rashi) fixes it on theft, and then "robbery" with a note. The Rabbinic
  *chamsan* is neither: **a coercive buyer**, or leave it transliterated.
- *Listim*: **brigand** or **armed robber**; "bandit" also works. Do not resolve
  him into *ganav* or *gazlan* in the English when the sugya leaves him open.
- Trap: English "theft" and "stealing" will absorb *genevah* and *gezeilah*
  together, and the whole passage in Bava Kamma 79b becomes unreadable. When
  both stand in one sentence, take "steal" for the first and "rob" or "seize by
  force" for the second, and never let "steal" do both jobs.
- Trap: "robbery" is also the natural English for *chamas* in Bereishis 6:13,
  which then collides with *gezeilah*. If both words are live in the passage,
  render *chamas* "lawlessness" or "violent wrongdoing" and reserve "robbery"
  for *gezeilah*.
