# Document: gett / shtar (and the biblical sefer kerisus)

## Bottom line

Neither *gett* nor *shtar* is biblical. In the language of Chazal both mean
**document**, and both often take a modifier (*gett chov*, *shtar erusin*).
*Gett* is the broader of the two for Rabbi Yisrael Yaakov Algazi: every *shtar*
may be called a *gett*, not every *gett* a *shtar*. Unmodified, *gett* means
**a bill of divorce** (Rashi; Rambam). In Tanach the bill of divorce is *sefer
kerisus*, literally **a scroll of cutting** (Devarim 24:1-3; Yeshayahu 50:1), and
a deed of sale is *sefer hamiknah* (Yirmiyahu 32).

## Distinctions

### gett (גט)
Technically any document. *Gett ishah*, a "woman's *gett*," is a writ of
divorce; *gett shichrur*, a "*gett* of freedom," a slave's writ of emancipation;
*gett chov*, an "obligatory *gett*," an IOU (Gittin
10a; Bava Kamma 95a; Bava Basra 160a). Rashi (Gittin 65b) and Rambam
(Commentary to the Mishnah, Gittin 2:5) record that the default *gett* in
colloquial terms is a bill of divorce. Rabbi Yisrael Yaakov Algazi adds, as an
aside, that every *shtar* can also be called a *gett* but not every *gett* a
*shtar*, and rules from that principle that communal enactments explicitly said
to govern a *shtar* do not apply to a *gett*. Why this word, and why for
divorce, is the subject of a long disagreement.

- **Twelve lines.** Tosafos (Gittin 2a) cite Rabbeinu Tam that a *gett* carries
  twelve lines because the *gematria* of גט is twelve. The Maharsha, Maharam
  Shiff, and *Elyah Rabbah* read Tosafos as explaining the *name* as well; the
  *Tosafos Yom Tov* objects that this reads more into Tosafos than the words
  bear (see Rabbi David Tzvi Fetene, *Maharsha HaAruch*, who answers him). Rabbi
  Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) raises two objections of his own: other
  documents are called a *gett* without the twelve-line requirement, and other
  two-letter pairs also total twelve (דח, הז, טג).
- **Letters that never touch.** Rabbi Chaim of Friedburg (*Sefer HaChaim*),
  brother of the Maharal, is the earliest known source: Hashem so dislikes
  divorce that He kept *gimmel* and *tes* from ever standing in that order
  anywhere in Tanach. Rabbi Algazi heard the same, tying it to divorce as
  separation, and the Vilna Gaon (*Divrei Eliyahu*) says the like. Rabbi Meir
  Mazuz sharpens it: other never-adjacent pairs (גק, סצ) are letters from the
  same organ of speech and hard to pronounce, while *gimmel* and *tes* come from
  different organs yet still never stand in that order, and they are the
  earliest incompatible pair in alphabetical order. Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky (*Taama DeKra*
  to Devarim 24:1) offers that last point too. The pair does occur across a word
  boundary at Yechezkel 22:25; see Rabbi Aryeh Leib Tzintz's introduction to
  *Seder HaGett*.
- **The stone.** Rabbi Yehoshua Boaz (*Shiltei HaGibborim* on Mordechai,
  Gittin 1) cites a Midrash no longer extant about a gem called *getta* that
  repels people, fitting a document by which a couple push one another away. The
  Chasam Sofer knows a version in which the stone *attracts*, and settles it
  from the other *Shiltei HaGibborim* (Rabbi Avraham the Physician of Portaleone)
  on the stone "gagate" that is said to draw straw when rubbed. On that reading every
  document is a *gett* because it binds parties together, and the divorce sense
  is the harder one, which is why the twelve-line explanation was needed.
- **Cutting or erasing.** *Tosafos Chachmei Anglia* (Gittin 2a), *Orchos Chaim*
  (Hilchos Gittin), *Kolbo* 76, the glosses to Rabbi Yaakov Margolis's *Seder
  HaGett*, and the *Levush* (Even HaEzer 125:11) all take *gett* as an
  expression of breaking or cutting, matching the biblical *sefer kerisus*, and
  cite a lost Yerushalmi that uses it so. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer
  similarly suggests it is an expression of erasure (compare Rashi to Berachos
  56a).
- **Akkadian.** Despite all of these explanations, the
  scholarly consensus is that the Mishnaic Hebrew *gett* is a loanword from the
  Akkadian *gittu*, "a (single-column) document."

On the word *ghetto*, the section of Rome to which the Jews were confined, the
late Rabbi Eliezer Herstik wrote that some explain it derives from *gett*, in
the sense of "chased away." Three other theories stand beside it:
that *ghetto* comes from the Italian *borghetto*, "small part of a city," from
the Germanic *burg*, "fortress, city, town," the equivalent of English
"borough" and the Old English suffix *-bury*; that it comes from the Venetian
*getto*, "metal foundry," there having been such a factory near the Jewish
ghetto in Venice; and that it comes from the Latin *Aegyptus*, Egypt, where the
Jews were first ghettoized in exile and slavery in biblical times.

### shtar (שטר)
Root ש-ט-ר. Absent from Tanach but quite common in later rabbinic literature,
including the Bar Kochba Letters and the Mishnah, and often specified by its
type: *shtar chov*, *shtar erusin* (Kesuvos 102b; Kiddushin 9b), *shtar nisuin*
and *shtar arisus* (Bava Basra 167b), *shtar amanah* (Kesuvos 19a; Gittin 19b;
Bava Basra 49a, 154b), *shtar berurin* (Moed Katan 18b), *shtar mivrachas*
(Kesuvos 79a). Ritva distinguishes a *shtar* from an *iggeret*: a *shtar* is
written with the intention that it last a long time (ostensibly so as to serve as
evidence of the transaction or event it records), while an *iggeret* is not
intended as long-term documentation. Whether it is the same root as *shoter*, the officer, is
disputed.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*) gives ש-ט-ר two separate entries, the
  enforcing officer and the document, and treats the two senses as separate.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 5:6) joins them: a *shtar* is the creditor's best means
  of presenting his grievances and enforcing his dues, so both words off ש-ט-ר
  are related.
- Dr. Chaim Tawil defines the biblical *shoter* as a "record-keeper" or
  "organizing officer" and connects the root to the Akkadian verb *sataru*, "to
  write"; on his account both *shoter* and *sofer* mean a scribe in Akkadian,
  the former specifically a civil servant who is an official scribe, the latter
  any scribe at all.

The Old English *starr* is a corruption of *shtar* and referred to documents or
contracts held by Jews, falling out of use after the expulsion from England in
1290 until later scholars such as the English parliamentarian John Selden
revived it.

### The biblical words
Tanach has neither term. A bill of divorce is *sefer kerisus*, literally a
"Scroll of Cutting" (Devarim 24:1-3; Yeshayahu 50:1). A deed of purchase is
*sefer hamiknah*, a "Scroll of Acquisition," five times in Yirmiyahu 32, the
standard, or the only, biblical word for a document of sale.

## Rendering guidance

- Unmodified *gett*: "bill of divorce," or leave it as *gett* in a frum register
  where the reader knows the word. Modified: translate the modifier and keep
  "writ" or "bond" for the noun (*gett shichrur*, "writ of emancipation").
- *Shtar*: "document," "deed," or "bond" by context; *shtar chov* is "bond of
  debt," not "IOU," which is too light for a court instrument.
- *Sefer kerisus*: "scroll of severance." Do not render it "bill of divorce" in
  a passage that is drawing the *gett*/*kerisus* connection, or the whole point
  (that *gett* mirrors the biblical language of cutting) disappears.
- Trap: English "bill" or "document" will cover *gett* and *shtar* alike, and
  will also cover *sefer*. If a source turns on Rabbi Algazi's rule that a
  communal enactment about *shtaros* does not reach a *gett*, the two must stay
  visibly distinct in English.
- Second trap: *shoter* is ordinarily "officer," not "scribe." A
  writing-related rendering ("record-keeper," "organizing officer") belongs to
  Dr. Chaim Tawil's Akkadian derivation, and Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur keeps the
  officer and the document apart altogether. Use either only where the source
  is one of those readings, and say which.
