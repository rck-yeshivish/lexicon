# Document: gett / shtar (and the biblical sefer kerisus)

## Bottom line

Neither *gett* nor *shtar* is biblical. In the language of Chazal both mean
**document**, and both normally take a modifier (*gett chov*, *shtar erusin*).
*Gett* is the broader of the two for Rabbi Yisrael Yaakov Algazi: every *shtar*
may be called a *gett*, not every *gett* a *shtar*. Unmodified, *gett* means
**a bill of divorce** (Rashi; Rambam), and that is how to render it standing
alone. In Tanach the bill of divorce is *sefer kerisus*, **a scroll of
severance** (Devarim 24:1-3; Yeshayahu 50:1), and a deed of sale is *sefer
hamiknah* (Yirmiyahu 32). Do not read a biblical *sefer* as a *shtar*.

## Distinctions

### gett (גט)
Technically any document. *Gett ishah* is a writ of divorce, *gett shichrur* a
writ of emancipation, *gett chov* a bond of debt (Gittin 10a; Bava Kamma 95a;
Bava Basra 160a). Rashi (Gittin 65b) and Rambam (Commentary to the Mishnah,
Gittin 2:5) record that in ordinary usage a *gett* with no modifier is a bill of
divorce. Why this word, and why for divorce, is the subject of a long
disagreement.

- **Twelve lines.** Tosafos (Gittin 2a) cite Rabbeinu Tam that a *gett* carries
  twelve lines because the *gematria* of גט is twelve. The Maharsha, Maharam
  Shiff, and *Elyah Rabbah* read Tosafos as explaining the *name* as well; the
  *Tosafos Yom Tov* objects that this reads more into Tosafos than the words
  bear (see Rabbi David Tzvi Fetene, *Maharsha HaAruch*, who answers him). Rabbi
  Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) raises two objections of his own: other
  documents are called a *gett* without the twelve-line requirement, and other
  two-letter pairs also total twelve (דח, הז, טג).
- **Letters that never touch.** Rabbi Chaim of Friedburg (*Sefer HaChaim*),
  brother of the Maharal, is the earliest known source: Hashem so dislikes
  divorce that He kept *gimmel* and *tes* from ever standing in that order
  anywhere in Tanach. Rabbi Algazi heard the same, tying it to divorce as
  separation, and the Vilna Gaon (*Divrei Eliyahu*) says the like. Rabbi Meir
  Mazuz sharpens it: other never-adjacent pairs (גק, סצ) are letters from the
  same organ of speech and hard to pronounce, while *gimmel* and *tes* come from
  different organs and are perfectly pronounceable, and they are the earliest
  incompatible pair in alphabetical order. Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky (*Taama DeKra*
  to Devarim 24:1) offers that last point too. The pair does occur across a word
  boundary at Yechezkel 22:25; see Rabbi Aryeh Leib Tzintz's introduction to
  *Seder HaGett*.
- **The stone.** Rabbi Yehoshua Boaz (*Shiltei HaGibborim* on Mordechai,
  Gittin 1) cites a Midrash no longer extant about a gem called *getta* that
  repels people, fitting a document by which a couple push one another away. The
  Chasam Sofer knows a version in which the stone *attracts*, and settles it
  from the other *Shiltei HaGibborim* (Rabbi Avraham the Physician of Portaleone)
  on the stone "gagate" that draws straw when rubbed. On that reading every
  document is a *gett* because it binds parties together, and the divorce sense
  is the harder one, which is why the twelve-line explanation was needed.
- **Cutting or erasing.** *Tosafos Chachmei Anglia* (Gittin 2a), *Orchos Chaim*
  (Hilchos Gittin), *Kolbo* 76, the glosses to Rabbi Yaakov Margolis's *Seder
  HaGett*, and the *Levush* (Even HaEzer 125:11) all take *gett* as an
  expression of breaking or cutting, matching the biblical *sefer kerisus*, and
  cite a lost Yerushalmi that uses it so. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer reads
  it as erasure (compare Rashi to Berachos 56a).

### shtar (שטר)
Root ש-ט-ר. Common from the Mishnah onward and always specified by its type:
*shtar chov*, *shtar erusin* (Kesuvos 102b; Kiddushin 9b), *shtar nisuin* and
*shtar arisus* (Bava Basra 167b), *shtar amanah* (Kesuvos 19a; Gittin 19b; Bava
Basra 49a, 154b), *shtar berurin* (Moed Katan 18b), *shtar mivrachas* (Kesuvos
79a). Whether it is the same root as *shoter*, the officer, is disputed. Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*) gives ש-ט-ר two separate entries, the enforcing
officer and the document, and treats the two senses as unrelated. Rav Hirsch (to
Shemos 5:6) joins them: a *shtar* is the creditor's instrument for pressing and
enforcing his claim.

### The biblical words
Tanach has neither term. A bill of divorce is *sefer kerisus*, a scroll of
severance (Devarim 24:1-3; Yeshayahu 50:1). A deed of purchase is *sefer
hamiknah*, five times in Yirmiyahu 32, which is the standard biblical term for
a document of sale.

## Rendering guidance

- Unmodified *gett*: "bill of divorce," or leave it as *gett* in a frum register
  where the reader knows the word. Modified: translate the modifier and keep
  "writ" or "bond" for the noun (*gett shichrur*, "writ of emancipation").
- *Shtar*: "document," "deed," or "bond" by context; *shtar chov* is "bond of
  debt," not "IOU," which is too light for a court instrument.
- *Sefer kerisus*: "scroll of severance." Do not render it "bill of divorce" in
  a passage that is drawing the *gett*/*kerisus* connection, or the whole point
  (that *gett* mirrors the biblical language of cutting) disappears.
- Trap: English "bill" or "document" will cover *gett* and *shtar* alike, and
  will also cover *sefer*. If a source turns on Rabbi Algazi's rule that a
  communal enactment about *shtaros* does not reach a *gett*, the two must stay
  visibly distinct in English.
- Second trap: *shoter* is "officer," not "scribe," and not "document-keeper."
  Only use a writing-related rendering where the source is Rav Hirsch's reading
  and you say so.
