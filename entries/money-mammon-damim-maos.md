# Money: mammon / damim / maos (zuz, kesef, mechir)

## Bottom line

*Mammon* is **money** in the broadest sense, one's assets and holdings; it
never appears in Tanach, only from the Mishnah on, and the *Pri Megadim* holds it
to be Aramaic. *Damim*
is **the price** or **the value** of a thing, always plural, and it is the
homonym of *damim*, blood. *Maos* and *zuzim* are coin
names that widened into general words for **money**; *kesef* is **silver**, which
probably widened the same way. *Mechir* is the Biblical **price**. In a Mishnaic passage
*damim* is regularly money, while in Tanach
it is generally understood to be blood, with only two pesukim disputed.

## Distinctions

### mammon (ממון)
Absent from Tanach, frequent from the Mishnah on: loving Hashem "with all your
*mammon*" (Berachos 9:5, on Devarim 6:5), a motive for kidnapping (Kesubos 2:9),
the exemption of one liable to death from monetary payment (Kesubos 3:2), and
Rabbi Yishmael's advice that one who wants to grow wise should occupy himself
with *dinei mammonos* (Bava Basra 10:8). Rabbi Natan of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*)
and Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*, *Meturgeman*): *mammon* is
Targum's standard Aramaic for the Biblical *hon* (wealth) and *rechush*
(property). Etymologies:

- *Bamidbar Rabbah* 22:8 reads it as *mah atah moneh, eino klum*, what are you
  counting, it is nothing, an exegesis that partly suggests the root מ-נ-ה,
  counting.
- Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionaries of Hebrew and of English,
  writes that it most probably derives from *ma'amon*, a deposit or trust, from
  א-מ-ן, true or trustworthy, with the opening mem added to the core root.
- The word also appears in the Christian Bible as a personification and
  deification of the greedy pursuit of wealth; and Rabbi Yitzchok Schmelkes of
  Lvov (1827-1905, responsa *Beis Yitzchak*, Yoreh Deah vol. 1 §152) explains
  that Mammon was originally the name of the pagan god of silver and money, and
  only afterward became an ordinary word for money.
- On a more esoteric plane, Rabbi Chaim Yosef David Azulai, the Chida, notes
  that each letter of ממון is spelled with its own letter doubled, hinting at
  "one who loves money is never sated with money" (Koheles 5:9): having one mem
  he wants a second; compare *Hafla'ah* to Kesubos 66b.

### damim (דמים)
Money in Rabbinic Hebrew, generally understood not to occur in this sense in
Tanach, and always plural. In the Mishnah: Maaser Sheini 1:5, Pesachim 7:3,
Kiddushin 1:6, Bava Metzia 5:3, Bava Basra 2:7, Arachin 5:2, Temurah 5:5, with
the construct *demei* in even more (Terumos 5:1, 6:3-4, 9:2-3; Maaser Sheini
1:4, 2:1; Pesachim 9:8; Kesubos 12:1-2; Nazir 4:4; Bava Kamma 5:4, 8:1-2, 9:1,
9:4, 10:4; Bava Metzia 3:5, 3:12; Zevachim 8:1-2; Bechoros 5:6; Arachin 5:2-3;
Meilah 3:2; Kinnim 1:4). Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur notes that the sense of money
never appears in the singular *dam*, which is what sometimes makes it hard for a
novice to tell it from the *damim* that means blood, a word found in both Tanach
and Mishnah; he also notes that *damim* is Targum's standard rendering of the
Biblical *mechir*, usually "price" (II Shmuel 24:24; Yeshayahu 45:13) and
*mecher*, sale price (Bamidbar 20:19). At Yeshayahu 55:1, where *kesef* and
*mechir* stand together, Targum Yonasan renders *kesef* as *damim* and *mechir*
as *mammon*. Offering translations into the European vernaculars, HaBachur
writes in *Sefer HaTishbi* that *damim* is *gelt* in Yiddish and German and
*dinero* in Spanish. *Dinero* takes its name from the medieval Spanish coin,
itself modelled on the Arabic dinar and the Roman denarius; *dinarim* are
mentioned many times by Chazal in the Mishnah. Two Biblical verses are disputed:
at Shemos 22:1, a householder who kills a burglar in the tunnel has "no
*damim*," which Rashi reads as blood (he is no murderer) and Rashbam as money
(he owes no compensation); and at Tehillim 72:14 Ibn Ezra reads *dam* as blood
while Ibn Janach and Radak (each in *Sefer HaShorashim*) read it as monetary
worth. Etymology:
- Rabbi Ernest Klein writes that *damim* in the sense of money probably derives
  from ד-מ-ה, being like or equal to something else. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
  builds that root out as "similarity, resemblance": *domeh*, *demus*, *dimyon*
  (imagination, which may resemble reality without truly reflecting it), *adam*
  (created in the image of Hashem, Bereishis 1:27, in that he resembles Him in
  some ways); and since to call two things merely similar already rules out
  their being exactly equal, similarity implies incongruity, whence
  *dom*/*domeim* (quiet, inactive, the incongruity between a mind that goes on
  working and an outward activity that has stopped) and *adamah* (ground, which
  stays passive while the plants in it grow). Pappenheim himself never ties any
  of this to *damim* as money, his work being confined to Biblical Hebrew; the
  step, that money likewise *resembles* commodities in that its value can stand
  in for the commodity itself, is Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own.
- Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Ehrenreich reads it instead from *dom*, silence: money
  given to the poor and to *talmidei chachamim* silences the prosecuting angel a
  sin acquires (Avos 4:11), and a Jew is better off silent about his wealth than
  flaunting it.
- Rabbi Chaim of Friedberg (*Igeres HaTiyul*) and Rabbi Yaakov Emden (*Ezer
  Ohr*) connect it to blood outright: a man lives off his money as his body
  lives off his blood. A pauper is accounted as dead (Nedarim 64b), and taking
  even a *perutah* from another is as if taking his soul (Bava Kamma 119a).
  Rabbi Chaim of Friedberg adds the mussar: as corrupted blood ruins the whole
  body, illicitly gained money brings total ruin, and as a man sometimes needs
  blood drawn for his health, he needs to give away money in tzedakah for his.
- Rabbi Azaria Figo (*Binah L'Itim*, derush 69) finds it implausible that Chazal
  named money for blood on the strength of how the ignorant value it.

### mammon against damim
- Rabbi Eliezer Herstik (*Ragli Mevaser*): *damim* is the value or price of a
  thing, *mammon* is any property or asset a man holds.
- Rabbi Yosef Teomim-Frankel (1727-1792), author of the *Pri Megadim*, writes in
  one of his letters that *mammon* is cash, *mobilia*, whose value is already
  known, and *damim* is moveable property whose value has still to be assessed
  before it can serve as currency; he also holds *damim* to be Hebrew (citing
  Rashbam at Shemos 22:1) against Aramaic *mammon*, and ties *damim* to
  *dimyon*/*demus*, something that only appears to resemble something else.
  Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says this ostensibly fits the Pri Megadim's view of
  *damim* as property that only resembles capital until it is appraised.
- Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit (*Leshon Chachamim*) similarly ties *damim*
  to *dimyon*, imagination: a man imagines his money equals every commodity
  there is, "money answers everything" (Koheles 10:19).

### maos (מעות) and zuz (זוז)
*Maos* is the plural of *maah*, a coin equal to the Biblical *geirah* (Targum to
Shemos 30:13), widened into a general word for money, as in *maos chitim*, the
pre-Pesach alms. *Bamidbar Rabbah* 22:8 reads *maos* as *mah* plus *l'eis*, what
is it worth for a time, on wealth's impermanence; a talmid of the Ritva (in
*Midrash Shmuel* to Avos 2:8) cites the Midrash slightly differently, tying it
to *ivus*,
crookedness, money corrupting a man so that he is taken from the world before his
time; the Tosafists (*Daas Zekeinim* to Bamidbar 32:1) have a version in which
money perverts justice. *Zuz* likewise began as a coin and widened; *Bamidbar
Rabbah* 22:8: liquid assets move (*zaz*) out of one man's hand into another's
quickly. A *maneh*, the mina, is a coin worth a hundred *zuz*, and ostensibly
from the same root as *moneh* (counting, appointing), *manah* (portion, lot),
*minyan* (number, quorum) and *mammon* itself; they also seem related to the name of the idolatrous deity Meni (Yeshayahu 65:11), who
was said to allot a man his destiny.

### kesef (כסף)
Silver the metal, which then became another word for money outright. Where
*kesef* and *mechir* stand in one pasuk (Yeshayahu 55:1), Targum keeps them
apart. The root כ-ס-ף also yields *nichsaf*, desiring or loving, and the Maharal
(*Netivos Olam*, *Nesiv HaOsher*, ch. 2) clarifies the connection: man's drive to
accrue more and more money derives from his desire to become rich.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein reports two of his own guesses about English, one
abandoned and one not. He used to think English and German *gold* was related to Yiddish and
German *gelt*, by Grimm's Law on the interchange of the *d* and *t* sounds,
paralleling the way Hebrew's word for silver became its word for money; on
further research it seems to him that the consensus among linguists is that *gold*
goes back to the Proto-Indo-European *ghelh*, the more direct ancestor of
English *yellow* and German *gelb*, while *gelt* goes back to the
Proto-Indo-European *gheldh*, to pay or award, which is the ancestor of English
*yield*. He likewise used to think English *money* came somehow from Mishnaic
*mammon*, and a look at the *Oxford English Dictionary* gives instead that
*money* and *mint* both come from the Latin *moneta*, the name of a Roman
goddess in whose temple money was coined, often said to be no more than an
epithet of the goddess Juno. But the original hunch, he suggests, may
not be so far off, since Professor Theo Vennemann Nierfeld proposes that Latin
*moneta* is a cognate of the name of the god Meni, which would give English
*money* and *mint* a Hebrew, or at least a Canaanite, Phoenician or Punic,
origin.

## Rendering guidance

- ממון: "money" generally, "funds" or "assets" where holdings are meant, "monetary"
  in *dinei mammonos* ("monetary law," not "civil law," which imports a foreign
  category).
- דמים in a Mishnaic or halachic context: "the price," "the value," or "the money."
  Do not render it "bloods."
- Trap: the same consonants are blood. When translating a Mishnah, ask what is
  being paid for; when translating a pasuk, assume blood unless a Rashbam is
  standing behind you. At Shemos 22:1 give Rashi's "there is no bloodguilt" in
  the body and Rashbam's monetary reading in a note, since they are the two
  readings of one word.
- Second trap: English "money" absorbs *mammon*, *damim*, *maos*, *zuzim* and
  *kesef* at once. Where two of them meet in a phrase, differentiate: *mammon*
  "money," *damim* "the price," *maos* "coin."
- כסף in a monetary clause: "money" (or "silver" where the metal is the point).
  In *kiddushei kesef* keep it *kesef*.
- Coin names (*zuz*, *maah*, *maneh*, *dinar*) stay transliterated; they are
  currency, not translations.
