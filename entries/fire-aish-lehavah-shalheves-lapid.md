# Fire: aish / ur / lehavah / shalheves / lahat / lapid

## Bottom line

*Aish* is the general word: **fire**. *Ur* is a **fire** too, but a smaller one
(Malbim). *Lahav* and *lehavah* are the **flame** that shoots outward from a
fire, which is why the same word means a **blade**. *Shalheves* is a flame
gripping some material it is consuming. *Lahat* is fire moving fast, a
**blaze**. *Lapid* is a **torch**, the object carrying the fire, and *avukah* a
multi-wick torch against *ner*, the single-wick **lamp**. The one thing not to
get wrong: *lehavah* is not a bigger fire than *aish*; it is the outward-reaching
part of one.

## Distinctions

### aish (אש) and the Aramaic nur / nura
The ordinary word for fire. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman*) observes that
the Targumim usually render *aish* as *aisha* or *aishata*, but sometimes as
*nur* or *nura*; he says outright that he does not know what governs the choice,
though he notes that where the pasuk speaks of a fire that burns or roasts
something, the Targumim tend to use *nura*. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek
Shlomo*) holds that the letter reish itself carries "throwing," so *ohr*
("light") is light rays thrown from their source, *ner* is the object holding
that source, and *menorah* the vessel the *ner* is fixed in. The article extends
this to *nura* as well, noting that Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim does not say so
himself.

### ur (אור)
Spelled like *ohr* but pointed differently, and plainly meaning fire. Six times
in Tanach, only in Yeshayahu and Yechezkel, and the preferred word in the
Mishnah, where it is common (*Challah* 4:8; *Shabbos* 1:11, 8:7, 16:5; *Yoma*
6:7; *Beitzah* 4:4 and 4:7; *Rosh Hashanah* 2:3; *Bava Kama* 6:4 and 9:4;
*Sanhedrin* 9:1; *Avodah Zarah* 5:12; *Avos* 2:10; *Zevachim* 12:6; *Menachos*
10:4; *Chullin* 3:3; *Tamid* 1:3; *Keilim* 5:11 and 29:8; *Ohalos* 11:7). Ur was
also the Mesopotamian city where Avraham Avinu lived, and the mesorah reads the
name as alluding to the fiery furnace its king threw him into. Malbim: *ur* is a
smaller fire than *aish*.

### lahav / lehavah (להב, להבה)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*): where *aish* is the general term, a
*lahav* is specifically the flame that spurts up into the air. It is attached to
a larger fire and is that fire's outlet for spreading outward. This is why
*lahav* also means the blade of a knife or sword: the blade is the part of the
tool that acts on things outside itself, and the word also carries the
shimmering glimmer of a flame, hence the glinting edge of metal. Ovadiah 1:18
reads accordingly: the fire within Beis Yaakov spreads outward through the flame
of Beis Yosef and burns up the straw, which is Beis Eisav.

### shalheves (שלהבת)
Close to *lahav*, but Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim distinguishes it: a *shalheves* is
a flame attached to something tangible. He links it to *meshulav* ("interlocked,
joined," Shemos 26:17, Melachim I 7:29), so a *shalheves* is flame mixed into
the material it is consuming.

### lahat (להט)
The fast movement of fire, what English calls spreading like wildfire. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to ל-ט, "bending," the same root behind the sleight
of hand of Paroh's magicians (*b'lateihem*, Shemos 7:11, 7:22, 8:3). Like
*lahav*, *lahat* can mean either flame or blade, and both words were later
borrowed for enthusiasm.

### lapid (לפיד) and avukah (אבוקה)
*Lapid* is used when the focus is the object carrying the fire rather than the
fire itself, so "torch." Radak (to Nachum 2:4) reads *aish pladot* as an
inverted way of saying *lapid aish*. *Avukah* is post-Biblical and first means a
bundle of twigs for fuelling a fire; from there it came to mean any candle or
torch with more than one wick lit. Halachah distinguishes the two: Havdalah
calls for an *avukah*, while *bedikas chametz* and *neros Chanukah* call for a
*ner*, a single wick.

### ner (נר) and shraga (שרגא)
*Ner* is the lamp or candle; the Targumim render it *shraga*, which later became
a personal name, its Yiddish counterpart being Feivish.

## Rendering guidance

- אש: "fire." Plain, always.
- אור (as fire): "fire," and where the contrast with *aish* matters, "a small
  fire" or "embers"; never "light," which is the other pointing of the same
  letters.
- להב / להבה: "flame." Where it is a weapon, "blade." If a passage plays on
  both senses, keep "flame" in the body and note the blade sense rather than
  splitting the word into two English words mid-derashah.
- שלהבת: "flame" as well, but if it stands beside *lehavah* render it "flame
  clinging to the coal" or similar; Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's point is the
  attachment.
- להט: "blaze," or the verb "blazed."
- לפיד: "torch." אבוקה: "torch" too, so where both appear, make *avukah* a
  "multi-wick torch" and reserve plain "torch" for *lapid*.
- נר: "lamp" in Tanach, "candle" in a Chanukah or Shabbos context.
- Trap: English "flame" will absorb *lehavah*, *shalheves* and *lahat* at once,
  and "fire" will absorb *aish* and *ur*. Ovadiah 1:18 sets *aish* and *lehavah*
  in one pasuk; render "a fire... a flame," never "a fire... a blaze," which
  reverses the relation by making the second sound larger than the first.
