# Vessel: pach / kankan / kad / chavis (kaneh)

## Bottom line

*Pach* is a pottery **flask** or **jug**, smaller than a *chavis* (Rashi), used
in Tanach only in *pach hashemen*. *Kankan* is a Mishnaic **jug** or **jar**;
*kad* is typically translated **jug** or **pitcher**, *chavis* **barrel**. The Mishnah uses *kad* and *chavis* as though
interchangeable, because in the vernacular they were not necessarily fixed (Bava
Kamma 27a), and Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds *kankan* (from Mishnayos that swap it with both)
and *pach* (from the version of Chullin 91a that reads *levado* as *l'kado*).
Why does *Maoz Tzur* sing of *kankanim* where the Gemara says *pachim*? Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein answers that it is poetic license, and that the
interchangeability justifies the swap as well; he calls that interchangeability
"a real possibility," and concludes that the two "essentially mean the same
thing." *Kaneh* is not a container but a **reed**, **stem**, or **tube**,
typically hollow.

## Where the authorities disagree

**The root of pach, and what it means.**

- The early lexicographers derive *pach* from *mefakim*, the flow of liquid
  (Yechezkel 47:2, Shekalim 6:3, Middos 2:6), the *pach* being what liquid flows
  or is poured from. The triliteralists take the root as פ-כ-ה (Radak, *Sefer
  HaShorashim*), the biliteralists as a two-letter פ-כ (*Machberes Menachem*,
  Rashi to Yechezkel 47:2).
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim also takes the root as פ-כ but gives it the sense of
  *reversal*, parent of *hafuch*: a flask with a narrow opening, which must be
  turned upside down to empty. He relates *puch*, eye makeup (II Melachim 9:30,
  Yirmiyahu 4:30), to it, since such kohl was typically stored in a *pach*, and
  says the gemstone names *puch* (Yeshayahu 54:11, Divrei HaYamim I 29:2) and
  *nofech* (Shemos 28:18, 39:11, Yechezkel 28:13) were borrowed from the
  cosmetic *puch*, the stones resembling its color.

**The etymology of kankan.** Dr. Alexander Kohut (*Aruch HaShalem*) claims
that Mishnaic *kankan* is a loanword from Latin *kanne*/*canna* ("reed," "cane,"
"pipe," "tube," "small vessel"), probably by reduplication. The *Oxford English
Dictionary* derives English *can* from the same Latin; other English words said
to come from it are *cane*, *canal*, *channel*, *kennel*, *canister*, *canon* and
*canyon*. The French *cancan* is unrelated (its etymology unclear, perhaps from
*canard*, duck). Rabbi Ernest David Klein
(*Comprehensive Etymological Dictionary of the English Language*) adds that the
Latin itself derives from Semitic, that is, Hebrew, *kaneh*. Rabbi Reuven Chaim
Klein draws the conclusion: *kaneh*'s senses are pipe-like and typically hollow
in the middle, so when Mishnaic Hebrew reborrowed the term from Latin as
*kankan*, it makes sense that it named a vessel with a hollow receptacle, which
fits its Hebrew etymon too.

**Why Maoz Tzur says kankanim.** The obvious answer is poetic license:
*kankanim* rhymes with *shoshanim*, which ends the next line, and *pachim* does
not. Rabbi Tzvi Elimelech Shapiro of Dinov (1783 to 1841) rejects this, since
*shemanim* would have rhymed too. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein still holds that poetic license
is correct, and answers that the word had already ended the previous line ("they
defiled all the *shemanim*") and rhyming a word with itself is tacky.
He then adds that the interchangeability of the words also justifies
the swap.

**Whether the Menorah's branches may be hollow.** Rambam (*Beis HaBechirah* 3:4)
rules that a Menorah made *chalulah* is fit, and there are two ways to read him.

- Rabbi Yosef Karo (1488 to 1575): *chalulah* means not made from a single hunk
  of gold; optimally the Menorah is one hunk of gold, but where it is made of
  another metal it may be fashioned from scraps joined together. (*Aruch
  HaShulchan HeAsid*: such a Menorah is called *chalulah* because there will be
  gaps between the pieces.)
- The Mabit, Rabbi Moshe of Trani (1505 to 1585, *Kiryas Sefer*): the branches
  may be made hollow *lechatchilah* in all cases, even out of one hunk of gold.

Rabbi Massoud Chai Rokach (1690 to 1768) and Rabbi Avraham Dovber Kahane-Shapiro
(1870 to 1943) explain that Rav Karo did not follow the Mabit because no source
in Chazal says, explicitly or implicitly, that the branches may be hollow. Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein answers for the Mabit: the Torah's word *kaneh* itself tells
us the branches can (or perhaps even should) be hollow, and Rashbam and Ibn Ezra
(to Shemos 25:32) write explicitly that the *kanim* ought to be hollow within;
that is the simple meaning of the Chumash, so the Mabit needed no explicit
source in Chazal.

## Distinctions

### pach (פך)
Three occurrences in Tanach, each in *pach hashemen*: Shmuel's anointing of Shaul
(I Shmuel 10:1) and the anointing of Yehu by Yonah, Elisha's disciple (II
Melachim 9:1, 9:3). Jastrow: "flask," "jug." In the Mishnah (Shabbos 17:5,
Beitzah 1:7, Bava Kamma 8:6, Keilim 2:2, 3:2, 5:3, Negaim 12:5) it is a pottery
jug typically used for storing oil, and Rashi (to Avodah Zarah 71b) makes it
smaller than a *chavis* (but see the interchangeable usage below). Chullin 91a
(cited by Rashi to Bereishis 32:25) has Yaakov recross the Yabok for small
*pachim*. Where does the pasuk allude to *pachim*? In the version the Tosafists
(*Daas Zekeinim*, *Hadar Zekeinim*) and Rabbeinu Bachya cite, the derashah reads
*levado* as *l'kado*, "for his *kad*," on the resemblance of beis and kaf. In
that version, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein concludes, the Gemara uses *pach* and
*kad* interchangeably.

### kankan (קנקן)
Absent from Tanach. In the Mishnah it stores wine (Maaser Sheini 1:3-4, 3:12-13,
Avodah Zarah 2:4), oil (Kesubos 13:4, Bava Metzia 3:8, Shevuos 6:3), or water
(Mikvaos 2:7, 2:9); Tosafot (Avodah Zarah 32a): typically pottery. So far, Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein says, *kankan* lines up with *pach*, and it seems very likely that the two
could be used interchangeably. On its etymology, from Latin *canna* (Kohut) and
the Latin from Hebrew *kaneh* (Ernest Klein), see above.

### kad (כד) and chavis (חבית)
The Mishnah may open a ruling with one word and continue it with the other
(Terumos 11:7-8, Demai 7:8, Bava Kamma 3:1, 3:5, 10:4); Bava Kamma 27a: in the
popular vernacular the two could be switched about and their meaning was not
necessarily fixed. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds *kankan* to the set, though the Gemara does
not note it: Maaser Sheini 3:13 interchanges it with *chavis*, and Maaser Sheini
1:3-4, Kesubos 13:4 and Shevuos 6:3 with *kad* (see *Tosafos Yom Tov* to Kesubos
13:4).

### kaneh (קנה)
Over fifty occurrences in Tanach: "reed," "stem/branch," "tube," all pipe-like
items with a typically hollow middle; in Rabbinic Hebrew it also came to name the
windpipe.
The Torah calls the Menorah's branches *kanim* (Shemos 25:31-36, 37:18-22),
which is how Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein explains the Mabit's ruling that they may be hollow.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "jug" will absorb *pach*, *kankan* and *kad*, and often that is
  right. Distinguish only where the passage turns on size: *pach* small flask,
  *kad* jug, *chavis* barrel.
- Where a Mishnah shifts from *kad* to *chavis* within one ruling, Bava Kamma
  27a explains the shift as loose vernacular; keep the Mishnah's two words
  visible in English rather than repairing them to one.
- *Pach hashemen* in a Chanukah context: "flask of oil" (Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein himself says "container").
- *Kaneh* is never a vessel: "reed," "stem," "branch," or "windpipe."
