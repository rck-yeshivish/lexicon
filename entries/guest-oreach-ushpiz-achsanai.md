# Guest: oreach / ushpiz / achsanai

## Bottom line

*Oreach* is the biblical **guest**, more precisely the **wayfarer** taken in
while travelling; the word is built on *orach*, a road. *Achsanai* is the
rabbinic **guest**, and *achsanyah* the **lodging** or the **host** who
provides it. *Ushpiz* is Aramaic and cuts both ways: in the Gemara usually the
**host**, in the Zohar (the *Ushpizin* of Sukkos) usually the **guest**.
*Achsanyah* shares something of that ambiguity: both words can point sometimes
to the guest and sometimes to the host.

## Distinctions

### oreach (אורח)
Four occurrences (Shoftim 19:17, Shmuel II 12:4, Yirmiyahu 9:1, 14:8). Menachem
Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak trace it to א-ר-ח, whose commonest derivative is
*orach*, a road or path (about sixty occurrences), and which also gives *orchah*,
a caravan (Bereishis 37:25, Yeshayahu 21:13). Ibn Janach and Radak: an *oreach*
is so called because he travels an *orach* and is put up along the way. Ibn
Janach translates *oreach* outright into the rabbinic *achsanai*.

*Aruchah*, a meal (Yirmiyahu 40:3, 52:34, Mishlei 15:17, Melachim II 25:30),
comes from the same string. Ibn Saruk (*Machberes*) files it as a separate sense
of א-ר-ח; Radak says plainly that it belongs with *orach* and *oreach* without
spelling out how. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests that perhaps *aruchah*
originally meant the food provisions a sojourner took with him on his travels.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim assigns all four (*oreach*, *orach*, *orchah*,
*aruchah*) to the biliteral ר-ח, "air, space," alongside *revach* (open space),
*ruach* (wind), *rei'ach* (smell, which travels in air), *yarei'ach* (the moon,
whose movements somehow govern the tides and winds), and *memarei'ach*
(smoothing, which squeezes out air). An *orach* is a wide road
with *revach* in it; an *oreach* travels it; an *aruchah* is the meal set out in
an *oreach*'s honor.

### ushpiz (אושפיז)
Aramaic. In Talmudic Aramaic it typically means **host**: *ushpiza* is a male
host (Rashi and Rabbeinu Chananel to Bava Metzia 24a) and a hostess (Rashi to
Shabbos 140b), but Rashi to Eruvin 53b takes it as guest. Rabbi Dr. Alexander
Kohut, Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein, Rabbi Yaakov Chaim Sofer and others read
*ushpizchan* as the host (Rashi to Beitzah 4a) and *ushpizi* as the guest,
though Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein notes that this does not always seem to hold.
Rabbi Shmuel Krauss
and Rabbi Yitzchak Avineri argue that *ushpiz* in the Gemara *never* means
guest, though it could sometimes mean a guest's accommodations, and that the
"guest" sense is a later development reflected in the Zohar's parlance. Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein's own guess is that perhaps all of this comes from non-expert
copyists who introduced spelling errors into the Talmud that obscure which variation
means what. In Zoharic Aramaic *ushpiz* usually means **guest**: most famously
the seven *Ushpizin* who grace the sukkah (Zohar *Emor* 103b).

On etymology:
- Eruvin 53b, as Rashi explains it, plays on *ushpiz* as *ish pi zo chai*, "the
  man by whom this mouth lives"; Tosafos there, citing Rabbeinu Chananel, answer
  that the word is simply Aramaic and not built from that phrase.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*), who vocalizes it *ashpaz*, takes it
  from Latin *hospes*, the etymon of English *hospitality*, *hospice*,
  *hospital* and *hospitaller*. According to modern linguists that Latin word
  apparently derives from proto-Italic *hospitpotis*, "the master of the guest,"
  and it is also said to be related to Latin *hostis*, stranger or enemy, behind
  English *hostile* and *hostage*.
- Others, including Rabbi Shmuel Krauss, argue that *ushpaz* comes not from
  Latin but from Old Persian, where *aspanj* means "inn." Either way HaBachur
  notes the word covers both the lodging house and the guest who uses it. (In
  Modern Hebrew *ishpuz* means hospitalization.)
- Rabbi Yaakov Emden argues for a Hebrew reading, *yesh po zin*, "there is
  sustenance here," for *ushpiz* as host.
- Rabbi Shimon Yehuda Leib Goldblit parses *ushpaz* as *ish paz*, "a man of
  gold," and uses it to explain why the Zohar reaches for a non-Hebrew word for
  the seven tzaddikim rather than the plain *orchim*: *ushpizin* pays them a
  compliment that the seemingly neutral Hebrew does not.
- Ashpenaz of Daniel 1:3 seems to be a proper name, though Ludwig Koehler and
  Walter Baumgartner, in their lexicon of biblical Hebrew and Aramaic,
  understand it rather as a title, something like "chief of the household" or
  "landlord"; on that reading it seems related to the later *ushpiz*, with an
  extra nun as often happens.

By the *Sefer Yuchasin*'s girsa of Taanis 24b (see *Dikdukei Sofrim*) Rav
Nachman's mother was named Ushpiztai, likely from the same word.

### achsanai (אכסנאי) and achsanyah (אכסניה)
Twice in the Mishnah (*Demai* 3:1, *Chullin* 8:2), at least once in Targum (to
Iyov 31:32), and throughout Shas and Midrash. HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*): the guest
lodging temporarily is the *achsanai*, and the inn or host itself the
*achsanyah*. As with *ushpiz*, the two words sometimes get mixed up. In
*Meturgaman* HaBachur renders *achsanyah* by the German and Yiddish *gast*;
related are the Jewish surname Gastwirth, innkeeper, and German *Gasthaus*,
guest house. Rabbi
Dr. Ernest Klein claims *achsanyah* derives from Greek *xenos*, stranger or
foreigner, with the prosthetic alef common in Greek words taken into Hebrew,
the same Greek word behind English *xenial*, *xenophobia*, *xenogenous*,
*xenogamy* and the element xenon. Rabbi Avi Kobernick offers a Hebrew
derivation from כ-ס-ן, read
against its metatheses כ-נ-ס (gathering in), ס-כ-ן (storing) and נ-כ-ס
(property): an *achsanyah* gathers wayfarers in and gives them property in
which to be housed. Rashi to Berachos 63b glosses *achsanyah* with *machnisei
orchim*, which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein takes as alluding to the link between
כ-ס-ן and כ-נ-ס that Kobernick posited.

Arachin 16b, from Rashi to Bereishis 13:3 on Avraham
stopping at the same lodgings coming back from Mitzrayim as he had on the way
down: one should not change his *achsanyah* between journeys, where the word is
the **host**, ostensibly so as not to insult the original hosts by taking
another next time.
Berachos 63b: *pos'chin bichvod achsanyah*, a guest called on to speak opens by
honoring his hosts. And Rashi to Bamidbar 20:17 uses both words in one comment,
teaching an *achsanai* (guest) to buy provisions from his *ushpiz* (host) even
when he has enough of his own (see also *Bamidbar Rabbah* 19:15 and *Midrash
Tanchuma, Chukas* 12).

English itself has a parallel: *host* is understood to be a
cognate of *guest*, both ultimately from Proto-Indo-European *ghostis*, guest
or stranger, which is said in turn to come from *ghos*, to eat. Words with an
h-sound in one Indo-European language often carry a c, k or g sound in another
(*cap* and *head*, *cent* and *hundred*), so Latin *hostis*, which became
*host*, is itself related to Germanic *gast*, which became *guest*. That is
similar to the way *ushpiz* and *achsanyah* are somewhat ambiguous, pointing
sometimes to the guest and sometimes to the host.

## Rendering guidance

- *Hachnasas orchim* stays transliterated with a gloss at first occurrence; a
  bare "hospitality" loses the "bringing in" that is the whole mitzvah.
- Trap: English "host" and "guest" are the very pair the Hebrew blurs. Do not
  render *ushpiz* or *achsanyah* from a glossary. In a Gemara sugya read it as
  host by default and check Rashi; in the Zohar or in Sukkos usage read it as
  guest.
- *Ushpizin* of Sukkos stays transliterated, not "guests," since Rabbi Shimon
  Yehuda Leib Goldblit's point is that the Zohar deliberately declined the
  ordinary Hebrew word.
- Where *achsanai* and *ushpiz* fall in one sentence, as in Rashi to Bamidbar
  20:17, translate them as an explicit pair, "guest" and "host," so the reader
  sees which is which; do not leave both transliterated there.
- *Oreach* against *achsanai*: both are "guest," but if a passage sets a
  biblical *oreach* beside a rabbinic *achsanai*, "wayfarer" for the first
  keeps the road sense that its root carries.
- *Aruchah* is "meal," not "way." The shared root is an etymology, not a
  translation.
