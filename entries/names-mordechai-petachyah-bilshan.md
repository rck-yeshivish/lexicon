# Names of Mordechai: Mordechai / Petachyah / Bilshan

## Bottom line

All three are names or titles of one man, and no two are the same name.
**Mordechai** is the Babylonian name the Megillah uses throughout, and
**Petachyah** his Hebrew name. For the Gemara, **Bilshan** is a title that
speaks of his mastery of languages; Ibn Ezra presumes it is the name of the next
man listed. One opinion in the Gemara makes the navi Malachi a fourth name for
him; others make Malachi a different man.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Mordechai's other names.**

- Megillah 16b and Menachos 65a read Mordechai-Bilshan (Ezra 2:2, Nechemiah 7:7)
  as one man with a title (see Maharsha and *Keren Orah* to Menachos 65a).
- Ibn Ezra (to Esther 2:21) presumes Bilshan is the next man listed.
- On the Babylonian name: *Torah Temimah* (to Shemos 30:23 §48 and Esther 2:5 §11) postulates that his
  true Hebrew name was Petachyah, though for some reason the Megillah
  consistently calls him Mordechai, and that because of that name's idolatrous
  implications the Gemara sought a favorable allusion for it in the Torah and
  thereby gave it some legitimacy.
- Rabbi Yehonatan Eybeschutz (*Yaaros Dvash* vol. 2 drush 13) holds that as
  Moshe's given name is absent from the Torah after "erase me from Your book,"
  so Mordechai, who likewise gave himself up for the Jewish People, caused his
  real name Petachyah to be left out of the Megillah.

**Whether there was a fourth name.**

- One opinion in Megillah 15a has the navi Malachi be Mordechai himself, called
  Malachi because he was viceroy to the Persian king, *mishneh l'melech*, Rabbi
  Klein presuming that *malachi* is read as kin to *melech*; that would give him
  three names, Mordechai, Petachyah and Malachi.
- Other opinions there make Malachi an alternate name for Ezra, or a separate
  man altogether, and the Rambam, in his introduction to *Yad HaChazakah*,
  follows the last, since he lists Malachi, Mordechai Bilshan and Ezra as
  separate members of the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah.

## Distinctions

### Mordechai (מרדכי) and Merodach
Chullin 139b asks where Mordechai is hinted in the Torah and answers *mar dror*, pure
myrrh (Shemos 30:23), rendered *mira dachya* by Onkelos. The idea behind the connection
is that a tzaddik exudes good deeds as myrrh gives off fragrance. Megillah 10b has Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani open his expositions on
the Megillah from "instead of the thorn-bush will arise a *brosh*" (Yeshayahu
55:13), playing on *brosh* as *b'rosh*, "at the head," and reading it of
Mordechai, whose name alludes to *mar dror*, which the Torah calls *besamim
rosh*, the head of all spices; *Peirush HaRokeach* (to Shemos 30:23) adds that
the gematria of *rosh*, 501, equals *zehu Mordechai HaTzadik*. Rabbi Klein
stresses that none of this is meant as *etymology*: the Gemara is giving an
*exegetical* allusion to the name in the Torah, and the question of the name's
actual origin is a separate one.

Many scholars have noted the name's similarity to the Babylonian name Merodach
in Tanach: Evil Merodach (Melachim II 25:27), Merodach Baladan (Yeshayahu 39:1), who appears
elsewhere as *Berodach* Baladan (Melachim II 20:12), probably by the
interchangeability of mem and beis, and Yirmiyahu 50:2, where Merodach stands as
another name for the Babylonian god Bel, the counterpart of the Canaanite Baal.
Archaeologists have found Merodach/Marduk to have been one of the chief gods of
the Babylonian pantheon, and the Babylonian creation myth *Enuma Elish* tells of
Marduk defeating Tiamat, the primordial sea monster of chaos, and using her body
to make the world. Rabbi Klein says plainly what he is *not* claiming: not that Mordechai was named after that deity, but that the
personal name Mordechai and its variants, current in Babylonian and Persian
society, were rooted in the god's name, and that once such names became ordinary
one bears them without being named for an idol, much as people today are called
Mark or Veronica without a thought for Mars or Nike.

Dr. Alexander Kohut, in *Aruch HaShalem*, suggests instead that Mordechai
means "small person" in Persian, a name given him by others in appreciation of
his exceptional humility. (Rabbi Klein adds that he had once taken the Irish and
Scottish name Murdoch for a corruption of Mordechai, but on looking further found
it more likely from the Gaelic *muir*, sea.) Mordechai's name appears 58 times in
the Megillah, and twice more in Tanach as Mordechai Bilshan.

### Petachyah (פתחיה) and Bilshan (בלשן)
Shekalim 5:1, which puts Petachyah in charge of the *kinnim*, identifies him with
Mordechai and says he was called Petachyah because he would *poteach* words and
expound them, and because he knew seventy languages. Unlike Mordechai, the name
has a Hebrew ring: it already belongs to a kohen in David's day (Divrei HaYamim I
24:16) and to Levi'im in post-exilic Yerushalayim (Ezra 10:23; Nechemiah 9:5, and
see 11:24), and onomastically it parses as the root פ-ת-ח with a theophoric
element, two of the four letters of the Shem HaMeforash. Rabbi Chaim Palagi finds
an allusion to the Mishnah's identification in gematria: half the value of each of
the first three letters of Petachyah (peh, tav, ches) equals each of the first
three of Mordechai (mem, reish, dalet), while double the value of Petachyah's last
two letters equals Mordechai's last two (kaf, yud).

Menachos 65a: all the Sanhedrin were required to know the seventy languages, but
Mordechai stood out even among them, in that he could mix and match between
languages rather than hold them as seventy separate bodies of knowledge; that is
what the Gemara reads out of the name Bilshan, a contraction of *bayil lishni
v'darish*. Stories illustrating the depth of his mastery are at Menachos 64b-65a
and Yerushalmi Shekalim 5:1. That skill is what let him understand Bigsan and
Seresh plotting regicide in a foreign tongue (Megillah 13b), which he reported to
Achashverosh, and which later helped turn the tide against Haman.

## Rendering guidance

- Transliterate all three, roman type, no translated gloss in the body; bring in
  what a name is read to mean only in a note, and say whose reading it is.
- Where the Gemara plays on a name (*mira dachya*), keep the Hebrew in
  brackets; these are derashos on sound.
- Do not write that Mordechai "was named after" a Babylonian deity. Rabbi Klein's
  own formulation is the one to follow: the name and its variants, current in
  Babylonian and Persian society, were rooted in the god's name, but by then one
  bore such a name without being named for an idol.
