# In-laws: cham and chamos / chosen and choseness

## Bottom line

Both pairs mean **father-in-law** and **mother-in-law**; in Biblical Hebrew
they differ by whose in-laws they are. Radak: *cham* and *chamos* are always a
**wife's** parents-in-law, *chosen* and *choseness* always a **husband's**. In
Mishnaic Hebrew *cham* and *chamos* widened to cover a husband's in-laws too,
but *chosen* and *choseness* never widened to cover a wife's (*Pri Megadim*).
So an English "father-in-law" is correct for both but loses the one thing the
Hebrew is telling you, which side of the marriage is speaking.

## Distinctions

### cham (חם) and chamos (חמות)
Root, on the Hebrew side, ח-מ, heat. The wife's words. Yehudah is called Tamar's
*cham* (Bereishis 38:13, 38:25), and Naomi is Rus's *chamos* (Rus 2:11 through
3:17).

Rav Hirsch: in a traditional patriarchal society a woman marrying leaves her
parents' family and joins her husband's, where his relatives stand around her
like a *chomah*, a wall; hence *cham* and *chamos*. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
reads the same letters as the biliteral ח-מ, hot: a bride must stress the warm
love she receives from her new relatives in order to honor her husband, and
love is spoken of metaphorically as fire (Shir HaShirim 8:7), so it makes sense
that she names them from the root of heat. The kabbalist Rabbi Menachem Azariah
of Fano: the husband's parents do not *give* their son in the way the wife's
parents give their daughter; their contribution is that they gave birth to him,
so the words derive from *yechemasni* ("birth," Tehillim 51:7).

The *Pri Megadim* (Rabbi Yosef Teomim-Frankel), in a letter on dikduk, suggests
working from the Aramaic instead: ח-מ there means "to see," and the Targumim always
render Hebrew *chosen*/*choseness* with cognates of *cham*/*chamos*. A woman's
mother-in-law is *chamos* because she is forever watching her daughter-in-law,
for good or otherwise (Yevamos 15:4 presumes that the two are always assumed to
hate each other). From the mother-in-law's title the matching word for the
wife's father-in-law was formed, and from there the pair spread to a husband's
in-laws as well.

### chosen (חותן) and choseness (חותנת)
The husband's words, and cognate with *chasan*. Yisro is called Moshe's *chosen*
thirteen times in Shemos 18:1-27, and the curse on incest with a mother-in-law
reads *shochev im chosanto* (Devarim 27:23).

Rav Hirsch: the husband does not join a new family; his family forms a
connection with another family while he remains in his own, so his words for
his in-laws are built on connection, like *chasan*. (The column adds, in
passing, that Hebrew and Yiddish have *mechutanim* for two people whose
children have married each other, and that English has no word for that
relationship at all.) Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
derives *chasan* from the biliteral ח-ת, descending or setting down, since the
groom branches off from his family to found a new subdivision; his parents-in-
law are *chosen* and *choseness* because in giving him their daughter they gave
him the chance to open that branch. (Radak gives the core sense of *chasan*
differently, as one who experiences a new joy, which is why the word covers a
baby at his bris as well as a groom.) Rabbi Menachem Azariah of Fano reads
*chosen* as a contraction of *achos nosen*, "a sister he gives," on the premise
that soulmates stand on a level and a husband and wife may be called brother
and sister; the father-in-law is the one who gave this man his "sister," which
is why the word can only ever be the husband's.

*Pri Megadim* on why this pair never widened: *chosen* names the man who made
him a *chasan*, and *choseness* was formed from it. Since *chasan* is by
definition the husband's standpoint, the pair cannot be turned around to the
wife's, even in Mishnaic Hebrew.

### The Mishnaic widening
Radak notes that in Mishnaic Hebrew *cham* is already used of a husband's
father-in-law (Kesubos 1:5) and *chamos* of a husband's mother-in-law (Yevamos
1:1). Rabbi Shimon Pollack (responsa *Shem MiShimon*), asked why a man says
*avi mori* of his father but *mori vechami* of his father-in-law, putting "my
teacher" first in the second, offers as one suggestion that *chami* in that
sense is only Mishnaic and rests on Aramaic, so the Hebrew *mori* is given
precedence; whereas for one's father both words are Hebrew. He supports this
from the fact that a man who uses the Biblical *chosni* will usually say
*chosni mori*, both words there being Hebrew.

## Rendering guidance

- Render both pairs plainly, **father-in-law** and **mother-in-law**. There is
  no English pair that marks whose in-law is meant.
- Where the passage turns on the difference (Radak's rule, the *Pri Megadim* on
  why one pair widened, a drashah on *chosen* and *chasan*), transliterate and
  gloss: "his *chosen* (his father-in-law, the wife's father)."
- Trap: English "in-laws" absorbs all four words at once, and worse, it hides
  the gender of the speaker. In a translation of a dikduk discussion, never
  write "in-laws" as a blanket.
- Trap: *cham* is also the ordinary adjective "hot," and Cham is a name. Do not
  let a play on ח-מ (Pappenheim's heat, or the Aramaic "to see") be rendered as
  though the pasuk itself said "hot."
- *Chasan* and *chosen* share a root and look alike in transliteration. Where
  both appear, spell them apart on the page and gloss the first of each.
