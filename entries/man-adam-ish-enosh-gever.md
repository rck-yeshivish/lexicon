# Man: adam / enosh / ish / gever

## Bottom line

*Adam* is the species name and the highest term: **man** or **mankind**.
*Enosh* is man in his weakness: **mortal**, **frail man** (the plural *anashim*
flips to "men of standing," Rashi). *Ish* is one individual, physically mature:
**a man**, "man" as against woman or child. *Gever* is man as strength: **man**
in the sense of the strong male, and not generic humanity, though whether it is
always male is itself disputed (Ibn Ezra says yes, Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel brings
gender-neutral uses against him). The Zohar ranks them (*adam* > *enosh* > *ish* > *gever*).

## Distinctions

### adam (אדם)
The all-encompassing word: singular and plural, male and female (Bereishis
1:26-27; Zohar Nasso 141b).

- Malbim: *adam* behaves like a proper name, which is why it never takes a
  plural or feminine form; "two adams" is as wrong as "two Reuvens."
- On the root, some explain that he was called Adam because *adam* comes from
  *adamah*, ground, and he was created from the earth. But all the animals too
  were created from the earth (Bereishis 1:24), and *adam* is never used of
  them, so why is he the one called Adam?
- The Maharal answers that the name ties him to the ground as a reminder that
  just as dirt is worthless yet holds great potential to foster growth, so a man
  is physically worthless, being only dust and ashes, yet holds great potential
  to grow.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim answers the question head on: the animals were not
  *created* from the earth, they merely *emerged* from it, while Adam was
  actually created from it.
- Pappenheim also ties *adam*, separately, to the biliteral ד-ם, "similar"
  (*domeh*, *demus*, *dimyon*), because man is made in Hashem's image and
  resembles Him in some ways. The most obvious example is the way his actions
  echo through creation.
- Rav Hirsch: from *hadom*, footstool, for man was meant to sit at Hashem's
  footstool as His deputy, so to speak.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: the *adamah* link marks man's pull toward the
  material, so *adam* names the great distance he can travel toward the
  spiritual.
- Shadal (to Bereishis 1:26): *adam* is from *adom*, red, because man's reddish
  skin is left bare where other creatures are covered in hair or fur.
- Rabbi Yitzchak ben Yosef of Pira, a student of the Rashba (to Bereishis 3:20):
  the name *Adam* alludes to the four elements. The aleph is *avir*, air; the
  dalet is *dam*, blood, which boils like fire; the mem is *mayim*, water; and
  the whole word is *adamah*, earth.
- The Zohar (Tazria 48a): *adam* is man at his highest potential.

### enosh (אנוש)
Usually man in degradation (Yeshayahu 51:12), lost hope (Iyov 14:19), or the
pursuit of bodily pleasure (Tehillim 104:15); the angels who argued against
giving man the Torah called him *enosh* (Tehillim 8:5).
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the root א-נ-ש means weak and sickly.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: from נ-ש, "moved from its natural condition"
  (*nosheh*, *nashah*, *anush*), so *enosh* is man whose powers are, in some
  ways, weak beside greater spiritual beings.
- Wertheimer: *enosh* is *ish* with a diminutive nun.
- The Vilna Gaon: the image of Hashem held fully only for Adam and Sheis; it was
  lost in the days of Sheis's son Enosh, when idolatry began to develop, and the
  loss of that spiritual resemblance was reflected in physical ways, leaving man
  weakened, to the point that according to the Midrash people's faces came to
  look like apes.
- Rashi (to Bamidbar 13:3): the plural *anashim* means men of importance. Rabbi
  Klein explains why: the lowly man who joins others and humbles himself before
  them becomes important through the group.

### ish (איש)
The exclusive term: sometimes man as against woman, sometimes man as against
child. Where *adam* names the species, *ish* names a single specimen of any
species, which is why the Tanach uses it of an angel (Yechezkel 9:11), a keruv
(Shemos 25:20), a star (Yeshayahu 40:26), an animal (Bereishis 7:2), even a
curtain (Shemos 26:3).

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: from י-ש, "has, is, exists"; the mature man *has* all
  he needs and stands at the peak of his physical existence.
- Mecklenburg: *ish* implies a more abstract, less material existence, so the
  road to the spiritual is shorter and less remarkable; hence it can name
  angels, but it does not name man's greatest possibilities.

### gever (גבר)
- Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 10:11): always a male as against a female.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel disputes this and cites gender-neutral uses.
- Malbim: from *gevurah*, so it recalls strength and potency and usually means
  the male.

A woman in power may be called by a cognate, *gevirah* (Yirmiyahu 13:18) or
*geveres* (Yeshayahu 47:5), queen, queen-mother, mistress, lady of the house.

Ibn Yachya reads the four as life stages: *adam* the child (nearest the ground),
*gever* the adolescent (physical strength arrives), *ish* the adult (from *aish*,
fire, at the height of his power), *enosh* the elder (strength diminished).

## Rendering guidance

- Standalone אדם in a mussar passage: "man" (generic, no article) or "a
  person." When the passage is about *gadlus ha'adam* or *tzelem Elokim*, keep
  *adam* transliterated; the word is carrying the argument.
- אנוש: "mortal" or "frail man" when the tone is lowering; plain "man" only
  where the context is neutral. אנשים: "men," and where Rashi's reading is in
  play, "men of standing."
- איש: "a man," "each man," "a person"; in the מה אנוש / מה אדם pesukim the
  contrast is the point, so vary the English.
- גבר: "man" with the strength sense audible ("a strong man," "the man who");
  never "mankind."
- Trap: English "man" absorbs all four. In a list, a doubled pair, or a
  derashah on why a pasuk says *enosh* rather than *adam*, do not let "man"
  stand for two Hebrew words; gloss the Hebrew in parentheses or vary the
  English (mankind / mortal / a man / strong man).
- Together, a rising or falling sequence follows the Zohar's order: *adam*
  highest, then *enosh*, *ish*, *gever*. Render so the rank is visible.
