# Chick: efroach / gozal / pargit / nipol

## Bottom line

*Efroach* is the Torah's word for a **hatchling**, the bird that has just
"flowered" out of its egg. *Gozal* is a **fledgling**. Ibn Janach and Radak take
it as primarily a young dove, widened to any young bird; Rabbi Yehonasan HaKohen
of Lunel holds that in Biblical Hebrew it is simply a dove. Rabbi Yaakov
Kamenetsky and Rabbi Shmuel Ashkenazi hold that the two words mark two stages in
a small bird's maturing: the *efroach* is the younger, which still needs its
mother, and the *gozal* the older, already able to fly in some limited way.
*Pargit* is a Rabbinic word for a **young chicken**. Rabbeinu Gershom and Rambam
take *nipol* as a **fallen nestling**, defined by the halachic case and not by
the species.

## Distinctions

### efroach (אפרוח)
Four times in Tanach: twice in the *shiluach haken* passage (Devarim 22:6-7),
then Iyov 39:30 and Tehillim 84:4; three times in the Mishnah (*Shabbos* 18:2,
*Eduyos* 4:2, *Chullin* 12:3).
- Menachem Ibn Saruk takes it as the sole derivative of a four-letter root
  א-פ-ר-ח.
- Ibn Janach and Radak read the root as פ-ר-ח, "to grow, to flower," the root of
  *perach*, a flower. Ibn Ezra (to Devarim 22:6) says outright that the *aleph*
  is not part of the root.
- Ibn Janach: the *efroach* looks as though it sprouted out of its egg.
- Radak and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: it is named for the growing it still has
  ahead of it before it flowers into a mature bird.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus reduces the root to the two-letter core פ-ר, spawning off
  into segments.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 16:11) groups it with *para*, [*pere*](donkey-chamor-aton-pere-ayir-pered.html#%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%90), [*parah*](cattle-shor-par-egel-bakar.html#%D7%A4%D7%A8),
  *bara*, *barach* and *barah*, all turning on unhindered movement and so on
  growth.

### gozal (גוזל)
Twice in Tanach, at Bereishis 15:9 (the Covenant Between the Pieces) and Devarim
32:11 (the eagle hovering over its young), and several times in the Mishnah
(*Bikkurim* 3:5, *Shekalim* 8:5 and 9:1, *Bava Metzia* 1:4 and 2:3, *Kinnim*
2:1, *Taharos* 8:6). Root ג-ז-ל, the root of *gezel*, theft. By tradition the
*gozal* Avraham was told to take was a dove (*Bereishis Rabbah* 44:14, Targum
Onkelos to Bereishis 15:9), since besides the pigeon that is the only bird fit
for offerings, and that covenant was a sort of offering (see Ibn Ezra there).

- Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer HaShorashim*): *gozal* primarily
  means a young dove, but the sense widened to any young bird, which is how it
  can be a young eagle at Devarim 32:11.
- Rabbi Yehonasan HaKohen of Lunel: in Biblical Hebrew *gozal* is simply a dove,
  as *yonah* is, while *efroach* is a young bird of no particular species; in
  Rabbinic Hebrew *gozal* shifted slightly, to a young dove specifically. Rabbi
  Reuven Chaim Klein observes that Rabbi Yehonasan's account leaves the eagle
  verse out of the reckoning, and guesses that Rabbi Yehonasan may have taken
  that usage as merely borrowed rather than as a real shift in the word's sense.
  Rabbi Klein adds the further observation that *gozal*, on this showing,
  carries both "dove" and "stealing," much as *yonah* is explained by some as
  tied to *ona'ah*, cheating or verbal abuse.
- On the tie to theft, Rabbi Yehuda Aryeh Leib of Carpentras: this dove steals
  from other people's fields.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 23:19): the hatchling begins as its mother's charge, and
  when it matures enough to leave the nest it "steals" itself away from her.

A cryptic remark of Ibn Ezra at Devarim 32:11 on how *gozal* and *efroach*
relate is read three ways:
- Rabbi Yitzchak Sarim, that *gozal* is any young bird, the young eagle among them;
- Rabbi Shlomo Kohen of Lissa, that *gozal* is synecdoche, naming both the whole
  set (young birds) and one part of it (a certain dove);
- and Rabbi Dr. Asher Weiser (editor of the Mossad HaRav Kook edition of Ibn
  Ezra on Chumash), that the two are plain synonyms.

Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky and Rabbi Shmuel Ashkenazi argue that the two words
mark two stages in a small bird's maturing: the *efroach* still needs its
mother and cannot yet fly, while the *gozal* is a little older, no longer needs
its mother, and can already fly in some limited way. Rabbi Ashkenazi himself
points to a place that cuts against it: the *Tosefta* (*Chullin* 10:2) uses
*efroach* of a young bird that no longer needs its mother and can already fly.

A different approach is found in an article of the Academy of the Hebrew
Language and another by Dr. Moshe Raanan of Herzog College. They explain that in
earlier times the two terms were indeed synonymous, but that Modern Hebrew
separates them on a zoological line: an *efroach* is a nidifugous bird, out of
the egg already feathered and able to leave the nest at once because it can feed
itself, like chickens and geese, while a *gozal* is a nidicolous bird, born
featherless and fed by its mother, like most songbirds, which must stay in the
nest until it is developed enough to go out on its own. On their reading the two
words are not two stages in one bird's growth but two kinds of bird.

### pargit (פרגית)
Rabbinic only: *Berachos* 39b, *Bava Metzia* 24b, *Bereishis Rabbah* 18:17,
*Vayikra Rabbah* 34:14, *Perek Shirah* ch. 4. A young chicken.

- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) ties it to the Arabic *fyruj*.
- Rabbi Ernest Klein traces it instead to the Greek *pterix*, a wing or winged
  creature, which he takes back in turn to the Proto-Indo-European root *pet-*,
  to fly, the source also of the English *feather*, *fetter*, *partridge*,
  *pterodactyl* and *helicopter*.

In Modern Hebrew it often refers to cuts of chicken meat, which is not the
Rabbinic sense.

### nipol (ניפול)
From the Mishnah at *Bava Basra* 2:6 (see 23b): a young bird found near a
dovecote, which the finder may keep if it was more than fifty *amos* away.
- Rashi glosses *nipol* as a *gozal*, though Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein cautions
  that this does not make the two terms synonymous.
- The Mainz commentary ascribed to Rabbeinu Gershom, and Rambam in his
  commentary to the Mishnah, define it as a bird that *fell* from its cote,
  cognate with *nofel*; some vocalize it *naful*/*nafil*, "the fallen one"
  (*Melechess Shlomo*, and the Kaufmann manuscript).
- Rabbi Shlomo Ganzfried, on the strength of *Chullin* 65a, takes *nipol* as a
  species of grasshopper rather than a bird at all.

## Rendering guidance

- אפרוח in the *shiluach haken* passage: "hatchling," or "fledgling" if the
  context needs a word that also covers eggs' issue generally. Do not write
  "chick"; it pulls toward barnyard poultry and loses the flowering image the
  root carries.
- גוזל standing alone in Chumash: "young dove" at Bereishis 15:9 (*Bereishis
  Rabbah* 44:14 and Targum Onkelos both identify it as a dove there), "fledgling"
  at Devarim 32:11, where the bird is an eagle.
- Trap: English "fledgling" will absorb both *efroach* and *gozal*. If a passage
  turns on the pair, follow Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky and Rabbi Shmuel Ashkenazi
  and render *efroach* as the younger stage ("hatchling," still nest-bound) and
  *gozal* as the older ("fledgling," flying a little). Rabbi Ashkenazi himself
  notes a counter-case: *Tosefta Chullin* 10:2 uses *efroach* for a bird that
  already flies and no longer needs its mother, so do not force the distinction
  where the source does not want it.
- Where Targum Yonasan renders *efroach* as *gozal* (Devarim 22:6) while Targum
  Onkelos keeps *efroach*, do not flatten the two Targumim into one English
  word; the divergence is itself the datum.
- פרגית: "young chicken," never "pargit" untranslated and never the modern
  restaurant sense.
- ניפול: "a fallen nestling," with the halachic sense supplied; "small bird"
  loses the falling that defines the case.
