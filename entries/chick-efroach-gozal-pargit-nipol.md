# Chick: efroach / gozal / pargit / nipol

## Bottom line

*Efroach* is the Torah's word for a **hatchling**, the bird that has just
"flowered" out of its egg. *Gozal* is a **fledgling**, primarily a young dove
but extended to any young bird; where the two are contrasted (Rabbi Yaakov
Kamenetsky, Rabbi Shmuel Ashkenazi) the *efroach* is the younger, still tied to
its mother, and the *gozal* the older, already able to fly. *Pargit* is a
Rabbinic word for a **young chicken**. *Nipol* is a **fallen nestling**, defined
by the halachic case, not the species. The trap: English "chick" or "fledgling"
will absorb *efroach* and *gozal* both, and the whole point of a passage may be
which stage is meant.

## Distinctions

### efroach (אפרוח)
Menachem Ibn Saruk takes it as the sole derivative of a four-letter root
א-פ-ר-ח; Ibn Janach and Radak read the root as פ-ר-ח, "to grow, to flower," the
root of *perach*, a flower. Ibn Ezra (to Devarim 22:6) says outright that the
*aleph* is not part of the root. Ibn Janach: the *efroach* looks as though it
sprouted out of its egg. Radak and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: it is named for the
growing it still has ahead of it before it flowers into a mature bird. Rabbi
Aharon Marcus reduces the root to the two-letter core פ-ר, spawning off into
segments. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 16:11) groups it with *para*, *pere*,
*parah*, *bara*, *barach* and *barah*, all turning on unhindered movement and
so on growth.

### gozal (גוזל)
Root ג-ז-ל, the root of *gezel*, theft. Ibn Janach and Radak (each in his *Sefer
HaShorashim*): *gozal* primarily means a young dove, but the sense widened to
any young bird, which is how it can be a young eagle at Devarim 32:11. Rabbi
Yehonasan HaKohen of Lunel: in Biblical Hebrew *gozal* is simply a dove, as
*yonah* is, while *efroach* is a young bird of no particular species; in
Rabbinic Hebrew *gozal* narrowed to a young dove. On the tie to theft, Rabbi
Yehuda Aryeh Leib of Carpentras: this dove steals from other people's fields.
Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 23:19): the hatchling begins as its mother's charge, and
when it matures enough to leave the nest it "steals" itself away from her.

Ibn Ezra's remark at Devarim 32:11 on how *gozal* and *efroach* relate is read
three ways: Rabbi Yitzchak Sarim, that *gozal* is not only a young eagle but any
young bird; Rabbi Shlomo Kohen of Lissa, that *gozal* is synecdoche, naming both
the whole set (young birds) and one part of it (a certain dove); and Rabbi Dr.
Asher Weiser, that the two are plain synonyms.

### pargit (פרגית)
Rabbinic only: *Berachos* 39b, *Bava Metzia* 24b, *Bereishis Rabbah* 18:17,
*Vayikra Rabbah* 34:14, *Perek Shirah* ch. 4. A young chicken. Rabbi Nosson of
Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) ties it to the Arabic *fyruj*; Rabbi Ernest Klein traces
it instead to Greek *pterix*, a winged thing. In Modern Hebrew it has drifted to
mean cuts of chicken meat, which is not the Rabbinic sense.

### nipol (ניפול)
From the Mishnah at *Bava Basra* 2:6 (see 23b): a young bird found near a
dovecote, which the finder may keep if it was more than fifty *amos* away.
Rashi glosses *nipol* as a *gozal*, but that is identification of the case, not
a claim that the words are equivalent. The Mainz commentary ascribed to
Rabbeinu Gershom, and Rambam in his commentary to the Mishnah, define it as a
bird that *fell* from its cote, cognate with *nofel*; some vocalize it
*naful*/*nafil*, "the fallen one" (*Melechess Shlomo*, and the Kaufmann
manuscript). Rabbi Shlomo Ganzfried, on the strength of *Chullin* 65a, takes
*nipol* as a species of grasshopper rather than a bird at all.

## Rendering guidance

- אפרוח in the *shiluach haken* passage: "hatchling," or "fledgling" if the
  context needs a word that also covers eggs' issue generally. Do not write
  "chick"; it pulls toward barnyard poultry and loses the flowering image the
  root carries.
- גוזל standing alone in Chumash: "young dove" at Bereishis 15:9 (*Bereishis
  Rabbah* 44:14 and Targum Onkelos both identify it as a dove there), "fledgling"
  at Devarim 32:11, where the bird is an eagle.
- Trap: English "fledgling" will absorb both *efroach* and *gozal*. If a passage
  turns on the pair, follow Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky and Rabbi Shmuel Ashkenazi
  and render *efroach* as the younger stage ("hatchling," still nest-bound) and
  *gozal* as the older ("fledgling," flying a little). Rabbi Ashkenazi himself
  notes a counter-case: *Tosefta Chullin* 10:2 uses *efroach* for a bird that
  already flies and no longer needs its mother, so do not force the distinction
  where the source does not want it.
- Where Targum Yonasan renders *efroach* as *gozal* (Devarim 22:6) while Targum
  Onkelos keeps *efroach*, do not flatten the two Targumim into one English
  word; the divergence is itself the datum.
- פרגית: "young chicken," never "pargit" untranslated and never the modern
  restaurant sense.
- ניפול: "a fallen nestling," with the halachic sense supplied; "small bird"
  loses the falling that defines the case.
