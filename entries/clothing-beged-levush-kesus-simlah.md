# Clothing: beged / levush / kesus / simlah (mad, me'il, maateh)

## Bottom line

*Beged* is the general word, **garment**, and covers the rest (Malbim). *Levush*
is not a garment-type but what one dresses in, so **apparel**, and in practice
the distinctive dress of a station (*levush malchus*). *Kesus* is a **covering**,
draped rather than put on; *simlah* (or *salmah*) a **cloak**, a *kesus* that
also confers honor. *Mad* is the **fitted garment**, *me'il* the **outer robe**,
*maateh* any **wrap**. The difference between *beged* and *levush* carries two
separate derashos.

## Where the authorities disagree

**beged against kesus.** *Beged* is the general term (Malbim) and
*kesus* the draped covering. The Maharik (Rabbi Yosef Kolon) reverses the axis:
*beged* is a plain cloth like a tallis, unfitted to its wearer, while *kesus* is
a garment specially sewn to his shape and dimensions. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon
Wertheimer is the one who points out that the two stand in contrast.

**What kesus names.** The manner of wearing, draped rather than donned; or, for
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, the function, protection from heat, cold or anything
else.

**simlah against salmah.** The consensus treats them as one word with the lamed
and mem transposed. Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 22:25) dissents: *simlah* is the general
category and *salmah* one item within it.

## Distinctions

### beged (בגד)
Malbim: the general term, taking in every sort of clothing, against the other
words, which each name a type. Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: *beged* is kin to *begidah*,
treachery, since a man who puts one on conceals his inner self and shows the
world a front.

### levush (לבוש) and malbush (מלבוש)
Neither inherently names clothing, as many commentators note: both are
conjugations of *lovesh*, "he
dresses," so they mean that with which one dresses. In usage a *levush* is worn
the ordinary way but is nonetheless special, since most people do not wear that
sort of garment; it is the dress reserved to certain people, hence *levush
malchus*, *levush sak*. Against *beged*, *levush* is the plain, honest use of
clothing: Shabbos 77b reads it as *lo bushah*, "no shame," since its purpose is
to cover a man respectably, with no dastardly motive in it. Rabbi Yochanan
called his clothes "my honorers"
(Bava Kamma 91b), seeing them as the means of presenting himself respectably in
public rather than as a mask to hide behind. Rabbi Shlomo of Karlin: every deed clothes the soul, a good
deed weaving a *levush* and a bad deed a *beged*. Rabbi Klein concludes that,
against the cliché, the man makes the clothes.

### kesus (כסות), simlah (שמלה) and salmah (שלמה)
*Kesus* literally means "cover," and names the garment one covers oneself with, a
shawl or poncho rather than something put on in the usual manner; the kaf sometimes drops
and it appears as *sus* (Bereishis 49:11). A *kesus* worn at night is a *salmah*
or *simlah*, and Malbim adds a second difference: *kesus* only covers, while
*simlah* also honors its wearer. Wertheimer describes it as a linen cloth that
can be folded to cover head, neck and upper torso, much like a kefiyeh, and
doubles as a bed sheet when opened to its full size; he agrees that as worn it
generally serves as a badge of honor.

### mad (מד), me'il (מעיל), maateh (מעטה)
*Mad* is cut to its wearer's measure, from *moded*; Modern Hebrew *madim* is a
uniform. *Me'il* is the outermost layer, from *mei'al* (Malbim), thin and
delicate (Wertheimer), and Rabbi Moshe Shapiro ties it to *me'ilah*, betrayal.
*Maateh* is wrapping or enveloping, and need not be clothing.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "clothing" will absorb *beged*, *levush* and *malbush*. Where a passage
  plays *beged* against *levush* (the Karliner's two dressings of the soul, or a
  mussar piece on the facade), use two English words: "garment" and "apparel."
- *Levush malchus* is "royal apparel"; the point is the station, not the cloth.
- *Kesus* in halachah (*kesus ishto*, a pledge) is "covering" as a maintenance
  category, not a specific item.
- *Me'il* in the Mishkan chapters is the Kohen Gadol's "robe"; do not vary it to
  "cloak," which belongs to *simlah*.
- Where *beged* and *kesus* appear together and tailoring is at issue, decide
  first whether the source follows Malbim's general *beged* with a draped
  *kesus*, or the Maharik.
