# Linen: sheish / bahd / butz / pishtim

## Bottom line

All four are linen. *Pishtim* (and *pishtah*, *pishtan*) is **flax**, the raw
material. *Butz* is **fine linen** as a material, on one view the Aramaic-Hebrew
rendering of the Egyptian *sheish*. *Sheish* is **fine linen** thread, six fibers to the
strand, and is the Torah's word for the Mishkan's weaving. *Bahd* is the
**single-fiber** linen thread, from *bad*, "alone," and it is the word for the
Kohen Gadol's white garments on Yom Kippur. Cotton is not in this family at all:
it did not exist in the ancient Near East, and where Rabbinic Hebrew needs it,
it says *tzemer gefen*. The modern *kotna* is not the Semitic *kitna*, "linen";
Kutscher traces it to *katan*, "small."

## Distinctions

### pishtim (פשתים)
The flax plant and the fiber it yields.

- Ralbag and Abarbanel (to Shemos 25:4): *pishtim* and *butz* name linen as a
  **material**, while *sheish* and *bahd* name particular **threads**.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim narrows *pishtah* / *pishtan* further, to the flax
  while it is still growing in the ground.

### sheish (שש)
- Ralbag and Abarbanel: a thread of six fibers, the number being the word
  (*sheish*, six); Rambam draws the same distinction in Hilchos Klei HaMikdash
  8:14.
- Some say that *sheish* is actually an Egyptian word for the choicest grade of
  linen, with *butz* as its Aramaic-Hebrew rendering (see *Daas Zekeinim* to
  Bereishis 41:42).
- Pappenheim reads *sheish* as combed linen strands not yet spun into thread,
  and ties the word to *shayish*, marble or limestone, for its smoothness.

Two readings explain why Yoma 71b has to prove that *sheish* means linen.

- The Steipler Gaon (in a letter to Rabbi Moshe Mordechai Schulsinger, *Mishmar
  HaLevi*): *sheish* names a **color**, whitish, exactly as *techeiles*,
  *argaman* and *shni tolaas* in the Mishkan passages name colors and not
  materials; tradition supplies that those three are wool, and the Gemara has to
  establish that *sheish* is linen.
- Rabbi Dov Landau, to the same question: *sheish* is a general term for any
  dignified garment, so the Gemara must show that in these pesukim it means
  linen specifically.

### bahd (בד)
From *bad*, alone, as in *levad* and *hisbodedus*.

- Ralbag and Abarbanel: a linen thread of a single fiber.
- Pappenheim: a garment made of linen, and it is called *bad*, "alone," because
  in those times linen fabric was never blended with another material, since
  nothing else held its dye the way linen did.

Zevachim 18b establishes that *bahd* is linen; on the Steipler's account this is
needed precisely because the word by itself only says "alone."

### butz (בוץ) and kitna (כיתנא)
*Butz* is the later Hebrew and Aramaic word for fine linen; the Latin *byssus*,
a thin silk-like linen, is said to be derived directly from it. *Kitna* is the
Talmud's Aramaic for linen (see also Targum to Shemos 9:31), and Yoma 71b and
Zevachim 18b define *sheish* and *bahd* by it. *Kitna* means linen in several
other Semitic languages, Akkadian and Arabic among them, and is possibly even the basis of the Biblical *kesones*, "tunic":
Professor Edward Yechezkel Kutscher explains that the Greeks borrowed the Aramaic
*kitna* as *chiton*, a linen shirt, which by metathesis became the Latin
"tunic." Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev (*Me'at
Tzari* to Vayikra 16:4) separates the two Aramaic words as Pappenheim separated
the Hebrew: *kitna* is flax unprocessed and still in the ground, *butz* is linen
already worked.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, who traces *botz*, mud, and *bitzah*, marsh, to the
biliteral ב-ץ, a fluid of mucus-like consistency, also asserts that *butz*
(Esther 1:6, 8:15; Yechezkel 27:16) refers specifically to a type of flax or
linen that grows in watery soil like that of a *bitzah*.

### cotton (כותנה, צמר גפן)
Not a Biblical category. Cotton simply did not exist in the ancient Near East,
so it makes sense that there would be no Biblical word for it. Some linguists
argue that *karpas* (Esther 1:6) is a loanword from Persian, where it meant a
cotton garment; the Latin *carbasus* and the Greek *karpasos* likewise refer to
a cotton garment. Beside *chur* and *techeiles* there, *karpas* is fairly
clearly some sort of fabric, and which sort is disputed.

- Rabbi Saadia Gaon (*Tafsir* to Esther 1:6) explains it as silk.
- Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) cites and rejects this, preferring a color,
  an explanation Rashi and Ibn Ezra adopt as well, and Radak adds that the color
  is specifically green, as do Ralbag and Rabbi Yosef Nechemias.
- Targum Rishon and Targum Sheini associate *karpas* with *karti*, leek.
- The Gemara (Megillah 12a) reads it as a portmanteau of *karim*, pillows or
  blankets, and *passim*, silk.
- Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionary, makes it a loanword
  from the Persian *karpas*, fine linen, which he derives from the Old Indian
  *kaprasa*, cotton, and the Greek *karpasos*, fine flax.

Rabbinic Hebrew's common term is *tzemer gefen*, "vine wool," which happens to
be quite similar to the German *baumwolle*, "tree-wool"; Rambam, in his
commentary to Mishnah Kilayim 7:2, correctly renders *tzemer gefen* into Arabic
as *al-qoton*.

Is the Modern Hebrew *kotna* then derived from the Semitic *kitna*, "linen"?
Professor Kutscher answers no, resoundingly. He points to the English *cotton*,
Italian *cotone*, Spanish *algodon* and French *coton*, all of them from the
Arabic *qoton*, and for the Arabic word itself he accepts the etymology proposed
by the linguist Sir Godfrey Rolles Driver: the Semitic root kuf-tes-nun, "small,"
as in the Hebrew *katan*. Kutscher explains that cotton was called "small"
because its fibers tend to run finer and thinner than the hairs of wool, which are much
thicker (compare the thin threads called *ketini* at Shabbos 105a). So on his
argument *kotna* goes back to *katan*, and its phonetic likeness to *kesones* /
*kitna* is merely coincidental: *kotna* really should be spelled with kuf and
tes, the others with kaf and tav. Rashi indeed spells the French *coton* with kuf
and tes (Berachos 28b, Shabbos 21a, Shabbos 110b, Niddah 17a). Linguists
take the Aramaic *pekolin*, "cotton tufts," to be borrowed from the Greek
*phakelos*, "bundle" or "fascicle." Cotton, then, is not a type of linen.

## Rendering guidance

- Mishkan and *bigdei kehunah* passages: *sheish* is "fine linen," *bahd* is
  "linen" (the Yom Kippur *bigdei lavan*), *pishtim* is "flax" or "linen" by
  context. Holding these apart keeps Ralbag's material-versus-thread distinction
  visible.
- Where *sheish* is doing color work rather than fabric work (the Steipler's
  reading), do not silently write "linen." Give "fine linen" and let a note carry
  that the Gemara had to establish the material.
- *Bahd*: resist "alone" in a garment context and resist "linen" in a context of
  separateness. The same word does both, and which one the pasuk wants is a
  reading decision.
- Trap: English "linen" absorbs *sheish*, *bahd*, *butz* and *pishtim* alike. In
  a passage that lists more than one, give *pishtim* the plant word ("flax"),
  keep "fine linen" for *sheish* and *butz*, and let *bahd* stand as plain
  "linen."
- Trap: never render *kotna* or *tzemer gefen* as linen, or *kitna* as cotton.
  The two words look alike and belong to different roots.
