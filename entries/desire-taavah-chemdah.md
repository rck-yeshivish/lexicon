# Desire: taavah / chemdah / chefetz

## Bottom line

*Taavah* is **craving**, and in a mussar context **lust**: the desire whose
fulfillment is direct bodily pleasure, born of the man's own appetite and
imagination, and in Scripture almost always the desire for intimacy (Vilna Gaon;
Malbim). *Chemdah* is **coveting**: desire drawn by the object's own worth and
roused by seeing it, typically for money, a house, fine clothes (Vilna Gaon;
Rabbi Pappenheim and Rabbi Mecklenburg; Malbim). *Chefetz* sits between them,
the Vilna Gaon's word for desire for food; render "desire" and see the *ratzon*
entry. Chazal pair them: *chemdah* with theft, *taavah* with illicit relations
(Chagigah 11b). Do not let "desire" flatten the two; the contrast is inside
(*taavah*) against outside (*chemdah*).

## Distinctions

### taavah (תאוה)
Root א-ו-ה. The Vilna Gaon: desire whose fulfillment leads directly to bodily
pleasure; sensual desire; Chagigah 11b attaches it to *arayos*. Rabbi Pappenheim
and Rabbi Mecklenburg: a desire not derived from the object itself but from an
alignment of other factors, the subject's own proclivities, the time, the place.
Malbim: created by the man's own power of imagination, whether or not he ever saw
the object; not a reaction to outside stimuli but something that comes from
within. Both *taavah* and *chemdah* are self-damaging desires for Malbim. The
column's opening case: the *erev rav* "desired a desire", התאוו תאוה (Bamidbar
11:4), and the word itself is why Chazal read the weeping "according to its
families" (Bamidbar 11:10) as a grievance over whom one may marry, not over the
mann. The Yiddish *tayvah* keeps the same range.

### chemdah (חמדה)
Root ח-מ-ד. The Vilna Gaon: desire for something whose attainment is not itself a
bodily pleasure: elegant clothes, a nice house, a lot of money; Chagigah 11b
attaches it to theft, and in another place the Gaon calls it monetary desire.
Rabbi Pappenheim and Rabbi Mecklenburg: the desire follows from the inherent,
universally recognized properties of the object, so the man who covets is
responding to real advantages of possession, not to his appetite. Malbim: created
by visual stimuli; one sees and then wants. The three approaches converge (the
column): *chemdah* recognizes the desired thing's goodness, *taavah* ignores the
thing and fixes on the desirer's own craving.

### chefetz (חפץ)
Root ח-פ-ץ. The Vilna Gaon, contrasting three words: *taavah* sensual, *chemdah*
monetary, *chefetz* culinary. The column notes why this is the middle case: the
taste of good food is a value in the food itself (like *chemdah*), but the wish
to eat is tied to the eater's appetite (like *taavah*). Malbim sets *chefetz*
against *ratzon* rather than against these two: *chefetz* is an emotional desire,
*ratzon* an intellectual one and usually positive. The full treatment is in the
*ratzon* / *chefetz* entry.

## Rendering guidance

- *taavah*: "craving" as the neutral word; "lust" where the object is intimacy
  or the register is mussar; "appetite" for the bodily faculty in general.
  Plural *taavos*: "cravings", "lusts". *Baal taavah*: "a man of appetite".
- *chemdah*: "covet" as a verb, "coveting" as the noun; the adjective *nechmad*
  is "desirable" (the thing's own worth, which is the point). "Greed" is
  acceptable when the object is money, since that is the Gaon's own example.
- *chefetz*: "desire"; never "lust".
- Together, Chagigah 11b: "coveting leads to theft, craving to *arayos*." Keep
  two English words; the Gemara's whole point is the pairing.
- Trap: "desire" absorbs all three. A second trap: "lust" for *chemdah*. The
  Gaon's rule is that *chemdah* is never a bodily pleasure, so "lust for money"
  is a mistranslation of the Hebrew even where it is idiomatic English.
- The inside/outside test settles hard cases: if the passage stresses that the
  man wanted before he ever saw (imagination, appetite), it is *taavah* and
  reads "craving"; if it stresses the thing's attractions, it is *chemdah* and
  reads "coveting".
