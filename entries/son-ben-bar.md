# Son: ben / bar

## Bottom line

Simplistically speaking, both mean **son** and the difference is language, not
sense: *ben* is Hebrew, *bar* is its Aramaic equivalent, which is why Targum
renders *ben* as *bar* everywhere. Render both "son of," and keep whichever form the text itself uses
in a name. What must not be lost is that *bar* in Aramaic also means **outside**
or **separate**, which is why halachah prefers *ben* in a get and why the
darshanim read a lesson into *bar mitzvah*.

## Distinctions

### ben (בן)
The Hebrew word, and the word of Tanach. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau
(1740-1814) traces it, as is his way, to the two-letter root ב-נ, building and
producing: children are produced from their parents' marriage. From the same
root he brings *binyan* (building), the building materials *even* (stone) and
*teven* (straw), *avnayim* (a birthing chair, which helps the birthing along),
and *binah*/*tevunah* (understanding), which are the products of contemplation
and thought. He holds that *bas* (daughter) is also from ב-נ and should really
be spelled *banas*, as it is in other Semitic languages, the nun generally
dropping out in the singular and returning in the plural *banos*.

### bar (בר)
The Aramaic word, at home in Aramaic texts. Wherever *ben* appears in Tanach the
Targumim render it *bar*. The one apparent exception is Mishlei 31:2, a Hebrew
pasuk that uses *bar*, and Malbim there argues that the word is Hebrew and means
"choicest," not the Aramaic for son.

*Bar* in Aramaic also means outside or separate, in constant use in the Gemara;
a *baraisa* is a Tannaic teaching from outside the Mishnah. Rabbi Pappenheim
traces this second sense to the two-letter root ב-ר, exclusion or separation:
*bar* is grain separated from its chaff, *bor*/*boris* is a cleaning agent used
to separate and exclude filth, and *bari* (against *shema*) is a sort of
certainty in which all other options have been conclusively excluded; to which
the article adds the common *bar minan*, "except from us." On this reading *bar*
means son because the child was separated from his mother's person by the act of
childbirth.

That double meaning has a halachic consequence. Rabbi Yaakov Margolis of
Regensburg (1430-1501) writes in *Seder HaGet* that in a get one writes
"so-and-so *ben* so-and-so" and not *bar*, even though the Aramaic *bar* is used
in all other legal documents, because a get must be as clear as possible and
*bar* carries more than one meaning. Rabbi Shlomo Luria (1510-1573), the
Maharshal, supports the ruling for a different reason: in this context nobody
would think *bar* means "outside," but it might be misconstrued as an acronym for
*ben reb*, which runs against the custom of keeping honorifics out of a get.
Rabbi Yosef Colon (1420-1480), the Maharik, contests the ruling; the Rema, Rabbi
Moshe Isserles (1520-1572), who himself died on Lag B'Omer, codified it twice in
his glosses to Shulchan Aruch (*Even HaEzer* 126:30 and 129:7).

### bat / bas (בת) and the bar mitzvah
The Aramaic for daughter is *barat*, but the Targumim tend to leave the Hebrew
*bas* untranslated (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur, *Meturgaman*), so *bas* serves in
Aramaic too; hence *bas mitzvah* even in an Aramaic phrase. Why the boy is a
*bar mitzvah* rather than a *ben mitzvah* the article says it answered by
scouring different sources and coming up with three possible lessons the word
*bar* may be teaching him. Rabbi David Ovadiah of Tiberias, a nephew of Rabbi
Ovadiah Yosef, in the name of Rabbi Meir Simcha of Dvinsk (1843-1926): because
*bar* also means outside, teaching the boy that he now stands outside, on the
threshold of entering adult life, and at that fateful juncture has the capacity
to decide which path he takes. The late Rabbi Alexander Sender Feuerstein of
London: the Aramaic word conveys that he must master not only the whole Written
Torah, mostly written in Hebrew, but the Oral Torah, mostly written in Aramaic.
Rabbi Meir Mintzberg, son-in-law of the late Rabbi Leib Mintzberg, the leader of
the Yerushalmi "Masmidim," takes a different approach: like Rabbi Pappenheim he
reads *ben* from building and producing, the son as the product of his parents'
building and nurturing, and *bar* as the opposite connotation, the son as
separate and independent of them, which is exactly what the day marks, that his
growth is no longer in the hands of the parents who built him up but is now his
own responsibility.

### Rashbi, "Rabbi Shimon" and "Rabbi Shimon ben/bar Yochai"
The article's opening question is the Lag B'Omer one, whether Rashbi is Rabbi
Shimon *bar* Yochai or *ben* Yochai, and it closes by noting that wherever the
Mishnah or Gemara says plain "Rabbi Shimon" it means Rashbi. Rabbi Yehuda ben
Kalonymos of Speyer, a 12th-century Tosafist, in his lexicon of the sages
*Yichusei Tannaim V'Amoraim*: whatever Rashbi said before he was famous is
ascribed to "Rabbi Shimon ben/bar Yochai" so that it is clear who said it, and
what he said once he was already an important figure is attributed to "Rabbi
Shimon" plain, because everyone already knew who that was.

## Rendering guidance

- Render both "son of," and do not translate the connector in a name. "Rabbi
  Shimon bar Yochai" stays *bar*; "Yehoshua bin Nun" stays *bin*. Do not
  normalize one to the other, and note that Rashbi appears in both forms.
- Trap: an English "son of" absorbs *ben* and *bar* alike, so any source
  discussing why a get says *ben*, or why the boy is a *bar mitzvah*, cannot be
  translated word for word; keep the Hebrew and Aramaic words themselves in the
  sentence and explain around them.
- *Bar* in its other sense: "outside," "external," and in a Talmudic context
  often best left as *baraisa*, *bar minan*, *milsa d'bar*.
- *Ben* in the construct before a noun works as an adjective in Hebrew (a
  *ben mavess*, a *ben shanah*); render by sense, not by "son of." See
  *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 12:43.
