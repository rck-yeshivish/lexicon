# Death: mitah / petirah (nifter)

## Bottom line

*Mitah* (*maves*, *meis*) is **death** as the physical end, the parting of body
and soul, said of anyone and of animals. *Petirah* (*nifter*) is **passing**,
literally "exiting, taking leave," the Rabbinic word for a person leaving This
World for the Next; it is reserved by usage for the righteous and implies a
continuation. Render *nifter* as "passed away" or "departed," never as "died"
when the text sets it against *meis*; and do not soften a pasuk's *mos* into
"passing."

## Distinctions

### mitah (מיתה), maves (מות), meis (מת)
The triliteralists (Rabbi Yehuda Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon, Radak)
trace it to מ-ו-ת; the biliteralists (Menachem ibn Saruk in *Machberes
Menachem*, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim in *Yerios Shlomo* and *Cheshek Shlomo*) to
מ-ת. Close to a thousand occurrences in Tanach. The surrounding pagan cultures
deified death (the Ugaritic Mot); Chazal speak instead of the *Malach HaMaves*,
an angel wholly subservient to Hashem. *Mitah* applies to any living being that
undergoes the separation of body from soul (halachah pegs death to the exit of
the soul, Yevamos 16:3, Ohalos 1:6); it names the conclusion of life and says
nothing of what follows. Even the wicked have a *mitah*.

### petirah (פטירה), nifter (נפטר)
Root פ-ט-ר, "exit": in Tanach the firstborn that opens the womb (*peter
rechem*); in Rabbinic Hebrew to be discharged of an obligation or to take leave
of a companion; and the legal *patur*, exempt, one who has exited the realm of
the obligated. For death, the Mishnah's *nifter min ha'olam* (Peah 8:9) and the
Bavli's variants (Berachos 17a, Kesubos 68a, 104a-105b, Kiddushin 72b,
Sanhedrin 113b); standing alone of a named person only for Avraham (Bava Basra
16b) and Moshe (Temurah 16a). The Gemara generally avoids *meis* for a person's
passing; another of its idioms is *nach nafshei*, "his soul rested."

The article's own account of the link between *petirah* and *patur*: while the
soul dwells in the body it binds the body to a higher purpose and to Hashem's
law; when the soul departs, the body is exempt of all obligation. One who lived
for a higher purpose is *nifter* when he dies, exiting one room to enter
another on the same trajectory; hence Chazal's ruling that the righteous are
called alive even in death and the wicked dead even in life (Berachos 18a-b),
and hence *petirah* is generally reserved for the righteous. Rabbi Moshe
Shapiro: mushrooms are *pitriyos* (Uktzin 3:2) because, rootless, they are
*patur* of any bond to a source of life, and such a life is nearer death.
Rabbi Avraham Chaim Schor: the pious man's life in This World is full of
physical suffering, and at death he is exempted from it; so *nifter* is said
specifically of a *tzaddik*, *mitah* of death in general.

## Rendering guidance

- מות / מיתה in a pasuk or a halachic setting: "death," "died"; keep the
  plainness. מיתת בית דין: "death penalty." *Malach HaMaves*: transliterate
  or "the Angel of Death."
- נפטר / פטירה of a person: "passed away," "his passing," "departed this
  world"; the *min ha'olam* idiom may be kept: "departed from the world."
- Trap: English "death" and "died" absorb both. In a text that pairs them
  (Chazal's *tzaddikim* alive and *reshaim* dead, or a shmuess on why the
  Gemara says *nifter* and not *meis*), keep "died" for *meis* and "passed"
  or "departed" for *nifter*, and gloss the Hebrew once.
- Do not upgrade: a pasuk's *vayamas* is "and he died," even of a *tzaddik*;
  the honorific belongs to Rabbinic *petirah*, not to the Torah's *mos*.
- *Patur* in halachah stays "exempt"; do not let the derashah on
  *patur*/*nifter* pull the legal term toward "released."
