# Death: mitah / petirah (nifter)

## Bottom line

*Mitah* (*maves*, *meis*) is **death** as the physical end, the parting of body
and soul, said of anyone and of animals. *Petirah* (*nifter*) is **passing**,
literally "exiting, taking leave," the Rabbinic word for a person leaving This
World for the Next; it is generally reserved for the righteous and implies a
continuation. *Nifter* is "passed away" or "departed."

## Distinctions

### mitah (מיתה), maves (מות), meis (מת)
*Maves* is a good test case for how the lexicographers found roots.

- The triliteralists (Rabbi Yehuda Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon, Radak)
  trace it to מ-ו-ת.
- the biliteralists (Menachem Ibn Saruk in *Machberes Menachem*, Rabbi Shlomo
  Pappenheim in *Yerios Shlomo* and *Cheshek Shlomo*) to מ-ת.

Cognates of these roots appear close to a thousand times in Tanach, by Even
Shoshan's concordance. Declensions of *maves*/*mitah* occur twenty-four times in
Acharei Mos and Kedoshim (Vayikra 16-20), more than in any other two consecutive
parshiyos, and the sidra opens *acharei mos* the two sons of Aharon (Vayikra
16:1).

In the ancient Canaanite cultures around Eretz Yisrael, death was personified and
deified as the god Mot (also spelled Mawet or Motu); those cultures tended to see
it as an independent entity on a par with the other gods and sometimes competing
with them; in Ugaritic mythology
Mot rules the underworld and is one of Baal's chief rivals, with a parallel in
the Greek Hades. That differs from the scientific view of death as a natural
process affecting every living thing. Rabbinic discourse takes a middle
approach: the *Malach HaMaves* is said to hold power over death, but as an angel
is wholly subservient to Hashem, not an independent death deity.

*Mitah* applies to any living being that undergoes the separation of body from
soul (halachah pegs death to the exit of the soul, Yevamos 16:3, Ohalos 1:6); it
names the conclusion of life and says nothing of what follows. Even the wicked
have a *mitah*.

Shadal (to Bereishis 25:8): *misah* is the general word; *geviah* is the death
of the body and *asifah* the death of the soul.

### petirah (פטירה), nifter (נפטר)
Root פ-ט-ר, "exit": in Tanach the firstborn animal that exits its mother's womb
(*peter rechem*); in Rabbinic Hebrew to be discharged of an obligation or to take
leave of a companion; and, most frequently of all, the legal *patur*, exempt, one
who has figuratively exited the realm of the obliged and beholden and so is free
of the burden of compulsion. (The English *exempt* itself comes from the Latin *exemptus*, literally "taking out, away.")
For death, the Mishnah's *nifter min ha'olam* (Peah 8:9) and the
Bavli's variants (Berachos 17a, Kesubos 68a, 104a-105b, Kiddushin 72b,
Sanhedrin 113b); standing alone of a named person only for Avraham (Bava Basra
16b) and Moshe (Temurah 16a). The Gemara generally avoids *meis* for a person's
passing; another of its idioms is *nach nafshei*, "his soul rested."

- Rabbi Klein's own account of the link between *petirah* and *patur*: while the
  soul dwells in the body it binds the body to a higher purpose and to Hashem's
  law; when the soul departs, the body is exempt of all obligation. One who
  lived for a higher purpose is *nifter* when he dies, exiting one room to enter
  another on the same trajectory; to this effect Chazal state that the righteous
  are called alive even in death and the wicked dead even in life (Berachos
  18a-b); and so *petirah* is generally reserved for the righteous.
- Rabbi Moshe Shapiro observes that plants, rooted firmly in the ground, are
  tied to their lifeline in the soil as a man is tied to his soul, and that the connection shows the plant, as it grows, joining to something loftier; mushrooms and the other fungi rise rootless, free-floating
  and self-serving, disconnected from any accountability, and so are called
  *pitriyos* (Uktzin 3:2), *patur* of obligation. Such a life, though it might
  technically count as life, is nearer death than life.
- Rabbi Avraham Chaim Schor: the pious man's life in This World is often full of
  physical suffering, and at death he is exempted from it; so *nifter* is said
  specifically of a *tzaddik*, *mitah* of death in general.

## Rendering guidance

- מות / מיתה in a pasuk or a halachic setting: "death," "died"; keep the
  plainness. מיתת בית דין: "death penalty." *Malach HaMaves*: transliterate
  or "the Angel of Death."
- נפטר / פטירה of a person: "passed away," "his passing," "departed this
  world"; the *min ha'olam* idiom may be kept: "departed from the world."
- Trap: English "death" and "died" absorb both. In a text that pairs them
  (Chazal's *tzaddikim* alive and *reshaim* dead, or a discussion of why the
  Gemara says *nifter* and not *meis*), keep "died" for *meis* and "passed"
  or "departed" for *nifter*, and gloss the Hebrew once.
- Do not upgrade: a pasuk's *vayamas* is "and he died," even of a *tzaddik*;
  the honorific belongs to Rabbinic *petirah*, not to the Torah's *mos*.
- *Patur* in halachah stays "exempt"; do not let the derashah on
  *patur*/*nifter* pull the legal term toward "released."
