# Prayer: tefillah / bakashah / techinah

## Bottom line

*Tefillah* is prayer proper. Rabbi Simcha Maimon offers two ways of taking it
against *bakashah*: either it is the praising side of prayer and *bakashah* the
asking side, or it is the fixed, formal **prayer** with its nusach and its
halachos against an *ad hoc* asking. On Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg's reading
of the root it is a plea that Hashem set right what the petitioner feels is
unjust. *Bakashah* is the **request**, the unscripted asking for one's needs.
*Techinah* or *tachanun* is
**supplication**: the appeal of one who admits he has no claim and asks to be
given anyway, out of *chen*, favor. Where Yaakov's sword and bow are darshened
(*Bava Basra* 123a), *tefillah* and *bakashah* stand side by side, which suggests
they are not the same thing.

## Distinctions

On his deathbed Yaakov leaves Yosef the city of Shechem, "which I took from the
Emori with my sword and with my bow" (Bereishis 48:22). Yaakov was neither a
swordsman nor an archer; his power was in his voice (Bereishis 27:22). So the
mesorah (*Bava Basra* 123a) reads the pasuk non-literally: "my sword" is
*tefillah*, prayer, and "my bow" is *bakashah*, request. Targum Onkelos renders
the words the same way, *tzeloti* and *bausi*. That the two stand side by side
suggests they are not the same thing.

### tefillah (תפילה)
Root פ-ל-ל.

- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg derives *tefillah* from *pelilah*, arbitration
  or judgment (in Modern Hebrew the word means "criminal"): the one who prays
  presses his case before Hashem, demanding that He address a perceived
  injustice.
- The Maharal (*Be'er HaGolah* §4:2) proposes instead that the core meaning of
  פ-ל-ל is intellectuality or thoughtfulness: *tefillah* is a thoughtful plea
  and appeal to the One Above, as judges are called *pelilim* (Shemos 21:22)
  because they use their minds to figure out how to decide the law.
- Rabbi Simcha Maimon, a prominent Rosh Yeshiva in Yerushalayim, gives two
  readings against *bakashah*. First, *tefillah* is the "flattering" side of
  prayer, in which one praises Hashem, while *bakashah* is the "wishful" side,
  in which one asks Him for one's needs. Second, *tefillah* is formal,
  instituted prayer with set formulae and set halachos, while *bakashah* is *ad
  hoc*. On the second reading, *tefillah* answers to Yaakov's sword: the words
  themselves carry inherent power, as a sword is dangerous by its own sharpness.

### bakashah (בקשה)
Root ב-ק-ש. The request made of Hashem in one's own words. The link between
*bakashos* and *keshasos*, bows, may simply be homiletical, since the two words
are spelled and sounded so much alike.

- Rabbi Simcha Maimon nonetheless works out a conceptual link: because a
  *bakashah* lacks the formality of *tefillah* it is not inherently a powerful
  form of prayer, just as an archer's projectile need not be as sharp as a
  sword; but an arrow's efficacy lies in the kinetic energy of its release, just as a *bakashah*'s lies in the urgency the supplicant feels.
- Rabbi Shmuel Laniado, a sixteenth-century Syrian scholar, offers a similar
  observation on the bow: an archer strikes without having to approach his
  enemy, and so a *bakashah* lets a man standing in a dire situation in This
  World, without leaving where he is, send his prayer through the upper realms
  to the Heavens.
- The Gemara (*Berachos* 31a), on *lishmoa el harinah ve'el hatefillah*: *rinah*
  is *tefillah* and *tefillah* is *bakashah*; Rashi explains *tefillah* as
  prayer of praise and *bakashah* as asking for one's needs.

### techinah / tachanun (תחינה, תחנון)
Root ח-נ-ן, from *chen*, favor. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg: the one who says
*tachanun* appeals as one who admits he is not worthy of what he asks and
beseeches Hashem to grant it graciously anyway, hoping to find favor in His eyes
undeserving as he is. This is the opposite footing from *tefillah* on his
reading: *tefillah* presses a claim, *techinah* disclaims one.

## Rendering guidance

- Bereishis 48:22 with *Bava Basra* 123a: "my sword" is *tefillah* and "my bow"
  is *bakashah*. Render them "prayer" and "request," never "prayer" twice.
  Onkelos already separates them, *tzeloti* and *bausi*.
- *Tachanunim* in a pasuk or a piyut: "supplications," or "pleas for favor"
  where the *chen* is the point. Do not use "prayers."
- *Bakashah* standing alone usually means the asking part of davening;
  "request" or "petition" both work, but stay consistent.
- Trap: English "prayer" absorbs all three, and "supplication" will absorb
  *bakashah* and *techinah* together. Where a passage lists two of them, or
  builds on the contrast (a claim of justice against a plea for favor), give each
  its own English word and gloss the Hebrew on first use.
