# Dirt: zuhamah / lichluch / tinuf / go'al / elach

## Bottom line

*Go'al* is dirtying by something inherently disgusting (blood, excrement,
forbidden food): **defilement, befouling**. *Tinuf* and *lichluch* are dirtying
by something not necessarily disgusting in itself but unwanted where it landed
(Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim): **soiling** and **grime**, with *lichluch* wet in its
one mishnaic use (*Mikvaos* 9:4). *Zuhamah* is the unwanted residue that clings and
usually smells: **filth**. *Elach* is the spoiling of something homogeneous by
an incompatible admixture: **tainted, sullied**. The difference is whether the dirtying agent is disgusting
in itself (*go'al*), merely misplaced (*tinuf*, *lichluch*), or a foreign
element mixed in (*elach*). The distinctions are not agreed, though: Rabbi
Tanchum HaYerushalmi writes fairly clearly, in at least two places, that
*tinuf*, *lichluch* and *zuhamah* are all synonymous, and Pappenheim himself
seems to equate *lichluch* with *tinuf*, as do Ibn Janach and Radak.

## Distinctions

### elach (אלח)
Root א-ל-ח; three occurrences in Tanach (Iyov 15:16, Tehillim 14:3, 53:4).
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: not soiling by something disgusting, but the ruin
  that comes when something of another type is mixed into an otherwise uniform
  substance (grape juice poured into milk is now undrinkable, and only because
  the combination is incompatible). Pappenheim takes the initial *aleph* as
  extraneous and the working root as the two-letter ל-ח, "liquid" (as in *davar
  lach*), so *elach* is an unwanted liquid sullying something else.
- The classical lexicographers, among them Menachem Ibn Saruk, Yonah Ibn Janach
  and Radak, trace the term to the full triliteral root א-ל-ח.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 14:3) links it to א-ל-ה, which yields *allah*,
  "curse," through the interchange of *hei* and *ches*: both name a clash in
  which one party is set against the other.

### lichluch (לכלוך)
Post-biblical; absent from Tanach, once in the Mishnah, then the standard word
for dirtiness in the Gemara and later. Dr. Alexander Kohut (*HeAruch
HaShaleim*) sees *lichluch*, with a *kaf*, as synonymous with its near-homonym
*lichluach*, with a *ches*, which means wet or liquid and derives from the
biblical two-letter ל-ח. That biblical root, Rabbi Klein suggests, might just
be the root of *lichluch* as well. That fits its one mishnaic use: *lichluch* of wet feces on
the skin is no *chatzitzah* for
immersion (*Mikvaos* 9:4), since the wet grime merges with the *mikvah* water,
while the same matter dried would interpose. Rashi uses *lichluch* to describe
the spiritual grime of forbidden food (to Daniel 1:8). The Gemara's anecdote in
*Nedarim* 66b turns on a woman named *Lichluchis*, "dirty," and Rabbi Yishmael
ben Rabbi Yose's ruling that the name fits.

### tinuf (טינוף)
Root ט-נ-פ, once in Tanach: "I have washed my feet, how shall I dirty them"
(Shir HaShirim 5:3). Three mishnaic uses: *tinofes*, spoiled produce tolerated
up to a twenty-fourth in a wholesale purchase (*Bava Basra* 6:2); uterine blood
that has *tinuf* (*Bechoros* 3:1); feet dirtied in a cave pool (*Machshirin*
4:5). *Tinuf* also serves in the Targumim as the Aramaic rendering of the
biblical *tinuf* (Shir HaShirim 5:3), of *go'al* (Eichah 4:14) and of *elach*
(Tehillim 14:3, 53:4), though some versions of the Targum to Tehillim 14:3 use
a different word.

Pappenheim writes that all the words under discussion refer to something
unwanted becoming attached to an object in such a way that the object is now
"disgusting" or "disgraced," so that a person is disinclined to use it. Within
that, he holds that with *tinuf*, as with *lichluch*, the dirtying agent is not
necessarily repulsive in itself; ketchup is desirable as food until it lands on
a shirt. So too the lover of Shir HaShirim: the dirt on the floor may not be
undesirable in itself, but clinging to her newly washed feet it makes them feel dirty. In a
separate discussion Pappenheim derives *tinuf* from the biblical two-letter
root ט-פ. *Machberet Menachem* lists four categories of words as coming from
that root: *taf*, children; *notef*/*tipah*, dripping; *netifos*, adornments
and niceties; and *matif*, speech or preaching. Pappenheim gives the core sense
of the root as "secondary, subordinate," and reads *tinuf* accordingly: the
dirtying element is secondary to the object it clings to.

Shadal (to Bamidbar 35:33) points out that both Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon and
Wilhelm Gesenius explained *chonef*, often translated flattery or hypocrisy, as
related to *tinuf*, both naming something polluting or defiling, one in a moral
sense and one in a physical one. Rabbi Klein adds that the philological basis
for the connection is presumably the interchangeability of *ches* and *tes*,
which are consecutive letters.

### go'al (גועל)
Thirteen occurrences in Tanach, on Even-Shoshan's concordance. Pappenheim:
dirtied by a pollutant that is
disgusting in its own right, excrement (Tzefaniah 3:1), blood (Yeshayahu 59:3,
Eichah 4:14). Daniel refused to *defile* himself (*yisga'al*) with the king's
food (Daniel 1:8); Hashem's garments are *befouled* (*ega'alti*) with Edom's
blood (Yeshayahu 63:3). Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg ties it to *geulah* and *go'el*
under ג-(א)-ל: a single axis of nearness and distance, with *go'al* naming what
one distances oneself from and redemption naming the drawing near; he also links
ג-ע-ל, "repugnant," through the interchange of *aleph* and *ayin*. Pappenheim,
taking a slightly different approach, ties it to the two-letter ג-ל, "round,"
through *galal*, the typically round pellets of animal droppings that no one
disputes are filth.

### zuhamah (זוהמה)
Root ז-ה-ם, once in Tanach: to the sick man food looks like *zuhamah* (Iyov
33:20).
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): the foul sweat a body gives off, or the residue
  thrown off in preparing certain foods.
- Pappenheim reduces it to the single letter *zayin*, "movement," and reads it
  as the fluid that oozes out after an unhealthy build-up, hence anything excess
  and unwanted.
- Donash Ibn Labrat: *zuhamah* is specifically the bad *smell* of something
  dirty, while *tinuf* is plain soiling; an Arabic cognate, *zahuma*, likewise
  refers to something dirty or smelly.
- Rav Hirsch (to Tehillim 7:12) relates ז-ה-ם to ז-ע-ם, *za'am*, anger or curse:
  both name a thing rejected by the beholder.

In the Mishnah it covers a blemished, foul-smelling animal (*Bechoros* 6:12),
fish odor removed by an onion (*Terumos* 10:1), and treating saplings before
*shevi'is* (*Shevi'is* 2:4; manure per Rabbi Shimon of Shantz, foul-smelling oil
to repel insects per Rambam; see also Rabbi Tzemach Duran's responsa *Yachin
U'Boaz* I §114). The Gemara: one should use cold water for *mayim acharonim*,
because hot water is less effective at removing the *zuhamah* from the hands
(*Chullin* 105a), and one who is *mezuham* may not recite a berachah just as a
*mezuham* Kohen may not serve (*Berachos* 53b), a halachah taught by Rabbi
Zuhamai, so named for it (Rabbi Yaakov Emden there, who also notes the name
Zaham of Divrei HaYamim II 11:19, which seems to be of the same root). Pseudo-
Rashbam (to Tehillim 26:10) glosses *zimah* as *tinuf*, from which Rabbi
Steinberg suggests he read ז-מ-ה as a metathesis of ז-ה-ם. In Modern Hebrew
*mezuham* means "infected" in the bacterial sense.

In at least two places in *HaMadrich HaMaspik*, Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (a
thirteenth-century exegete who lived in Eretz Yisrael) writes fairly clearly
that *tinuf*, *lichluch* and *zuhamah* are all synonymous. Pappenheim too seems
to equate *lichluch* with *tinuf*, as do Ibn Janach and Radak in their
respective *Sifrei HaShorashim*. Rabbi Klein suggests that where they are
equated the difference may be etymological rather than semantic, and offers two
ways to argue it: *tinuf* seems to be actual biblical Hebrew while *lichluch*
seems to be a later Aramaic borrowing; or, alternatively, *lichluch* may derive
from biblical ל-ח while *tinuf* is the loanword that came in from Aramaic, on
the evidence that the Targumim favor it. Rabbi Shaul Goldman sums up what he
sees as the differences in rabbinic usage: *tinuf* usually for contamination by
bodily fluids or contact with animals, *lichluch* usually implying unwanted
wetness or moisture, *zuhamah* pointing to some unwanted element, usually a
contaminant.

## Rendering guidance

- The famous *zuhamah* of the snake (*Shabbos* 146a, *Yevamos* 103b, *Avodah
  Zarah* 26b), which the primordial snake injected into Chavah and of which all
  Israel was cured at Sinai, is best left transliterated in a mussar or aggadic
  context. On Rabbi Klein's reading, *zuhamah* in general means dirtiness, but
  in this context it may refer to physical or spiritual deformities, or to
  susceptibility to death, both of which temporarily left the Jewish People at
  Sinai. So "filth" alone loses the sense.
- Trap: English "defile" will swallow *go'al*, *tinuf* and *tamei* alike. Keep
  "defile" for *go'al* (the agent is genuinely repulsive), use "soil" for
  *tinuf*, and never let either stand for ritual *tumah*, which is a different
  category entirely.
- Second trap: "dirty" absorbs *lichluch* and *tinuf*. When a passage turns on
  the wet/dry distinction (*Mikvaos* 9:4), render *lichluch* as "wet grime" and
  keep "soiling" for *tinuf*.
- *Elach* rarely wants "dirty" at all. Tehillim 14:3, *ne'elachu*, reads better
  as "they have become tainted" or "gone rancid" than "they are filthy."
- *Mezuham* of a person (*Berachos* 53b) is "unclean and foul-smelling," not
  merely "dirty"; the odor is the operative fact.
