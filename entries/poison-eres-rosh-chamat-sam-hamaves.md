# Poison: eres / sam hamaves / rosh / chamat / ra'al

## Bottom line

*Eres* is **venom**, most often snake venom, and it is Talmudic, not Biblical.
*Sam hamaves* is the general **poison**, literally the potion of death, and
never venom. *Rosh* is **poison** in Tanach, drawn either from a snake's head or from
a poisonous plant. *Chamat* is **venom** as what an angered snake emits. *Ra'al*
is disputed: poison, or a draught that leaves a man limp and reeling. Chullin
3:5 shows that *sam hamaves* is not venom.

## Distinctions

### eres (ארס)
Not in Tanach or Mishnah; it appears in Hebrew passages of the Bavli and a
handful of times in the Yerushalmi, there almost always with a yud after the
aleph, so likely *iras*. The Gemara's usage fixes the sense. Avodah Zarah 30b:
three kinds of *eres*, a young snake's which sinks in a liquid, an old snake's
which flows on top, a middle-aged one's which dallies in between (Sukkah 50a,
Bava Kamma 115b). Avodah Zarah 31b: all *sheratzim* have *eres*, but only a
snake's is lethal. Sanhedrin 78a and Bava Kamma 23b ask whether a snake releases
venom only when angered or whether the venom sits always on its teeth. So *eres* is specifically venom, most often a snake's; this is what
Bigsan and Seresh meant to put in Achashverosh's cup (Megillah 13b). An
alternate tradition has them planning to kill him in his sleep and present his
severed head to the Greek kings (*Yossiphon* ch. 4), and *Targum Rishon* to
Esther 2:21 combines the two, having them poison Esther with a *sam hamaves*
first and then slay her husband; *Targum Sheini* there has them put a
poisonous snake in the king's golden cup instead.

The origins of *eres* are shrouded in mystery.

- Eminent etymologists derive it from the Latin *virus*: Dr. Alexander Kohut
  (1842-1894), Rabbi Ernest Klein (1899-1983) and Avraham Even-Shoshan
  (1906-1984).
- The *Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon* calls that derivation "highly dubious," so
  the question stands open.

### sam hamaves (סם המות)
Literally the potion of death. Not in Tanach; once in Mishnah, where an animal
that swallowed *sam hamaves* or was bitten by a snake is no tereifah but is
still forbidden as dangerous (Chullin 3:5). The two are listed separately there,
which shows *sam hamaves* is not venom. In the Gemara: whether a radish is *sam
hachaim* or *sam hamaves* (Eruvin 56a), Torah learned without proper intent
(Taanis 7a), a mother's oath on her child's life (Gittin 35a), and liability for
killing another's animal by feeding it (Bava Kamma 47b, 56a).

- Because sin and samech are often understood to interchange, *Midrash Sechel
  Tov*, *Midrash Aggadah* and *Daas Zekeinim* read *vayusam lefanav le'echol*
  (Bereishis 24:33) as a hint that Besuel tried to poison Eliezer; a commentary
  ascribed to Rashi on Divrei HaYamim II 22:10 has Asaliah killing this way too.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814) takes ס-ם / ש-ם as putting a
  thing in its place, and derives *samim*, the ketores ingredients (Shemos 30:7,
  30:34, 40:27; Vayikra 16:12; Divrei HaYamim II 2:3, 13:11), from their being
  stowed in specially designated places.

It seems from all this, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein concludes, that *sam hamaves*
is the general word, covering any lethal preparation and naming none in
particular.

Targum Yonasan renders *nashchisah etz belachmo* (Yirmiyahu 11:19) as *nirmei
sama demosa bemeichleih*, let us cast deadly poison into his food.

### rosh (ראש / רוש), chamat (חמת)
*Rosh*: Devarim 29:17, Iyov 20:16, Eichah 3:19 and elsewhere.

- Targum and Rashi (to Yirmiyahu 8:14) take it as poison drawn from a snake's
  *rosh*, its head, so essentially venom. It is normally spelled with an aleph,
  like the word for head, once with a vav (Devarim 32:32).
- Other sources take it as a poisonous plant extract, Rabbi Dr. Yehuda Feliks
  (1921-2004) writing that *rosh* is best identified with poppy and opium or
  with conium, and to that effect Pappenheim traces it to ר-ש, head, for that
  plant's round top, which resembles a head.
- Rav Hirsch (to Devarim 29:17) clarifies that this deadly extract can come from
  both the plant kingdom and the animal kingdom.

*Chamat* (Devarim 32:33, Tehillim 58:5) is poison named for what a snake emits
when its *cheimah* is aroused.

### ra'al (רעל) and tarelah (תרעלה)
Nachum 2:4, Zecharyah 12:2; *tarelah* in Tehillim 60:5, Yeshayahu 51:17, 51:22.

- Commonly rendered poison, but Rashi (to Yeshayahu 51:17 and Nachum 2:4) says
  it is a concoction that leaves a man weak and immobile, as though tied up and
  detained. A near word *re'alah* (Yeshayahu 3:19) is an ornament one wraps
  oneself in.
- Radak (to Yeshayahu 51:17 and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) cites the poison reading
  but also defines the word as shake, rattle and roll, akin to *ro'ed*, to which
  it might be etymologically connected through the interchangeability of lamed
  and dalet.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein offers his own hedged reconciliation of the two
readings: perhaps *ra'al* names a toxic contagion that throws a man into seizure-like convulsions, rather than a lethal poison. In Modern Hebrew *ra'al* is deadly
poison.

### eches (עכס)
A separate dispute, since Rashi reads it as venom. Yeshayahu 3:16 has
*uvragleihem te'akasnah* as a verb, and one that appears nowhere else in Tanach,
so what it denotes is unclear; 3:18 has *ha'achasim* as an ornament, which Rashi
and Ibn Ezra (to 3:18) explain as special shoes.

- The Gemara (Shabbos 62b, Yoma 9b): the Bnos Tzion put perfumes in their shoes
  and, meeting the young men in Yerushalayim's markets, kicked their feet to
  spray the fragrance at them shamefully, infecting them with the yetzer hara,
  which works like *eres*. On that basis Rashi (to Yeshayahu 3:16) glosses
  *eches* as snake venom and the verb as poisoning them to sin; the Vilna Gaon
  joins the two senses by taking *eches* as shoes enclosed in snakeskin.
- Ibn Ezra and Radak (to 3:16) dissent, tying the verb to the noun: the women
  drew attention to themselves by the noise of their shoes, which Radak adds
  chimed like bells in a steady beat. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds a suggestion
  of his own, which these commentators do not make: perhaps that sense too runs
  back to snakes, since the sound resembles a rattlesnake's rattle.
- At Mishlei 7:22 Rashi again reads venom, while Rabbi Yeshayah of Trani
  (1180-1250) takes it as a prisoner's ball and chain, which ties back to the
  shoe sense since both are fastened to the feet (so too Ibn Ezra there and
  Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim*); the point of the analogy, Rabbi Klein suggests,
  is presumably that a sin is bound to a man forever and resurfaces on the Day
  of Judgment (Sotah 3b).

Others explain *eches* as "dog."

- Dr. Alexander Kohut was the first to note that Rashi's reading of *eches* as
  snake venom was likely informed by the Greek *echis*, viper, and *echis* does
  seem to appear in rabbinic literature in that sense, since the Midrash renders
  *efeh*, a Biblical snake, as *echis* (*Midrash Tanchuma*, *Beshalach* 18, and
  *Mechilta* to Shemos 15:22).
- Rashi himself (to Shabbos 62b) gives a different etymology of *eches*: a
  metathesis of *ka'as*, anger, since a snake emits venom only when angered.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim supposes the converse, that *ka'as* is a metathesis of
  *eches*, since anger puts a sort of poison in a man's heart.

Either way, the connection is already in the Gemara, which expounds the name of
Kalev's daughter Achsah (Yehoshua 15:16-17, Shoftim 1:12-13, Divrei HaYamim I
2:49) as one at whom every man who saw her grew angry (*ka'as*) at his wife,
which Rashi explains to mean that she was so beautiful that the comparison might
make a man angry at his wife for falling short; the exegesis rests on the
metathesis of ע-כ-ס against כ-ע-ס. The great Italian mekubal Rabbi Menachem
Azariah of Fano (1548-1620) reads her name as marking her a gilgul of Tzipporah,
who saved Moshe from death by *nachash* through circumcising their child (Shemos
4:24-26).

### la'anah (לענה)
Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino (*Ohel Moed*) counts *la'anah* a synonym of *rosh*,
*chamat* and *ra'al*, all poison. This is quite novel: the earlier commentators,
such as Rashi and Radak, call *la'anah* very bitter but do not write that it is
deadly.

## Rendering guidance

- *Eres*: "venom." Never "poison" in a sugya where a snake is in view, since
  Avodah Zarah 31b turns on which creatures' *eres* is lethal.
- *Sam hamaves*: "deadly poison," or "a deadly drug" where the substance is fed
  or administered. Chullin 3:5 lists it beside a snakebite, so rendering it
  "venom" makes the Mishnah repeat itself.
- *Rosh* in a pasuk: "poison," with the *rosh*/head wordplay noted where Rashi's
  derivation is cited; paired with *la'anah* (Devarim 29:17), "poison and
  wormwood," which keeps Rashi and Radak against Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino.
- *Chamat taninim* (Devarim 32:33): "the venom of serpents," not "poison."
- *Ra'al* / *tarelah*: "a reeling draught" or "cup of staggering" following
  Rashi, "poison" only if the reader is told which reading is taken.
- Trap: English "poison" will take *eres*, *sam hamaves*, *rosh*, *chamat* and
  *ra'al* without protest, and "venom" will take *eres*, *rosh* and *chamat*.
  When two appear in one passage, force the split: venom for what a snake emits,
  poison for what a man administers.
- *Eches* (Yeshayahu 3:16): pick a side and say so. "They poison with their
  feet" is Rashi; "they tinkle with their feet" is Ibn Ezra and Radak.
