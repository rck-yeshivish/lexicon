# Elephant: pil / shenhav

## Bottom line

*Pil* is the ordinary word for **elephant**, but it is post-biblical; it belongs
to the language of Chazal, not to Tanach. *Shenhav* (plural *shenhabim*) is the
biblical word, and most of the commentators read it as **ivory**, not the animal
(Targum, Rashi, Radak, Metzudos, Ralbag, Rabbi Yosef Karo). A minority read it
as the elephant itself: HaKallir, Rabbi Avraham ben Shlomo, and seemingly Ibn
Janach, as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein understands him. The commentary printed as
Rashi to Divrei HaYamim takes it as both the tusk and the animal. Even if it
means the elephant, Rabbi Klein holds, *shenhav* focuses on the beast
specifically as the source of ivory, while post-biblical *pil* names it for
other properties, its superlative mass or possibly its knack for burying the
dead.

## Distinctions

### shenhav (שנהב)
Twice in Tanach, in the identical lists of what Shlomo HaMelech's ships brought
back: gold and silver, *shenhabim*, monkeys and peacocks (Melachim I 10:22;
Divrei HaYamim II 9:21). The whole question is which half of the list it joins,
the precious materials or the exotic animals.
- The *taamim* suggest that *shenhabim* goes with the animals.
- The commentators mostly go the other way: the Targum to both places renders it
  *shen d'pil*, an elephant's tooth, that is ivory, and Rashi, Radak, the
  Metzudos, Ralbag, and Rabbi Yosef Karo follow.
- Abarbanel (to Melachim I 10:22) records that the Christian commentators read
  it as pearls, which is again a material rather than an animal.
- On the other side, the commentary printed under Rashi's name to Divrei HaYamim
  (not his) says *shenhav* means both the tusk and the animal, tying the word to
  the clauses on either side of it.
- Rabbeinu Yonah Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) suggests that it is a compound
  of two words stuck together, without explaining himself; Rabbi Reuven Chaim
  Klein takes it as safe to assume that Ibn Janach means *shen*, tooth, plus
  *hav*, gives, so that *shenhav* would be the beast that "gives" teeth, and
  that on that reading Ibn Janach too seems to mean the animal rather than the
  ivory.
- HaKallir, in an unpublished *piyut* for Yom Kippur, lists *shenhabim* among
  animals of exceptional height, implying that the word means elephant, and
  Rabbi Avraham ben Shlomo, the fourteenth-century Yemenite commentator to
  Melachim, says outright that it means elephants and not ivory.

Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein and others parse the word differently again. They agree
that *shen* is "tooth," but hold that *hav* is related to the Ancient Egyptian
*yev*, elephant, the word behind the name of the island of Elephantine in the
Nile, also known as Yev or Yebu. Rabbi Dr. Klein adds that English *ivory* itself
comes ultimately from Egyptian *yev*, by way of the Latin *ebur*. Dr. Chaim Tawil
points out that *shenhabim* is a semantic cognate of the Akkadian *sinni piri*,
"elephant tusk."

### pil (פיל)
Absent from Tanach. Fairly common from the Mishnah on: Kilayim 8:6, in sorting which
creatures count as a *behemah* and which as a *chayah*, and repeatedly in the
Gemara, in Aramaic as *pila*. Chazal use it for the outer bound of the
impossible, the elephant passing through the eye of a needle, in proving that a
man dreams only of what he has seen or thought of (Berachos 55b); they debate
whether an elephant in a dream is a good sign (Berachos 56b-57a); and one recites
a berachah on seeing one (Berachos 58b). Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein notes that the Mishnaic
word is related to the Persian *pil*, the Arabic *fil*, and the Akkadian
*piru*/*pilu*. The chess piece we call a bishop was originally
the *alfil*, Arabic for "the elephant," shaped like one and likewise moving
diagonally, though it could move only two squares at a time and could jump over
whatever stood between. Where the word comes from within Hebrew is unsettled:

- Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg: Chazal themselves seem to associate *pil* with
  *pele*, wonder (Berachos 56b, 57b), which suits a creature of wondrous size.
- Rabbi Yechiel Michel Stern: *pil* belongs with *nefilim*, giants, since both
  elephants and giants make dread *fall* on whoever sees them (Bereishis Rabbah
  26:7). Rabbi Steinberg adds that Rabbi Yosef Bechor Shor (to Bereishis 6:4 and
  Bamidbar 14:33) likewise ties *nefilim* to *pele*.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own suggestion, which he offers humbly, farfetched
  as it may sound: פ-ל is often bound up with death, in *nefel*, a stillborn
  (Iyov 3:16; Tehillim 58:9), and in *nefilah*, commonly a euphemism for dying
  (Shemos 19:21; Devarim 21:1; Shoftim 3:25, 4:22). Rabbi Menashe ben Israel
  writes that an elephant that kills a man stands by the corpse until it can
  bury its victim; other researchers have noted that elephants bury dead animals
  of all kinds and otherwise seem to take a special interest in death, and a
  2014 *BBC World News* headline reads, "Kenya elephant buries its victims."

The *Oxford English Dictionary*
cites the suggestion that English *elephant* derives ultimately from the Hebrew
*elef*, ox, which might itself be related to *pil*/*pila* by metathesis; and the
Jewish surnames Helfand, Gelfand and Elfant all mean "elephant."

### pildash (פלדש)
The sixth son of Nachor (Bereishis 22:22), whose etymology is somewhat unclear.

- Some linguists read the name as a portmanteau of *pladot*, torches, and
  *aish*, fire, two words that stand side by side in the pasuk (Nachum 2:4);
  *pladot* itself occurs only once in Tanach and seems to be a metathesized form
  of the more familiar *lapidot*, torches (see
  [fire](fire-aish-lehavah-shalheves-lapid.html#%D7%9C%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%93)).
- Another tradition, from Rabbeinu Efrayim ben Shimshon, reads the name as *pil*
  plus *dash*, an elephant that threshes, an allusion to Pildash's superlative
  height, which let him "thresh" over everyone shorter than himself. That is the
  only place *pil* comes near the biblical text, and only by way of a name.

## Rendering guidance

- *Shenhabim* in Shlomo's cargo list: "ivory." If the passage is arguing from
  the *taamim* or from the shape of the list, say in a note that the accents
  group it with the animals and that a minority renders "elephants."
- *Pil* in a Gemara: "elephant," plainly. Do not reach back for "shenhav"; the
  words belong to different strata of the language.
- Trap: English "ivory" and "elephant" split cleanly, but *shenhav* on the
  minority reading is both at once. Where a source turns on that double sense
  (the beast named for what it yields), render "ivory" and gloss, rather than
  choosing "elephant" and losing the material the pasuk is listing.
- Second trap: a derashah on *pil* and *pele*, or on *pil* and *nefilim*, cannot
  survive translation. Keep the Hebrew in the body and put the wordplay in a
  note.
