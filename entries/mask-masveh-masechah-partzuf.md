# Mask: masveh / masechah / partzuf (suto)

## Bottom line

*Masveh* is the Biblical **mask** or **veil**, and it appears only of the
covering Moshe Rabbeinu put over his face. *Masechah* in Tanach is not a mask at
all but a **molten image**, cast metal, as in *egel masechah*; the modern sense
of "mask" is a later development. *Partzuf* is a **face**, a Greek loan-word, and
it is the word the early poskim use for the Purim custom. The trap: reading the
modern *masechah* back into a pasuk turns a golden calf into a disguise.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether *masveh* has any relative in Tanach.** Rabbi Yehuda Chayyuj, Ibn
Janach, Radak, and Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon: *masveh* is the sole derivative of
the root SAMECH-VAV-HEI, standing alone. Rashi suggests instead that the word
comes from Aramaic, finding apparent cognates of it in the Gemara (Kesubos 60a,
62b). Rashbam (to Shemos 34:33), Ibn
Ezra (to Bereishis 49:11 and Shemos 34:33), and Chizkuni: *suto*, Yehudah's
"clothing" in Bereishis 49:11, is its
cognate, so *masveh* means a fabric before it means a mask; Menachem Ibn Saruk
(*Machberes Menachem*) files both under the one-letter root SAMECH. Donash Ibn
Labrat rejects that reading of *suto*, taking it as *kesuso*, "his covering,"
with a missing KAF. The Bottom line follows the *suto* cognates, which make sense
of Ibn Parchon's definition: a *masveh* is cloth first, and a mask by use.

**Where *masechah* as "mask" comes from.** Ibn Janach and Radak: the root is
NUN-SAMECH-KAF, *nesech*, pouring, hence molten metal; the same root also carries
"hiding" and "covering," which would be the bridge to a mask. Alternatively the
root is MEM-SAMECH-KAF, mixing, both of liquids (Yeshayahu 5:22, Mishlei 9:2,
9:5) and of threads into cloth (*masach*, a curtain; *masechet*, Shoftim
16:13-14), which would make *masechah* and *masveh* near neighbours after all.
That root, the column adds, seems to be congruent with the more familiar
MEM-ZAYIN-GIMMEL, "to mix" or "dilute," samech and zayin being interchangeable
and kaf and gimmel likewise. A third line is foreign: Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein and
Avraham Even-Shoshan hold that Modern Hebrew *masechah* in the sense of "mask"
also developed under the pull of phonetically similar words in other languages,
the English *mask*, the French *masque* and the Arabic *maskhara*. Nothing in
translation turns on which is right, since the mask sense is post-Biblical either
way.

## Distinctions

### masveh (מסוה)
Three times in Tanach, all in Shemos 34:29-35, of the covering over Moshe
Rabbeinu's shining face. Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*): a fabric
laid over the face with holes cut for the eyes, nose, and mouth. On the *suto*
reading the literal sense of *masveh* is "clothing" or "fabric"; the word *sutot*
in that sense appears in the Mishnah (Keilim 16:7), and the Rambam, in his
commentary to the Mishnah there, connects it to the Biblical *suto*. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim ties *masveh* and *suto* to *stav*, autumn: soft, spongy fabrics worn
against the cold, or cloths laid over one's clothes to keep them from dirt, as
one does in that season. Rabbi Ephraim Lenchitz uses *masveh* for the Ashkenazi
Purim custom, and Rabbi Zerach Eidlitz uses *partzuf* and *masveh* both.

### masechah (מסכה)
In Tanach a cast-metal figure: *egel masechah*, the molten calf (Shemos 32:4,
32:8; Devarim 9:16; Nechemiah 9:18). Only in Modern Hebrew does it mean a mask.
The related *masechet* has its own life: in Mishnaic Hebrew the woof threads
running across a weave (Rabbeinu Chananel to Shabbos 59b; Rambam to Keilim 21:1;
the Rash to Negaim 11:9), and thence a tractate. *Sefer Chassidim* 929: a
*masechta* is the woven fabric of the rulings on one topic. *Tosefos Yom Tov*
(introduction): from *maschah yeinah*, "she mixed her wine" (Mishlei 9:2), a
tractate being a mixture of laws. Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia suggests SAMECH-KAF, to
listen (*haskeis*, Devarim 27:9), since listening is the core mechanism by which
Torah she'be'al peh, typified by the Mishnah, is transmitted.

### partzuf (פרצוף)
Once in the Mishnah (Yevamos 16:3) and often in Gemara and Midrash, with the
Aramaic *partzufa*; a Greek loan-word, *prosopon*, literally "facing the eyes,"
*pros* being "toward" and *ops* "eye" (as in optometry). The column adds from the
Oxford English Dictionary that the Latin *persona*, a mask worn by an actor, also
derives from *prosopon*, and is the etymological ancestor of the English word
*person*. The earliest poskim to record the Purim custom call it wearing
*partzufim*, "faces" (*Mahari Mintz* 17; Rema to Orach Chaim 696:8; Rabbi Yuzpa
Shamash on the old traditions of the community of Worms), so the custom was
literally to wear faces on one's face.

The column closes with why. The Gemara (Megillah 12a) says the Jews of
Achashverosh's time were liable to destruction for having bowed to
Nevuchadnetzar's idol, but since they had not really forsaken Hashem and bowed
only out of fear, they were not truly deserving of it: as their idolatry was
superficial only, so their ordeal was one that looked like destruction on the
surface and did not end in it. Elsewhere (Chullin 139b) the Gemara reads the
Purim story as Hashem hiding "His face" (Devarim 31:18), as though wearing a mask
over His true plans. On that basis Rabbi Moshe Chagiz writes that the Purim
custom of wearing *partzufim* mimics how Hashem dealt with Klal Yisrael then, a
facade of intending destruction with no such intention behind it.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "mask" will absorb *masveh*, *partzuf*, and modern *masechah*.
  In a pasuk, *masveh* is "mask" or "veil" and *masechah* is "molten image";
  never let the second take the first's English.
- Shemos 34:33-35: "veil" reads better than "mask" for Moshe Rabbeinu, since the
  point is shielding others from the light, not disguise.
- In a teshuvah or minhagim sefer on Purim, render *partzufim* "masks," with the
  literal "faces" in a gloss where the wordplay matters.
- *Masechet* in a Mishnaic context is "woof threads"; in a bibliographic one it
  is "tractate." Do not carry the weaving sense into a citation.
