# Bone: etzem / gerem

## Bottom line

Both mean **bone**, and both also mean **the thing itself**. *Etzem* is the bone
as something hard, strong, and self-contained, and for Rabbi Shlomo Aharon
Wertheimer the general word;
*gerem* is the bone as one part within a frame, which is why the same root gives
a single step of a staircase and, in rabbinic Hebrew, *grama*, indirect
**causation** (Rav Hirsch). The Vilna Gaon puts the line differently: *etzem* is
the bone's hard outside, *gerem* the hollow inside where the marrow is.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| עצם | etzem | bone, itself | hard, durable, closed-up (Rav Hirsch); the general term for any bone (Wertheimer); the outer surface (Vilna Gaon) |
| גרם | gerem | bone, itself | one member of a larger system (Rav Hirsch); the big load-bearing bones (Wertheimer); the inner cavity with its marrow (Vilna Gaon) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**How etzem and gerem differ.** Three accounts:

1. **Whole against part.** Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 49:14, Shemos 1:7, Bamidbar
   24:8, Devarim 25:4, Tehillim 139:15): the two words can name the same body
   part, but *etzem* attends to its strength and durability, which he groups with
   *osem* (storing up), *chosem* (blocked), and *otzem* (shutting the eyes),
   since something strong is closed and self-contained; *gerem* attends to the
   bone as one piece of a bigger structure. *Gerem hamaalos*, a single step
   of a staircase (Melachim II 9:13, and see *Metzudas Tzion* there), illustrates
   the point; hence, for Rav Hirsch, *grama* for causation as one link in a causal chain, and hence the verb of
   ג-ר-מ for breaking, that is, pulling the parts of a system apart.
2. **General against large.** Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: *etzem* is the
   general word for bone of every kind. A fetus is at first fleshy tissue, and as
   it develops parts of that tissue close up and harden into bone (Iyov 10:11;
   *Chullin* 89b). *Gerem* names specifically the larger bones, the ones that
   carry a heavy load, which is what Yaakov invoked for Yissachar.
3. **Outside against inside.** The Vilna Gaon, commenting on "a broken spirit
   dries the *gerem*" (Mishlei 17:22): *etzem* is the hard outer surface of the
   bone, *gerem* the cavity within, where the marrow lies. Rabbi Klein adds that
   the tie of *gerem* to the liquid marrow may find precedent in Tanach if one
   juxtaposes that pasuk with "a good report fattens the *etzem*" (Mishlei 15:30);
   the juxtaposition is his, the distinction the Gaon's.

Rav Hirsch's reading explains the rabbinic *grama*, and the Vilna Gaon's
bears on his ruling on *germani* below.

**Chamor garem (Bereishis 49:14).** Yaakov's berachos compare several sons to
animals, Yehudah to a lion cub (49:9), Dan to a snake (49:17), Naftali to a
gazelle (49:21), Binyamin to a wolf (49:27). What, then, did he call Yissachar?

1. **A strong-boned donkey.** Rashi: *garem* is *etzem*, so the blessing is a
   strong donkey able to bear heavy burdens, fitting a tribe of talmidei
   chachamim who take on the yoke of Torah (see also Rabbeinu Bachaya there).
   Rabbi Yaakov Emden reaches the same result from causation: the bones are the
   frame on which the body stands, so they cause the animal's stoutness.
2. **A bony donkey.** Ralbag, exactly the reverse: a donkey so poorly fed that
   its bones show, and therefore too weak to go out, like the scholars of
   Yissachar who stay in their place, against Zevulun's merchants who cross the
   sea.
3. **A donkey caused it.** The Gemara (*Niddah* 31a) reads *garem* as "caused":
   Yissachar was born because Yaakov's donkey brayed as he came in from the field
   and Leah went out to meet him (Bereishis 30:14-18; see *Sefer HeAruch* s.v.
   *chamra*). The Tosafists offer another route to the same reading: mandrakes
   were said, by a long-standing legend, to shriek lethally when uprooted, so
   they were commonly harvested by starving an old donkey and clearing the area of people,
   leaving the animal to pull them up and die of the shriek; apparently the
   *dudaim* in the trade between Rachel and Leah were got that
   way, so a donkey "caused" Yissachar to be born.

**Yigarem (Bamidbar 24:8).** Bilam's blessing that Israel will consume their
enemies "and their bones *yigarem*."

- Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*): ג-ר-מ has two senses, bone and
  breaking, and here it means they will break them; Rashi cites him.
- Rashi himself prefers gnawing: scraping (*gorer*) the meat off the bone with
  the teeth and sucking out the marrow, leaving the bone bare (see also Rashi to
  Yechezkel 23:34).

**Germani (Negaim 2:1).** The Mishnah says the whitest shade of *tzaraas* looks
dim on the skin of a *germani*.

- Rambam (commentary to the Mishnah) and Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi (*HaMadrich
  HaMaspik*) derive the word from *gerem*, since bones are white, so a *germani*
  is a man of especially white skin.
- The Vilna Gaon (*Shenos Eliyahu* there) does not accept this and takes
  *germani* as a member of the European tribes of the lands the Romans called
  Germanica.

Rabbi Klein suggests it is likely the Gaon's own distinction that drove him: if
*gerem* is the bone's interior, tying *germani* to it becomes problematic, since
that is not the part whose whiteness one sees.

## Distinctions

### etzem (עצם)
Yosef charged his brothers to carry his *atzamos* out of Mitzrayim (Bereishis
50:25), and Moshe took them (Shemos 13:19). The same root gives the reflexive
pronouns *atzmi*, *atzmo*, *atzmecha*, literally "my bones," for "myself." Hebrew
has several such words for the self, each naming a different part: *nafshi* from
the soul, *gufi* from the body, *galmi* from one's matter, *atzmi* from the
bones. Menachem Ibn Saruk lists five senses of ע-צ-מ: bone; a visible hue
(Shemos 24:10; Eichah 4:7; Tehillim 139:15); strength (Bereishis 26:16; Shemos
1:7, 1:9; Yeshayahu 40:29, 41:21; Tehillim 38:20); shutting the eye (Yeshayahu
29:10, 33:15); and the thing itself (Bereishis 7:13; Shemos 12:41). Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim joins *etzem* to *atzum*, strong, and to *eitz*, a tree, since the
bone is the hard part of the body as the trunk is of the tree, against the
flimsier growths; Ramban (to Vayikra 23:28) had already said that a thing's
*etzem* is its strongest point.

### gerem (גרם)
The Targumim typically render *etzem* with *garmei*, in the sense of bone and in the sense
of self alike; the Aramaic appears in Daniel 6:25. In Hebrew the noun is written
*gerem* but usually pronounced *garem* under the influence of the cantillation and
vocalization (Mishlei 17:22, 25:15). "A soft tongue breaks a bone (*gerem*)"
(Mishlei 25:15), said of the power of prayer to overturn harsh decrees from
Above, is plainly the body part. The self-sense may
already be seen in *gerem hamaalos*, the step itself (Melachim II
9:13); it would seem, Rabbi Klein writes, that *gerem* as bone gives *gerem* as
self the same way *etzem* does, a man's self being sometimes characterized by the
hardest parts of his body. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur notes the rabbinic
extension to causation, *gorem*, *grama*, *garmi*, seen already where Targum
renders *sabosi*, I brought about, as *garamti* (Shmuel I 22:22), and running
through the Gemara and the poskim. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives *gerem* from
the two-letter ג-ר, temporary lodging, the bone being where the marrow and the
body's moisture are housed, alongside *ger* (a sojourner), *goren* (the grain's
temporary home), *nigar* (gathered water), and *garger* (a grape holding its
juice); Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche argues the same from the marrow.

Two further terms belong here. *Garmei shamayim*, the celestial bodies, in
Rambam's *Moreh Nevuchim* and other works rendered from Judeo-Arabic, are
independent "selves" in the heavens. And *hagramah* (*Chullin* 9a), cutting at
the wrong point of the animal's throat so that the *shechitah* is invalid, may
belong to *gerem* as well (see Rashi to Melachim II 9:13 and *Maggid Mishnah*,
Hilchos *Shechitah* 3:12).

## Rendering guidance

- Both words are "bone." Where a passage sets them beside each other, or a
  commentary turns on which one the pasuk used, mark the difference: *etzem* as
  the bone in its strength, *gerem* as the bone within the frame, or the Vilna
  Gaon's outside against inside.
- Trap: "bone" and "self" are the same Hebrew word, so a phrase like *b'etzem
  hayom hazeh* is "on that very day," not anything skeletal. Do not carry the
  bone into it, and do not lose it either when a derashah plays on both.
- *Grama* is a term of art in nezikin; leave it transliterated and gloss it once
  as indirect causation. Translating it "cause" flattens the whole distinction
  between *grama* and direct damage.
- *Chamor garem*: Rashi's "strong-boned donkey" is the standard rendering, but a
  translation that follows Ralbag says the opposite, so name whose reading is
  being used rather than writing a compromise.
- *Germani* stays transliterated. "German" commits to the Vilna Gaon and
  "white-skinned" to Rambam, and the Mishnah's point survives either way only if
  the word is left standing.
