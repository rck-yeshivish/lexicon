# Wall: chomah / kir / shur / cheil / chayitz / kotel

## Bottom line

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's own summary: *chomah* is the **city wall** (or a lookout post),
*kir* the generic **structural wall**, *cheil* a **short wall**, *chayitz* a
**flimsy partition** and *mechitzah* any partition (Rabbi Moshe Zacuto,
where Pappenheim makes *chayitz* synonymous with *mechitzah*), *shur*
the Aramaic for *chomah*, *kotel* the Aramaic for *kir*. Where the *cheil*
stands is disputed: outside a taller wall, with the empty space between them
(Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim), or a shorter wall inside the *chomah* (Rashi); Radak
seems unsure which, and suggests it may be a moat. Rabbi Shlomo of Urbino, as
is his wont, holds the six mean exactly the same thing; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein sees more to it than that, and Pappenheim's two-letter roots help bring out
the nuances.

## Distinctions

### chomah (חומה)
Over 130 appearances, always a wall surrounding a city or a large and important
building.
- Rabbi Pappenheim, like Menachem Ibn Saruk, traces it to the two-letter root
  ח-מ, which he explains as primarily heat: from it *milchamah*, the culmination
  of a heated clash of parties, and so, he suggests, perhaps *chomah*, whose
  main purpose is to protect a city from warfare; or else because the wall might
  block cool winds and keep the city warm.
- The Raavan (Rabbi Eliezer ben Nosson, 1090 to 1170) notes that *chomah* is
  also related to sight, as the Aramaic ח-מ-ה is seeing (see Targum Yonasan to
  Shemos 14:31), and Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 49:22) makes a similar point.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (*HaKsav VeHaKabbalah*) connects it to
  *cheimah*, anger, and *chamah*, sun, on the point that a wall sets a city
  apart from all beyond it; Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 21:15) puts *cheimes*, a
  flask, in the same family, since it encloses and protects what is inside it.

### kir (קיר)
About 74 appearances as "wall." Most grammarians take the root as ק-י-ר; some
drop the yud. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*) links *kir*
to *kor*/*kar*, cold, a wall that gives shade to cool off under. Rabbi
Pappenheim works from the two-letter ק-ר, the strong impact of weight or
velocity: *korah*, a beam, and *tikrah*, a ceiling, whose horizontal timbers
press down on the supports beneath. On that reading *kir* is originally the
upright beam that bears a structure's weight, and only then any wall of stone
or metal. The root also destroys: Bilaam foretells that Yisrael will *karkar*
the nations (Bamidbar 24:17), which most commentators, Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Ibn Janach among them, render
"destroy" without explaining the etymology. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) derives
*karkar* from *kir*, to destroy a wall and so strip a nation of its protection,
comparing *shoresh*, root, whose verb *misharesh* means to uproot; thus, Rabbi Reuven
Chaim Klein concludes, *kir* can refer both to building a wall and to tearing one
down. Rav Hirsch (to Vayikra 19:28,
Bamidbar 24:17) and Rabbi Yitzchok of Volozhin's *Peh Kadosh* (to Bamidbar
24:17) offer comparable explanations.

### shur (שור)
Rare in Hebrew (Bereishis 49:22; Shmuel II 22:30; Tehillim 18:30; possibly
Yirmiyahu 5:10 and Iyov 24:11), but common in the Targumim as *shura*,
rendering *chomah* (Vayikra 25:29; Yehoshua 2:15, 6:20; Eichah 2:8). Rabbi
Pappenheim traces it to ש-ר, a focal point: *shar*/*sharir*, the umbilical
cord joining a fetus to its mother (Yechezkel 16:4; Mishlei 3:8; Iyov 40:16);
*shor*, an ox, whose strength sits in its midsection; *shoresh*, the focal
point of a plant's growth; *yashar*, the shortest way to a point; *shir*, a
composition holding to one theme. *Shur* in the sense of seeing belongs here
too, since sight, unlike the other senses, can be aimed at one point (Bamidbar 23:9, 24:17; Iyov 35:5). With it go *teshurah*, a gift
for one who greets ("sees") a dignitary; *nesher*, the eagle that sees far; and
*sheirus*, service, overseeing a master's needs (or, alternatively, straightening
out the household or staying straightforward with the master's wishes). For
*shur* as "wall" he offers two links: a wall built in a straight line (*yashar*), as against a
*chomah* that encircles; or a tall wall used as a lookout, from *shur* as
seeing. In line with the latter, Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein notes that *shur* and
*chomah* both carry the double sense of wall and seeing. Rashi to Bereishis
49:22 seems unsure which sense *shur* has there, and Radak reads *shor* at
Bereishis 49:6 as "wall," like the *shur* later in the perek.

### cheil (חיל)
- Rabbi Pappenheim derives it from ח-ל, circular movement and the empty space
  inside a circle: the *cheil* is a low wall running outside a taller one, and
  the empty ground between them is a kind of no man's land. He adduces the
  Mishnah (Middos 2:3), which calls the space between the walls of the Har
  HaBayis and the Temple building the *cheil*.
- Pesachim 86a describes *chomah* and *cheil* as *shura* and *bar-shura*, a wall
  and the son of a wall; Rashi takes the *chomah* as the outer wall and the
  *cheil* as a shorter wall within it, the small son beside his large father.
- Radak seems unsure whether the *cheil* stands outside the *chomah* or inside,
  and then suggests that perhaps it is not a wall at all but a moat dug outside
  the *chomah*.

### chayitz (חיץ)
A single appearance in Tanach, Yechezkel 13:10.
- Ibn Janach: the yud stands in for a second tzadi, so the root is ח-צ-צ, to
  partition.
- Rabbi Pappenheim likewise puts it under ח-צ, dividing in two, with *chatzi*,
  *chatzos*, *chutz* and *cheitz*, and theorizes that *chayitz* is a wall
  dividing one domain into two, equivalent to the Mishnaic *mechitzah* (Bava
  Basra 1:1).
- Rabbi Moshe Zacuto (1625 to 1697, *Kol HaRamaz* to Sheviis 3:8) narrows it:
  *chayitz* is a flimsy partition, *mechitzah* any partition at all.

### kotel (כותל)
Three appearances: once in Hebrew (Shir HaShirim 2:9, *omed achar kosleinu*)
and twice in the Aramaic *ktal* (Daniel 5:5; Ezra 5:8). It appears more commonly
in the Targumim, rendering *kir* (Vayikra 1:15, 14:37; Yehoshua 2:15, and more).
Rabbi Ernest Klein connects *kotel* with the Akkadian *kutallu*, "backside."

## Rendering guidance

- *Chomah*: **city wall**, or **rampart** where the fortification is the point.
  *Chomos Yerushalayim* are the walls of the city, not the walls of a building.
  (Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein himself writes "the wall of the rampart (the *Cheil*).")
- *Kir*: **wall**, plain, of a house or of the mizbeach.
- *Cheil*: **the Cheil**, transliterated, in Mikdash contexts, with a gloss the
  first time (a shorter wall and the space it marks off; outside or inside the
  *chomah* according to the view followed). "Rampart" alone will not carry it.
- *Chayitz*: **partition**; *mechitzah*: **mechitzah** or **divider**. Where
  Rabbi Moshe Zacuto's distinction is live, *chayitz* is a **flimsy
  partition**.
- *Kotel*: **the Kosel** for the Western Wall; otherwise plain **wall**.
- Trap: English "wall" absorbs all six. Pesachim 86a's *shura uvar-shura* pairs
  two of them in three words, so render "a wall and a wall's son," keeping the
  Gemara's image, and gloss which is which.
- Second trap: *shur* and *chomah* both carry "wall" and "see" (Rabbi Ernest Klein), so a
  pasuk using either may not be about masonry at all; Rashi seems unsure at
  Bereishis 49:22.
