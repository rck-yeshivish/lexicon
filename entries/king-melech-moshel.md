# King: melech / moshel

## Bottom line

Both words name the same position of power; they differ in how the man got
there. A *melech* is **king** by the people's willing acceptance. A *moshel* is
a **ruler** who holds them by force or by proven superiority, accepted or not.
Where Yosef's brothers set them side by side (Bereishis 37:8), the second word
is not mere repetition.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מלך | melech | king | king by the people's willing acceptance; their equal without it (Vilna Gaon) |
| מושל | moshel | ruler | rules by force or proven ability, accepted or not (Vilna Gaon); some say a deputy, as Bereishis 24:2 and 45:8 fit (Rabbi Klein) |

## Distinctions

### melech (מלך)
Root מ-ל-ך. The commentators explain that this is one whose ascent to the throne
is commissioned directly by the people: they willingly elect to anoint him, and
that acceptance is what makes him king. Ibn Ezra, on *ki laHashem hameluchah umoshel bagoyim* (Tehillim 22:29), explains that both words refer to sovereignty, but *meluchah* denotes a popular sovereign whose dominion his constituents willingly accepted, while a *moshel* is a dictator who continues to rule whether or not his people object to him. The Vilna Gaon
sharpens this: the *melech* rises from within the camp of the masses and holds
no inherent advantage over any of them; strip away their recognition and he is
their equal. Rabbi Moshe Soloveitchik of Switzerland reads the Midrash that
likens a *chasan* to a *melech* (Pirkei d'Rabbi Eliezer ch. 16) on this basis:
as even the most calculating king must keep conceding to his people so that
they go on accepting him lovingly, so must a *chasan* act with patience and
reliability so that his wife goes on wanting to remain his partner.

Rabbi Klein uses this also to answer a question on the Gemara. Rosh
Hashanah 16a has Hashem say, "Recite before Me verses of kingship so that you
make Me *melech* over you," and the Gemara takes the recital not merely as our
affirming His kingship but as making Him King; why should that be contingent on
our acceptance at all? On the above the answer is clear: by accepting His
sovereignty willingly we consolidate His role as *melech* of the world rather
than merely a dictatorial *moshel*.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Bereishis 14:18 and Shemos 4:10, p.
67 in Zini's edition): *melech* may come from Aramaic *mamlach*, counsellor, as
*nimlach* in the Gemara, and he cites the commentary ascribed to the Rashbam (to
Tehillim 2:2) saying the same, a work in fact written by Isaac Satanow. He also
considers that *melech* is related to *malach*, since a king is the people's
agent.

### moshel (מושל)
Root מ-ש-ל. One who takes the position rather than receiving it. The Vilna
Gaon: the *moshel* leads by his abilities, not merely by the people's will; he proves
himself in battle and the like, shows himself more capable than the rest, and
on that strength seizes his constituency and forces them under his hand. This
is why the people offered Gideon the post of *moshel* (Shoftim 8:22) and not of
*melech*: even though the masses offered it willingly, he would still have been
called a *moshel*, because they offered it only on the strength of his
acknowledged military prowess.

Some explain instead that *melech* is the king at the top and *moshel* a
governor or deputy to whom he has delegated power. The consensus
holds the two equal in position and different only in origin.

Rabbi Klein observes that *hamoshel bechol asher lo* (Bereishis 24:2), of
Avraham's servant, fits the view that a *moshel* is one delegated by a greater
authority, as does Yosef calling himself *moshel* in Egypt (45:8, 45:26) and the
sun and moon being given *memshalah* over day and night (1:16, 1:18).

Avi Hurvitz (pp. 229-230): *shalit* is an Aramaic-influenced form standing for
Hebrew *radah* and *mashal*.

Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to Shemos 4:10, p. 66 in Zini's
edition): *moshel* is related to *mashal*, since the first leaders were those
who could address and rebuke the people clearly by means of parables. Other
words of speech are likewise tied to leadership: *he'emarta*, *yisamru kol
poalei aven*, *yadber amim tachteinu*. On p. 68 he writes that *davar* is a
title of rule in Avestan Persian and suggests it may be related to Hebrew
*davar*.

## Rendering guidance

- Bereishis 37:8, *hamaloch timloch aleinu im mashol timshol banu*: not a
  rhetorical doubling. "Will you be **king** over us? Will you **rule** us?"
  The brothers are asking two things, whether he expects their consent and
  whether he means to take it without.
- Tehillim 22:29, *ki laHashem hameluchah umoshel bagoyim*: "for the kingship
  is Hashem's, and He **rules** over the nations." The Vilna Gaon takes note of
  an apparent contradiction between this and the other pasuk at the close of
  Aleinu: here the shift implies two roles, *melech* to Yisrael, who accept His
  sovereignty, and *moshel* to the nations, who do not, while Zecharyah 14:9,
  *vehayah Hashem lemelech al kol ha'aretz*, has Him *melech* over the whole
  world. The answer must be that in time to come, when all truth is revealed,
  everyone will recognize and accept, so that He will be *melech* universally
  and not *moshel*.
- Rosh Hashanah 16a on Malchiyos works only on this distinction (see above).
  Render *melech* there as **king** and let the commentary carry the point that
  a *melech* is made by acceptance.
- Trap: "sovereign," "monarch," and "ruler" all read as free variants of "king"
  in English. Fix *melech* to king and *moshel* to ruler across a passage and
  keep them fixed; if a third word for power is present, recast rather than
  borrow one of these two.
- Trap: *moshel* in English easily picks up "dictator" or "tyrant." The word
  says how he came to power, not that he governs badly; use "ruler" and let the
  context supply the harshness.
