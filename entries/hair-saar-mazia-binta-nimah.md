# Hair: saar / mazia / binta / nimah

## Bottom line

*Saar* (Aramaic *saara*) is the plain word for hair, whether one strand or the
whole growth: **hair**. Rabbi Klein found no earlier theory of how the Aramaic
words divide the field, and offers his own suggestion: *mazia* is a patch of
hair, **hair** or **a head of hair**; *binta* is one single strand, **a hair**;
*nimah* is a hair that protrudes or is unwanted, and outside the hair sense it
is a **string** or **thread**. *Binta* and *mazia* stand side by side in Yoma
69b and Bava Metzia 84b, where a single *binta* comes loose from the *mazia*.

## Distinctions

### saar / saarah (שער / שערה)
The Hebrew word, used both for a single strand (Vayikra 13:37) and for hair
collectively (Vayikra 14:9). Some *medakdekim* separate the two forms, *se'ar*
for a patch of hair and *se'arah* for one strand (see Tosafos Yom Tov to Negaim
4:1 and Niddah 3:2). Rabbi Dr. Jared Greenblatt theorizes that Hebrew and
Aramaic *saar(a)* appear to be cognate with the words for barley, *se'orah*,
barley being possibly called "hairy" because its awns are longer than wheat's.
Indeed, the Mishnah uses *se'ar* for the fibers protruding from produce (Shabbos
21:3, Kilayim 3:5). Greenblatt adds that both sets of words may be related to
*sa'ir*, a goat, as *ma'azia* is. Apart from the three places where the Targum
uses *binta* (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur, *Meturgeman*, counts only those three),
the Targumim do not really translate *sa'ar* at all, but simply Aramaicize it
into *saara*. A reader pointed out to Rabbi Klein that Biblical Aramaic itself
has *se'ar* three times (Daniel 3:27, 4:30, 7:9), which suggests a native
Aramaic word, and that the standard Arabic word for hair is the same, with a
shin for the sin.

### mazia (מזיא)
Its etymology is disputed.

- Rabbi Dr. Jared Greenblatt argues that it is related to the Syriac and Aramaic
  *ma'azia*, goat hair (Targum Onkelos to Shemos 25:4, 26:7, 35:6), plainly a
  cognate of *eiz*, and that in later permutations the *zayin* doubled, giving
  *mazia*, as if the *ayin* had dropped.
- Rabbi Dr. Alexander Kohut contends the opposite, that *mazia* is unrelated to
  *ma'azia* and derives instead from Old Persian, noting its relation to the Old
  Persian word for the hair of the eyebrows.

Either way, Rashi (to Devarim 32:24) identifies the *mezei* of *mezei ra'av* as
the one appearance of this Aramaic word in Tanach; others, including Ibn Janach,
Ibn Parchon, Ibn Ezra and Radak, explain *mezei* differently.

*Mazia* is used of Mordechai asking Haman to cut his hair (Megillah 16a), of
Rabbi Akiva picking the straw out of Rachel's hair (Nedarim 50a), of Rebbi
letting his hair grow as *teshuvah* (Sanhedrin 25a), of the shaving of the
*yefas to'ar* (Targum Yonasan to Devarim 21:12), and of the wife of On ben Pelet
uncovering her hair to keep Korach's men away (Sanhedrin 109b to 110a; On is
listed with Korach's entourage at Bamidbar 16:1 and then drops out of the
story). It is also the word in Korach's wife's taunt that shaving all the
Levi'im (Bamidbar 8:7) was meant to humiliate him (Sanhedrin 110a).

### binta (בינתא)
One strand. Death by *misas neshikah* is "like drawing a hair (*binta*) out of
milk" (Berachos 8a). The Targum to Shoftim 20:16, where the Binyaminite slingers
could hit a hair without missing, renders the Hebrew *sa'arah* as *binas saara*,
one strand of the hair; cognates also appear at Tehillim 40:13 and Iyov 9:17,
and per Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur those three are the only places *binta* occurs in
Targum. Kohut explains the medicinal leech called a *bini* of the water (Gittin
68b) as related to *binta*, such leeches being long and thin like strands of
hair, and notes that elsewhere the same leech is called a *nimah* of the waters
(Avodah Zarah 12b).

The two words appear side by side twice. When the *chachamim*
captured the fiery lion of the *yetzer* for *avodah zarah*, a single strand
(*binta*) slipped from its hair (*mazia*), a sign that the fight is not finished
(Yoma 69b); and the body of Rabbi Elazar beRabbi Shimon, preserved in the attic,
would bleed if one strand (*binta*) came loose from his hair (*mazia*) (Bava
Metzia 84b). Rabbi Avraham Meir Israel (*Yalkut HaMeiri*) hears a hint of the same word in
the pasuk Chazal applied to the wife of On ben Pelet, "*chachmos nashim bansah
beisah*" (Mishlei 14:1): *bansah*, she built, sounds like *binta*, hair.

Where the Gemara speaks of a nazir uprooting some of his hair it says *mazia*
(*Nazir* 39a), but Rabbi Yoel Sirkes deletes the word and, following the
commentary ascribed to Rashi there, reads *binta*.

### nimah (נימא)
The ordinary Talmudic word for a hair: no one can touch what is set aside for
another, not even by a hair's worth (Yoma 38b); the hair of the body as a
*chatzitzah* in *tevilah* (Eruvin 4b, Sukkah 6a); pubic hair (Sanhedrin 21a,
Gittin 6b). But it seems the hair sense is only secondary and borrowed. Elsewhere *nimah*
is a string or cord: the strings of an instrument (Targum to Tehillim 6:1,
Tosefta Erchin 2:7, Eruvin 102b), a bowstring (Targum to Tehillim 11:2 and Iyov
30:11), threads for sewing (Shabbos 64a, 74b), threads sticking out of a garment
(Menachos 42b, Sukkah 9a), and by extension a kernel (Shabbos 79a). Several
philologists and linguists derive the Aramaic *nimah* from the Ancient Greek
*nema*, thread.

### The proposal that ties them together
Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein has found no theory of why Talmudic Aramaic would have
four words that all mean hair. His own suggestion: *mazia* is a patch of hair, *binta* is a
single strand, *nimah* is hair that sticks out or is otherwise unwanted, and
*saara* is simply the Hebrew word taken over into Aramaic in its general sense.

## Rendering guidance

- Yoma 69b and Bava Metzia 84b: render the pair as "a hair slipped out of his
  hair" only if you are willing to lose the point. Better: "one strand came away
  from his hair." English "hair" would otherwise absorb both words in one
  sentence.
- *Binta* on its own: "a hair" or "a single strand," never "hair" as a mass
  noun. Berachos 8a reads "like drawing a hair out of milk."
- *Nimah* in a *tevilah* or *tzitzis* context: keep the string sense in view;
  "a loose thread" for a garment, "a hair" for the body, and in a passage that
  plays on both (a bowstring beside a hair), say so rather than choosing one.
- *Sa'ar* in a *nega'im* or *taharah* context: follow the Tosafos Yom Tov and
  translate *se'arah* as "a hair," *se'ar* as "the hair," where the halachah
  turns on one strand against a patch (Negaim 4:1, Niddah 3:2).
- Trap: Korach's name means bald, and the two sources on his grievance choose
  different Aramaic. The *Zohar* (Tazria 49a) has him resenting being left
  shaven of *saara* while Aharon was decked in clothes befitting a king; the
  Gemara (Sanhedrin 110a) has his wife telling him the shaving of the Levi'im
  (Bamidbar 8:7) was to humiliate him, using *mazia*. If a piece quotes both, do
  not silently harmonize them into one English word without noting the
  difference.
