# Slipping off: shemittah / chalitzah / shalaf / shal

## Bottom line

*Shemittah* is **releasing**: loosening one's grip so that a thing slips out of
one's control, which may be why the same word covers letting a field lie and letting
a debt go. *Chalitzah* is **extracting** something from what encloses it.
*Shalaf* is **drawing** a thing quickly out of a loose holder, most often a sword
from its sheath. *Shal* is a shoe **slipped off** with no hands at all. Shoresh Yesha draws a three-way
distinction among the shoe-removal verbs.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| שמיטה | shemittah | releasing, letting slip | letting go so that it falls (Ibn Janach; Radak to Melachim II 9:33); it seems, forsaking and the falling that follows |
| חליצה | chalitzah | extracting | drawing out of what envelops it (Pappenheim); the shoe that must be untied first (Shoresh Yesha) |
| שלף | shalaf | drawing, slipping out | quick, from a loose holder: sword from sheath, shoe for a transaction (Pappenheim) |
| של | shal | slipping off | a sandal off the foot without using the hands (Shoresh Yesha) |
| אלץ | alatz | pressing, urging | Delilah on Shimshon; through *chalatz* to *lachatz* (Ramban) or directly from *chalatz* (Sefer HaShoham, Ben Yehoyada) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**How the three shoe verbs differ.**

- Shoresh Yesha: Tanach has three verbs for taking off a shoe, and they answer
  to three ways a shoe can be fastened. *Shal* (Shemos 3:5, Yehoshua 5:15) is
  slipping a sandal off without even using the hands; *shalaf* is taking a shoe
  off by hand; *chalitzah* is removing a shoe fastened to the foot, which must
  first be untied.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740-1814) runs in a similar vein: *chalitzah* is
  extraction from something that engulfs, while *shalaf* is used wherever the
  two items were only loosely attached and could easily be separated, which is
  why it fits both the sword a warrior needs readily at his disposal and the
  shoe handed over to close a deal before the other party backs out; since both
  must come away swiftly, we would not expect either to be fastened too
  strongly.

Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein raises a difficulty of his own here and leaves it open.
He finds it perplexing that the Gemara names a load mounted on an animal a *shlif*
(*Bava Kamma* 3a; see Rashi to *Bava Kamma* 17b, *Shabbos* 154b, *Eruvin* 16a,
*Kiddushin* 22b), a word seemingly derived from ש-ל-ף, and says outright that he
does not understand why a word for a parcel one hopes will *not* slip off the
animal's back should derive from the act of slipping off. He offers two possibilities: perhaps *shlif*
names precisely that hope, or perhaps *shalaf* in fact denotes the same untying
that *chalitzah* denotes, against *Shoresh Yesha*, so that *shlif* is what one
does to the package once it reaches its destination (Rashi to *Bava Basra* 75b
notes the *shlif* was *tied* to the animal).

**Are chalitzah and shalaf the same act?**

- Targum Yonasan renders the verb of the *chalitzah* ceremony (Devarim 25:9)
  with a cognate of *shalaf*, and Rashi (to *Chullin* 53a, *Gittin* 33b) links
  the two verbs as though they were synonyms.
- Pappenheim, as above, keeps them apart.
- Radak (to Yeshayahu 58:11 and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) seems to have had an
  alternate version of the Midrash below that used *shalaf* where ours uses a
  cognate of *shemittah*.

**What alatz means.** Delilah "pressed" (*alatz*) Shimshon to give up the secret
of his super-human strength (Shoftim 16:16).

- Ramban (to Bereishis 32:25) reads the aleph of *alatz* as interchangeable with
  the ches of *chalatz*, which he understands as a metathesized permutation of
  *lachatz*, pressure.
- Chazal (*Sotah* 9b, *Bereishis Rabbah* 52:12) explain *alatz* differently, and
  concretely, as Delilah slipping away (*nishmatah*) from under Shimshon in the
  throes of intimacy as a way of pressing him. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein infers
  from that that perhaps Chazal took *alatz* as directly related to *chalatz*,
  slipping, rather than to *lachatz*, and reports finding that contention stated
  explicitly by Rabbi Moshe ben HaNessiah Britannico (13th-century England) in
  *Sefer HaShoham* and by Rabbi Yosef Chaim of Baghdad (1832-1909) in *Ben
  Yehoyada* to *Sotah* 9b.

## Distinctions

### shemittah (שמיטה)
Root ש-מ-ט. Devarim names the Sabbatical year *Shemittah* five times (15:1-2,
15:9, 31:10), and the same root supplies its verbs in both halves of the mitzvah:
the land, "and in the seventh you shall release it (*tishmitenah*) and abandon
it" (Shemos 23:10-11), which Rashi takes as the ban on working it, and the loans
a lender lets go (*shamot*, *tashmet*, Devarim 15:2-3). The literal sense comes
out of the narrative uses. When the oxen *shamat* the Aron and Uzza put out his
hand (Shmuel II 6:6, Divrei HaYamim I 13:9), Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) explains that the weight was bursting the animals' joints and the
Aron would have slipped off had they collapsed. When Yehu had Izevel thrown from
the window (Melachim II 9:33), the verb is a cognate, and Radak there with Ibn
Ezra (to Devarim 15:1) gloss it as letting something go so that it falls. So, it
seems, the root denotes leaving a thing to itself and the falling that results,
which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein suggests is what a man does to his field and to his
debts in the seventh year.

Two further uses seem to be extensions rather than forsaking. The woodchopper whose hand
slips, killing a man, so that he flees to a city of refuge (Devarim 19:5): Rashi
uses a cognate, following the Mishnah (*Makkos* 2:1), and himself links it (to
Shmuel II 6:6) to Uzza. And a dislocated shoulder or thigh in an animal, whether
it is a *tereifah*, is *shmutas* (*Chullin* 57a), the bone having slipped out of
place. Rashi widens the root further to escaping danger or fleeing trouble
(Bereishis 19:17, Iyov 1:15), and rabbinic usage has *hishmit* for skipping a
letter in writing a sefer Torah or a pasuk in reading it (*Megillah* 18b),
whence the *hashmatos*, the omissions, at the back of many sefarim. Despite its currency in speech, *Shemittah* is fairly rare in the Bavli outside
quotations of these pesukim (*Nazir* 8b, *Sotah* 41a, *Gittin* 36a, *Bava Metzia*
30b and 48b, *Sanhedrin* 32a, *Shevuos* 44b, and several other
places).

### chalitzah (חליצה)
Root ח-ל-ץ.

- The Midrash (*Vayikra Rabbah* 34:15) gives four senses: slipping, as in the
  *yevamah* drawing the shoe off her brother-in-law's foot (Devarim 25:9);
  arming, since the soldiers of Israel are *chalutzim* (Devarim 3:18); saving,
  from "*chaltzeini* from an evil man" (Tehillim 140:2); and letting rest, from
  the Shabbos tefillah *retzei v'hachalitzeinu*. Three of these run parallel to
  senses of *shemittah*, and the Gemara itself uses a cognate of *shemittah* for
  the *chalitzah* act: the *yevamah* becomes permitted to others with the
  *shemittah* of most of the heel (*Yevamos* 102a).
- Pappenheim's unifying sense is extraction from within: the foot out of the
  shoe, the *tzaraas*-stricken stones out of the wall (Vayikra 14:40, 14:43),
  the soldiers drawn out from the nation for the war with Midian (Bamidbar
  31:3), who are then the *chalutzim* (Bamidbar 32:30, Devarim 3:18).

*Chalatzayim*, the loins, appears either of a soldier girding himself (Yeshayahu
5:27, 11:5, 32:11; Iyov 38:3, 40:7) or of birth (Bereishis 35:11; Melachim I
5:19; Divrei HaYamim II 6:9; Yirmiyahu 30:6); *Shoresh Yesha* takes both from
the senses above, the place weapons were typically worn, or the place a child
slips out from. The Aramaic *charatz* (Daniel 5:6) seems to be the same word
derived through the interchange of lamed and reish. Aramaic also uses a cognate
of *chalitzah* for removing clothing (Targum to Shmuel I 31:8-9, Divrei HaYamim
I 10:8), possibly because stripping off one's clothes is extracting the body
from within its garments, and this Aramaic usage may be the etymological basis
for the Modern Hebrew *chultzah*, a shirt. Against that, Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein
(1899-1983) writes in his etymological dictionary that *chultzah* was originally
coined by Prof. Joseph Klausner (1874-1958) to mean "blouse," and derived it
from the hips-and-thighs sense of *chalatzayim*, a blouse typically covering the
loins.

### shalaf (שלף) and shal (של)
*Shalaf* appears twenty-five times, most commonly of drawing a sword (Bamidbar
22:23, 22:31; Yehoshua 5:13; Shoftim 3:22, 8:10, 8:20, 9:54, 20:2, 20:15, 20:17, 20:25,
20:35, 20:46; Shmuel I 17:51, 31:4; Shmuel II 24:9; Melachim II 3:26; Divrei
HaYamim I 10:4, 21:16, 21:25), and less commonly of slipping off a shoe for
symbolic deal-making, the ancient equivalent of a handshake (Rus 4:7-8).
Pappenheim parses it as a portmanteau of ש-ל, removing, with א-ף, face, taking
something out in a confrontational, in-your-face way. Yoktan, son of Ever, had a
son named Shalaf (Bereishis 10:26), and Targum Yonasan there with Targum Rav
Yosef (to Divrei HaYamim I 1:20) say he was the first to draw water off rivers
into channels, so his name seems to allude to that innovation. *Shal* is the verb of "remove your shoes from
your feet" at the burning bush and to Yehoshua (Shemos 3:5, Yehoshua 5:15).

## Rendering guidance

- *Shemittah*: keep it transliterated as the name of the year, and render the
  verb "release" or "let go," not "cancel." "Cancel" is a bookkeeping word and
  loses the picture of a hand opening.
- Trap: English "remove" will absorb *shal*, *shalaf*, and *chalitzah* at once.
  Where a passage names the manner of removal, mark it: "slipped off," "drew
  off," "drew out."
- Second trap: *chalitzah* has four senses in one Midrash. In Devarim 3:18 the
  *chalutzim* are armed men, not extracted ones; in Tehillim 140:2 *chaltzeini*
  is "rescue me." Do not carry the shoe ceremony's sense into either.
- The *chalitzah* ceremony itself stays transliterated; "the removal of the shoe"
  as a gloss, once.
- *Shalaf* of a sword is "drew," never "unsheathed" when the pasuk also names the
  sheath, or the English says the same thing twice.
