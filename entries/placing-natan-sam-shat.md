# Placing: natan / sam / shat

## Bottom line

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim ranks the three by how deliberate and how lasting the
placing is. *Natan* is the loosest: to **put** something down, anywhere. *Sam*
is to **place** a thing in a spot designated for it, as part of a plan, but for
no stated duration. *Shat* is to **set** or **lay** a thing into an order, where
it is meant to stay indefinitely. The Rokeach draws the line differently: *sim*
is placing with intent, and *yashis* placing without it.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| נתן | natan | put | put down anywhere, haphazardly (Pappenheim); the sockets and bars of the Mishkan |
| שם | sam | place | into its designated spot, by plan, for no stated duration (Pappenheim); with intent (the Rokeach) |
| שת | shat | set, lay | set to stay indefinitely (Pappenheim); usually in a negative context (Shick); without intent (the Rokeach) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether shat is the deliberate placing or the undeliberate one.**

- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the three rise in deliberateness and permanence, and
  *shat* is placing into an order meant to stay indefinitely.
- The Rokeach (*Sefer HaChochmah*, *Shaar HaChiluk* §5, p. 20): *sim* is placing
  with intent, *yashis* without.

## Distinctions

### natan (נתן)
Root ת-ן, "giving," in Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's system. The most haphazard way
of simply placing something somewhere. He notes that *natan* and *sam* are often
interchangeable, since both at bottom just mean putting a thing in a spot.

### sam (שם)
Placing into a spot specifically designated for it, deliberately, as part of a
larger plan or system, but with no implication about how long it stays. Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ס-ם (sin and samech interchange),
whose core sense is putting a thing in its place. From the same root he derives
*samim* (potions and elixirs, commonly kept in designated places), *bosem* and *besamim*
(fragrances, commonly made from *samim*, though the beis is normally a radical
in his system), and *osem* (Devarim 28:8, Mishlei 3:10), the storehouse where
*samim* are commonly kept. He also derives the Mishnaic *siman* ("sign") from it, a sign
being the means of placing things in their proper category (compare Yeshayahu
28:25); others derive *siman* instead from the Greek *semeion*
("sign, mark"), which is the etymon of the English "semantics" and "semiotics."

### shat (שת)
Placing into a system, like *sam*, but meant to remain in place for an
indefinitely long time. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's cognates: *shesos*, the
foundations of a building, laid to stay and to carry what is built on them
(Tehillim 11:3), and *sheti*, the warp, the vertical axis of a weave (against
*erev*, the woof), the threads deliberately set within a larger structure. In
*Cheshek Shlomo* he treats ש-ת as a standalone biliteral; in *Yerios Shlomo* he
derives it from ש-א. The first *shat* in the Torah is Hashem's sentence on the
snake, "I will place enmity between you and the woman" (Bereishis 3:15): had the
pasuk said *sam*, Rabbi Pappenheim argues, the enmity would have been temporary;
*shat* makes it lasting, and it lasts still. Sheis, Adam's youngest son, is named
for *shat* (Bereishis 4:25); Rabbi Klein explains that Hashem, in giving Adam
another son, had set mankind going again for the long term. After the Aron was
hidden away, the Mishnah relates, the focus of the Kodesh HaKodashim was the stone
called the *Even Shesiyah* (*Yoma* 5:2), which the Gemara (*Yoma* 54b) explains is
the foundation stone from which the world was built.

### The three together
Shemos 40:18, erecting the Mishkan: he *placed* (*natan*) its sockets, *placed*
(*sam*) its beams, *placed* (*natan*) its bars. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: it did
not matter which socket or bar went where, so *natan*; the beams were lettered
so each would go in its own spot (*Shabbos* 103a), so *sam*.

Shemos 10:1-2 uses *shat* of the signs Hashem set in Paroh and his servants, and
*sam* of the signs recounted to sons and grandsons. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
builds on Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: for those who lived through the *makkos* the
memory is etched permanently, hence *shat*; for later generations, who have only
a received tradition, the placement is less fixed, hence *sam*.

The Rokeach (*Sefer HaChochmah*, *Shaar HaChiluk* §5, p. 20): *sim* is placing
with intent, *yashis* without.

### Where the distinction seems to fail
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim himself concludes by noting several cases where Tanach
seems to use the two words indiscriminately. Yosef advises Paroh to *shat* an overseer
(Bereishis 41:33) but later says he was *sam* as lord over Egypt (Bereishis
45:9); Yaakov *shat* his right hand on Ephraim (Bereishis 48:14, 48:17) while
Yosef asks him to *sam* it on Menashe (48:18); Paroh *lo shat libo* to the plague
of blood (Shemos 7:22) while the Egyptians who ignored the hail *lo sam libo*
(Shemos 9:21). Rabbi Moshe Zuriel answers all three from Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim's own rule: Yosef proposed a long-term post but Paroh, in his jealous
stinginess, gave him a short-term appointment whose mandate he kept renewing;
Yaakov's hand enacted the standing reality that Ephraim would play a more
prominent role than Menashe through the generations, while Yosef thought his
father had laid the hand down haphazardly; and Paroh sought to strengthen his
refusal for the long term, holding out even after the Jews had left Egypt, while
the Egyptians who ignored the hail acted out of no strong conviction and simply
failed to take the threat seriously.

A separate reading: Rabbi Avraham Shick of Slonim (d. 1820) writes in *Eshed
HaNechalim* (to *Shemos Rabbah* 32:2) that most times *shat* appears in Tanach it
is in a negative context, while *sam* more often carries a positive or even
neutral connotation. The snake's curse, the first *shat* in Tanach, is a case in
point. Whether that accounts for every instance of the two words remains to be
seen.

## Rendering guidance

- נתן: "put." Keep the plainest English verb for the plainest Hebrew one.
- שם: "place" or "set in place."
- שת: "set" or "lay," and where the permanence is the *chiddush*, "set for all
  time" or "established."
- Trap: "put" will swallow all three. Shemos 40:18 alternates *natan*, *sam*,
  *natan* in one pasuk; three "put"s destroy the reason the Torah alternated.
- Trap: לא שת לבו and לא שם לבו both come out "he did not take it to heart."
  Where both appear (Shemos 7:22 against 9:21), distinguish them:
  "would not set his heart to it" against "did not give it a thought."
- Trap: do not upgrade *sam* to permanence. It says a designated place, not a
  lasting one; the duration is what *shat* adds.
- Frequency, as a check on how marked *shat* is: by Even-Shoshan's concordance,
  cognates of *shat* appear some 85 times in Tanach, and cognates of *sam* five
  hundred times more than that.
