# Shame: bushah / klimah / cherpah (chafrah)

## Bottom line

*Bushah* is the general, milder word: **shame**. *Klimah* is the graver,
inherently disgraceful kind: **humiliation** (or **disgrace**). *Cherpah* is
shame inflicted by another's insult or reproach: **disgrace, reproach**; its
partner *chafrah* is the mortified wish to dig a hole and hide:
**mortification**. In Tanach *bushah* is always written first when it stands
beside *klimah* or *cherpah*. *Why* it stands first is disputed: the
grammarians read the order as ascending intensity, while others read it as
chronological sequence.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| בושה | bushah | shame | mildest; written first in a pair (Radak); can be social only, without contrition (Edel) |
| כלימה | kelimah | humiliation | graver; inflicted from outside (Malbim); the deed itself is disgraceful (Edel) |
| חרפה | cherpah | disgrace, reproach | follows another's insult (Wertheimer); disparagement rather than shame (Bedersi); more intense than *bushah* and *klimah* (Chelouche) |
| חפרה | chafrah | mortification | wanting to dig a hole and hide (Wertheimer); the most intense, wanting to bury one's head (Malbim) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Why bushah always precedes klimah in a pasuk.** Three readings.

1. **Intensity.** Radak (to Yeshayahu 45:16 and *Sefer HaShorashim*), Rabbeinu
   Yonah (*Shaarei Teshuvah*), and Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*):
   the milder word comes first, so *bushah* is the lighter shame. Bedersi makes
   this his general approach to Hebrew synonyms, holding that in general, when
   several synonyms for one concept appear in tandem, Tanach sets them in
   ascending order from least intense to most intense. On that basis he holds
   *bushah* less intense than *klimah* and *cherpah* both.
2. **Physiological sequence.** Isaac Satanow (1732 to 1804), a Maskillic
   scholar, builds on *Bava Metzia* 58b, that one
   publicly shamed first blushes and then turns white. *Bushah* he ties to the
   root BET-SHIN, which also means "delaying," because shame first delays the
   normal circulation of the blood and reddens the face; *klimah* is the later
   stage, when the blood rushes away and the face pales, and so it comes after
   *bushah* chronologically rather than in intensity. *Chafrah*, for Satanow, is the shamed man's reaction:
   he begins to search and "dig" (*chofer*) for an answer to justify what he
   did.
3. **Cause, then effect.** Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter (1743 to 1826)
   alludes to Satanow's explanation and rejects it, berating Satanow for
   irreverence and perversion. Yet he assumes, as Satanow does, that the terms
   do not mark intensity but chronological sequence: *bushah* is the shaming
   act or occurrence that causes the shame, *klimah* the shame itself that
   results, so the word order reflects cause and effect.

**Which word is the most intense.**

- Bedersi says *klimah*.
- Malbim says *chafrah*, one who would bury his head.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (1920 to 2016), the late Chief Rabbi of Netanya,
  says *cherpah*, more intense than *bushah* and *klimah* both.

**Whether cherpah is shame or disparagement.**

- Bedersi understands it as related to disparaging rather than to shame.
- Wertheimer and Malbim treat it as shame received from outside, and Chelouche
  as a more intense shame than *bushah* and *klimah*.

## Distinctions

### bushah (בושה)
Root ב-ו-ש. The hypernym.
- Radak (to Yeshayahu 45:16 and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) notes that wherever
  *bushah* stands beside *klimah* it comes first, because it is the less intense
  form; Rabbeinu Yonah (*Shaarei Teshuvah*) says the same. Radak in *Sefer
  HaShorashim* adds a second way to divide the two: *bushah* is a general
  hypernym covering shame for a positive thing (a humble man shamed at being
  praised for a good deed) as well as for a negative one, while *klimah* is a
  hyponym confined to the negative reason.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*): *bushah* is the shame of an
  expectation that failed, whether of something happening or of one's own
  ability to do something; *chafrah* he makes its inverse, shame at something
  happening that was not supposed to happen.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel (1760 to 1828): *bushah* sometimes names a shame not
  inherent to the act at all but felt from the circumstances, as with the thief
  ashamed at being seen (Yirmiyahu 2:26), which stems from man's standing as a
  social creature and bespeaks nothing of his moral contrition.
- Malbim: *bushah* is internal shame.
- Wertheimer: *bushah* can arise from the shamed man himself or from someone
  outside him.
- Targum typically renders it *bahat*.

### klimah (כלימה)
Root כ-ל-ם.
- The most intense of the three for Bedersi: shame for having done what one
  ought not, or for being screamed at or otherwise called out for it.
- Edel: *klimah* and *cherpah* name the inherently shameful and humiliating
  aspect of what was done, so a thief who describes his theft with one of them
  shows he recognizes the moral reprehensibility of the act, beyond his shame at being caught.
- Malbim and Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935): *klimah* is shame
  that comes from outside, from another person humiliating him.
- Rabbi David Golumb (1861 to 1935) relates the root KAF-LAMED-MEM to
  KAF-LAMED-ALEPH, whence *kele* ("jail, incarceration"): a shamed man retreats
  into himself and locks himself up, in some sense, to avoid the outside world.
- Rav Hirsch (to Vayikra 13:59, Tehillim 139:16) similarly sees the root as
  related to the phonetically close GIMMEL-LAMED-MEM, through the interchange
  of kaf and gimmel, whence *golem* ("unshaped material"): as a *golem* lacks
  shape in a physical sense, so a shamed man feels he lacks shape or form in a
  spiritual and moral sense, as though part of his humanity had been taken from
  him.

### cherpah (חרפה) and chafrah (חפרה)
Root ח-ר-ף / ח-פ-ר.
- Wertheimer: *cherpah* is the shame felt after being belittled or insulted by
  another; *chafrah* is wanting to dig (*chofer*) a hole and crawl into it so
  that nobody sees him, again as the result of being shamed by another.
- Malbim: *chafrah* is the most intense, burying one's head out of sheer shame;
  he also separates internal *charfah* from external *cherpah*.
- Bedersi reads *cherpah* as closer to disparagement than to shame (see Rabbi
  Klein's separate essay "Disgraceful Disparagement," November 2021).
- Chelouche takes *cherpah* as more intense than *bushah* and *klimah*: the
  maidservant designated to a particular man for conjugal purposes is a
  *charufah* (Vayikra 19:20), a conjugation of *cherpah*, because she is placed
  in a humiliating position, having no matrimonial bond to the man for whom she
  is designated.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg relates *chafirah*, digging, to *cherpah*,
  disgrace: in the sense of digging for information, *chafirah* has a negative
  connotation, as if one were searching out negative information about another
  to bring it to light, and the spies' *vayachperu* (Devarim 1:22) refers to
  their digging for negative information about the Land.

Shapira-Frankfurter does not himself address *cherpah*/*chafrah*. Rabbi Klein
offers his own conjecture: that the root ח-פ-ר may be related to ח-ו-ר
(*chivar*, "white") through the interchange of peh and vav, since shame whitens
the face, so that *cherpah* would be no more than a metathesized *chafrah*.

The Vilna Gaon (to Mishlei 19:26): *bushah* is in private, *chafurah* in public.

### The Aramaic
Targum typically renders *bushah* as *bahat* and *klimah* as *kissuf*; hence the
Talmudic Aramaic *ichsaf*, to become ashamed, and the kabbalistic *nehama
d'kisufa*, "the bread of shame," for the Divine sustenance Hashem grants a man
who is undeserving. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469 to 1549) in *Meturgeman* points out
that *cherpah* is usually rendered by Targum as *chisda* or the like, which led
the Maggid of Kozhnitz (Rabbi Yisroel Hopstein, 1737 to 1814) to read *chassid* as
one ashamed at the thought that he could accomplish anything on his own without
Hashem's help.

The root כ-ס-ף also yields *kesef* ("silver, money") and *nichsaf* ("desiring").
The Maharal (*Netivos Olam*, *Nesiv HaOsher*, ch. 2) ties those two together:
man's drive to accrue more and more money derives from his desire to become
rich. Radak's father, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (1105 to 1170), finds the shame sense of
this root already in Tzefaniah 2:1, though others read that verse as "desiring."
Rabbi Klein admits that it remains unclear how *kissuf* as shame relates to
money and desire, and offers three possibilities only as guesses: shame at not having acquired the wealth one thinks one should, the
shameful things a man may do in pursuit of wealth, or the whitish color of silver
resembling the paled face of a shamed man.

## Rendering guidance

- Doubled pair בושה וכלימה: "shame and humiliation." Keep the order; the Hebrew
  order is fixed, though what it marks is disputed.
- Standalone בושה in a mussar context: "shame." Do not reach for "embarrassment";
  it reads light and modern.
- כלימה alone: "humiliation" when the source is another person, "disgrace" when
  the deed itself is the disgrace.
- חרפה: "disgrace" or "reproach" (KJV-style "reproach" fits a pasuk); חפרה:
  "mortified" as a verb, "mortification" as a noun.
- Trap: English "shame" can cover all three. If the passage turns on the
  difference (a list, an ascending pair, a derashah on the order), do not let
  one English word absorb two Hebrew ones.
