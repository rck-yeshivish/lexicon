# Heretic: min / apikores / kofer / meshumad (mumar)

## Bottom line

Rambam gives a taxonomy (*Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:6-9). *Min*
is a **sectarian**, wrong about Hashem Himself. *Apikores* is a **scoffer**,
wrong about prophecy, and in practice one who insults Torah and its scholars.
*Kofer* is a **denier**, wrong about the Torah, techiyas hameisim or Moshiach.
*Meshumad* is an **apostate**, one who has left Klal Yisrael for another faith
or is notorious for deliberate transgression. *Mumar* is the tamer word the
censors sometimes substituted for *meshumad*, a **convert-out**. In colloquial
speech the terms overlap, but in Rambam's codification each carries a specific
meaning.

## Distinctions

### min (מין)
Rambam (*Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:7) gives five: one who says there is no God running
the world, one who says there are many, one who says God has a body, one who
says God did not create everything, one who serves an intermediary. *Avodah
Zarah* 26b: the *min* transgresses to anger Hashem, the *meshumad* for pleasure.

There are many theories as to how sectarians came to be called *minim*.
- Rabbi Yishaya of Trani associates *min* with the pagan deity Meni (Yeshayahu
  65:11).
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) argues that Manes, also called Mani
  or Manichaeus, and Apikores were Greek philosophers who rejected conventional
  religion, and their followers came to be called *minim* and *apikorsim*
  respectively.
- Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto (to Shemos 20:4) works from the plain biblical
  sense of *min*, "type, species": each sectarian holds a slightly different
  creed, but all fall into the one broader *type*.
- Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia: the original sectarians called themselves *maaminim*,
  "believers," to bolster their religious bona fides, and the rabbis bowdlerized
  the word down to *minim*.
- Rabbi J. D. Eisenstein (*Otzar Yisroel* s.v. *min*, *minus*) cites some
  scholars, unnamed, who have it that the rabbis called the sectarians
  *maaminim* sarcastically and the word was then shortened to *minim*; he also
  cites a view that *min* was originally an acronym for *maamin Yeshu Notzri*,
  "believer in Jesus of Nazareth."
- Rabbi Shlomo Tzvi Shick writes that the rabbis called the Tzedukim *minim*
  because the Tzedukim denied that water pooled in a vessel is disqualified for
  a mikvah, so they never immersed properly and were permanently tamei, and the
  Greek word for an impure person is *mijan*.

### apikores (אפיקורוס)
Never in Tanach; twice in the Mishnah (*Sanhedrin* 10:1, *Avos* 2:14). Rambam
(*Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:8) gives three: one who denies prophecy altogether, one
who denies the prophecy of Moshe Rabbeinu, one who denies that Hashem knows
man's deeds. In his Mishnah commentary he adds the settled usage, anyone who
denigrates the Torah or those who learn it, matching *Sanhedrin* 99b, which
defines an *apikores* as one who insults a talmid chacham or insults another in
his presence. Rambam there connects the word to *hefker*, "ownerless."
Conventional wisdom ties the term to the Greek philosopher
Epicurus (341-270 BCE), who taught the hedonistic pursuit of physical pleasure as
a way of life. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur also derives it from a man's name, and adds
that since the term denotes one who insults talmidei chachamim, it makes sense to
presume the historical Epicurus behind it was himself given to insulting those
who learn Torah.

### kofer (כופר)
Root כ-פ-ר. Rambam (*Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:6) gives three *kofrim*: one who denies
the Torah, one who denies techiyas hameisim, one who denies the coming of
Moshiach; and (3:8) splits the first into denying that the Torah is from Hashem,
denying the Oral Torah's authority to explain the Written, and denying that the
Torah is unchangeable. The root's Mishnaic sense is legal denial, a litigant
denying a claim (*Shevuos* 4:1, 4:3-4, 5:1-2, 6:3); in the heresy sense Hebrew
*kofer* parallels its Arabic cognate *kafir*. In Biblical Hebrew the same root
yields a whole family, words for redemption, atonement, covering, pitch, village
and lion. The bridge to heresy seems to be covering over: one who denies might
cover up the facts by insisting on the truth of something other than the truth.

### meshumad (משומד)
Rambam (*Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:9): one notorious for deliberately transgressing
any single law or the whole Torah, who has turned his back on Klal Yisrael and
attached himself to another nation and its religion, uncoerced. Rashba (Responsa
3:352) rejects "renegade" and follows *Avodah Zarah* 26b, above.

In the berachah praying for the dissolution of heretics we call them
*malshinim*, informers; but some argue that this is a censored version and that
the original nusach had *meshumadim*, and some keep the custom of davening with
the older wording.

Etymologies split three ways. The simplest is ש-מ-ד, to destroy. On
Even-Shoshan's biblical concordance the root occurs ninety times in the Hebrew
portions of Tanach and once in the Aramaic (Daniel 7:26); it also seems to stand
behind the personal name Shemed, a man of the tribe of Binyamin (Divrei HaYamim
I 8:12). *Shmad* as a noun is the Gemara's word for the destructive decrees
imposed on Klal Yisrael from time to time (see the uncensored *Rosh HaShanah*
19a). On this root:
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*), because most such conversions
  happen in times of *shmad*.
- Rabbi Yair Chaim Bachrach (*Mekor Chaim* to *Orach Chaim* 118), because the
  apostate has been cut off and destroyed by detaching himself from the nation.
- Rabbi Shmuel David Luzzatto (to Shemos 12:43), one who converted under a
  decree of *shmad* and kept practicing once the coercion lifted.

Rabbi Ernest Klein's etymological dictionary of Hebrew cites the German Bible
scholar and philologist Friedrich Delitzsch, who argued that Hebrew *shmad* is a
metathesized form of the Akkadian *mashadu*, "striking," with the mem and shin
trading places. The Akkadian sense does not seem too far from that of the
biblical root.

Second, Ramban (to Shemos 12:43) derives it from מ-ד-ע, the root Targum Onkelos
uses for Yosef recognizing his brothers (Bereishis 42:8): the root can seemingly
also carry the reverse, one who has become estranged and does *not* recognize, so
the *meshumad* has estranged himself from Klal Yisrael and from their God by his
evil deeds. On this reading one must assume the final ayin was simply dropped,
and Ramban brings other examples of the same phenomenon.

Third, from ע-מ-ד. In Biblical Hebrew that root means standing or stopping, but
in the Syriac dialect of Aramaic its declensions mean to immerse in water: the
*Peshitta* renders the immersing of vessels in a mikvah (Bamidbar 31:23) with a
verb from ע-מ-ד, and the usage is common in early Syriac Christian writing,
especially of baptism; a version of the verb also appears in *Gittin* 67b of
submerging in water, there with the initial ayin dropped. On this, Rav Hai Gaon,
cited by Rabbi Moshe Ibn Ezra (*Sefer HaIyunim VeHaDiyunim*) and Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*), reads the word as originally *meshuamad*, with an
ayin that was dropped because it grew too hard to pronounce, so that a *meshumad*
is specifically a Christian, by his baptism. On Rav Hai Gaon's reading the shin
is radical to the root, which led the philologist Avraham Even-Shoshan to suggest
the word reflects the *shaphel* inflection, where an initial shin does grammatical
work. Because of that reading, Rabbi Moshe Ibn Ezra holds Rabshakeh could not
have been an actual *meshumad*, since he predated Christianity; Rabbi Shlomo Tzvi
Shick similarly takes *meshumad* literally as "he who had hitherto immersed in
waters," a reference to Christians, who baptize by sprinkling water that had stood
in a vessel and so cannot stand in for a mikvah. Rashbatz (*Magen Avos*) keeps
both forms, *meshuamad* for Christians and *meshumad* for other idolaters; the
*Kuzari* (3:65) uses *meshuamadim* of Christians for their baptism. Rabbi Tanchum
HaYerushalmi (*HaMadrich HaMaspik*) rejects Rav Hai Gaon outright: *meshumad*
covers any apostate, which is why Chazal (*Sanhedrin* 60a) call Rabshakeh one,
though he obviously predated Christianity.

### mumar (מומר)
Root מ-ו-ר, to switch. Christian censors sometimes found *meshumad* too
offensive, and it was switched out for the tamer *mumar*; switching religions
sounds milder than being destroyed. Rabbi Shmuel
David Luzzatto: *mumar* is one who changed religion willingly, against the
coerced-then-persisting *meshumad*. Chasam Sofer (*Kovetz Teshuvos* 79): *mumar* is anyone who traded the eternal Torah for something
passing, even in one mitzvah, while *meshumad* is reserved for one who rejects
the whole Torah; he adds that the printers blurred the two in practice.

## Rendering guidance

- Translating Rambam's list at *Hilchos Teshuvah* 3:6, keep four English words:
  "sectarians, scoffers, deniers and apostates." A single "heretics" destroys
  the taxonomy the halachah builds.
- מין: "sectarian." Avoid "heretic," the generic, and any historical
  identification the sources do not make.
- אפיקורוס: "scoffer" where the sense is insulting Torah and talmidei chachamim
  (*Sanhedrin* 99b, Rambam to the Mishnah); "*apikores*" transliterated where
  Rambam's three doctrinal denials are meant, since no English word covers them.
- כופר: "denier," not "unbeliever"; the root is active covering-over. In a
  Shevuos sugya it is plain "denial" of a claim, so keep heresy out.
- משומד / מומר: "apostate" and "convert-out," never one word for both. Where a
  source turns on which is graver, say which view you follow: Rabbi Shmuel David
  Luzzatto has *meshumad* the coerced-then-willing one and *mumar* the freely
  willing one; Chasam Sofer has them the other way about.
- Trap: the berachah against heretics. Where the text reads *malshinim*, render
  "informers," noting that some argue this is a censored form of the older nusach
  *meshumadim*, which some still say.
