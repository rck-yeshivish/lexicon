# Fish: nun / kavra / shibuta (sandal, tanin, nin)

## Bottom line

*Dag* is the Hebrew for fish and the only one Tanach uses; *nun* and *kavra* are its
Aramaic counterparts, both **fish**. *Shibuta* and *sandal* are named species. *Tanin*
is a **sea monster**. *Nun* never occurs in Tanach in the sense of fish;
where it does occur, in *Yehoshua bin Nun*, it is a man's name, and the fish sense is a
derashah on it.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| נון | nun | fish | Aramaic, the Targum's *dag*; absent from Tanach, perhaps as a deified name (Rabbi Aharon Marcus) |
| כוורא | kavra | fish | Aramaic; possibly a species rather than the general term (Tosafos to Moed Katan 11a; Chullin 109b) |
| שיבוטא | shibuta | shibuta | a specific kosher river fish of Bavel whose brain tastes like pork (Chullin 109b) |
| סנדל | sandal | sandal-fish | a sea fish (Niddah 25b); like a piece of meat without clear limbs (Meiri) |
| תנין | tanin | sea monster | the sea-monsters of Bereishis 1:21; a big fish (Rashi), perhaps a sea snake (Vilna Gaon) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which came first, nun or nin.**

- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:21): *nun* derives from *nin*, offspring, the fish
  named for its fruitfulness; he compares this to *dag*, which primarily denotes
  fecundity.
- Rabbi Ron Yosef Chaim Masoud Abuchatzeira: the reverse. Berachos 20a teaches
  that fish are fruitful and multiply in large quantities because the ayin hara
  has no hold on them, so naming descendants by the fish-word turns the ayin hara
  from them; he compares this to the well-known custom among Tunisian Jews,
  especially those of Djerba, of giving children fish-related names to immunize
  them against the ayin hara. Examples: masculine Hayuta/Hauita ("fish" in some
  North African dialects of Arabic, though in Aramaic it means "snake"), Manani
  (grouper, possibly also related to *nun*), Bugid (striped red mullet), Hadir
  (torpedo fish), Karutz (bass), Uzifa, Wurgana; feminine Shelbia (salema porgy),
  Svirsa, Murgana, Manana (the feminized Manani), and Baharia (mermaid).

**Why Ninveh may be named for a fish.**

- Rabbi Avraham Rivlin: *nun* plus *naveh*, a fish inside a house, and Yonah, who
  refused to go, was made to live out the name inside a fish.
- Rabbi Nissim Paniri: *Yonah* and *Ninveh* share letters, Yonah's name lacking
  the second nun, so Hashem put him inside a *nun* to supply it.
- Rabbi Aryeh Moshe Teicholtz suggests that the name recalls the fish-cult
  worshipped there.

**What kavra and shibuta are.**

- Whether *kavra* means fish in general or a particular fish remains unclear
  (see Tosafos to Moed Katan 11a).
- Dr. Marcus Jastrow reads it as live fish kept in a basket, which would let it
  cover any fish so housed.
- Chullin 109b favors a species.
- On *shibuta*, Jastrow says probably the mullet, others the sturgeon or
  porpoise.
- Drs. Zohar Amar and Ari Zivotofsky identify the shirbot or shabout
  (*Arabibarbus grypus*), which Rabbi Klein calls the most definitive approach
  and which fits the Yerushalmi (Taanis 4:5) that *shibuta* is found in Bavel and
  not in Eretz Yisrael.
- *Minchas Chinuch* (550:2) suggests the name can cover kosher and non-kosher
  fish both.

**Yehoshua bin Nun.** His father's name was Nun (Divrei HaYamim I 7:27).

- An apocryphal midrash made famous by Rabbi Avraham Vilner has Yehoshua put into
  the river as a small child and swallowed by a fish, the fish caught and brought
  to Pharaoh, the child found inside, raised in Pharaoh's house and risen to
  Chief Executioner; Rabbi Yitzchak Yishaya Weiss of Neve Achiezer in Bnei Brak
  has already debunked the midrash's provenance.
- Other traditions give two further reasons for the name: *Midrash HaBiur* (to
  the haftarah of Shelach), that he would swallow the thirty-one kings of Canaan
  like a fish, and *Megaleh Amukos* 27, that Hashem was ready to hear his
  *tachanunim* once he entered the Land.

## Distinctions

### nun (נון)
The ordinary Aramaic word, as *nun*, *nuna* and *nuni*, and the Targumim's standard
rendering of the Hebrew *dag*; it remains the standard word for fish in Hebrew's
Semitic sister languages, Aramaic and Ugaritic alike. Kiddushin 25a records that a town mocked Rav Hamnuna,
whose name sounds like *cham nuna*, hot fish, by calling him *kar nuna*, cold fish. In
*Ksav Ivri* the letter nun is drawn like a fish. Rabbi Aharon Marcus theorizes that the
word's absence from Tanach is deliberate: the Canaanites and other nations deified the
fish or sea-creature the word denotes, turning Nun into the name of a god, and Biblical
Hebrew left the word out of every book to downplay that, leaving *dag* as the standard.

### kavra (כוורא), shibuta (שיבוטא) and sandal (סנדל)
*Kavra* is common in Judeo-Aramaic beside *nun*, and the doubt about its scope is old
(Tosafos to Moed Katan 11a). Dr. Marcus Jastrow notes that the Mishnaic *kaveres* means a
beehive or basket (Sheviis 10:7; Bava Basra 5:3; Keilim 8:1, 15:1, 22:10; Ohalos 5:6, 8:1,
8:3, 9:1) and so explains *kavra* as live fish kept in a cauf, a basket, which would let
the word cover any fish housed in such a portable tank. On the other hand Chullin 109b,
which says *kavra* tastes like the *girusa* bird (Jastrow: the moor hen), reads like one
species rather than a class. *Shibuta*: Chullin 109b, that its brain tastes like pork and so serves
as the kosher stand-in for that taste; Kiddushin 41a, that Rava salted *shibuta* in honor
of Shabbos. There is an ancient tradition of a fish that does not swim on Shabbos (Radak to
Bereishis 2:3; *Yalkut Reuveni* to Bereishis 2:2; *Shevet Mussar* ch. 11), and on that
basis some sources connect *shibuta*, spelled with a tes, to *Shabbos*, spelled with a
tav, identifying the *shibuta* as that fish (*Megadim Chadashim* to Shabbos 119a).
*Sandal*: the Mishnah (Bechoros 8:1, Kerisos 1:3, Niddah 3:4) discusses the halachic
status of a miscarriage shaped like a *sandal*; Niddah 25b explains that the shape is
that of a sea-fish, and Rashi (there and to Kesubos 39a), his son-in-law Rivan (to
Yevamos 12b) and, by implication, the Yerushalmi (Niddah 3:4) take *sandal* as an actual
fish by that name; Meiri (to Yevamos 12b) adds that it resembles a free-floating piece
of meat with no clear limbs, on which Rabbi Klein wonders in passing whether a jellyfish
is meant.

### tanin (תנין) and nin (נין)
Bereishis 1:21, the sea-monsters of the fifth day.

- Rabbi Aharon Marcus argues that the tav is not part of the root, leaving *nun*
  at the core, and explicitly rejects the scholarly speculation that *taninim* is
  a Sanskrit loanword.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 1:21) also suggests that *tannin* derives from *nun*,
  but adds that *nun* itself derives from *nin*, offspring (in Modern Hebrew,
  great-grandchild), comparing this to *dag*, whose primary sense is fecundity.
- The Vilna Gaon writes that *nachash* is to land as *tanin* is to sea, an
  analogy which suggests that *tanin* is a sea snake (possibly an eel or
  something similar).
- Rashi (to Bereishis 1:21 and Niddah 22b) simply writes that a *tanin* is a big
  fish.

## Rendering guidance

- *Nun* and *kavra* in a Gemara are both simply **fish**; do not manufacture a
  distinction the sugya does not need.
- The trap is "fish" absorbing the named species. Where Chullin 109b names *shibuta* or
  Niddah 25b names *sandal*, keep the Hebrew and gloss it ("the shibuta fish"), since
  the identification is disputed and an English species name commits you to one side of
  it.
- *Tanin* must not be rendered "fish": **sea monster** at Bereishis 1:21, and elsewhere
  a serpent, as context decides. *Nin* is **descendant**, never fish.
- In *Yehoshua bin Nun*, render **son of Nun**, a name; bring the fish sense in only
  where a derashah is quoted, and say that it is a derashah.
