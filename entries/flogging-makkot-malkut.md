# Flogging: makkot / malkut (lakah)

## Bottom line

*Makkah* is a **blow**, and from there a **wound** and a **plague**; the plural
*makkot* is the Torah's own word for judicial **lashes**. *Malkut* is the
Mishnaic word for the same punishment, **flogging**, built on the Aramaic
*lakah*. They translate alike but are not one word in two coats: *makkeh* is the
one who strikes and *lokeh* the one struck (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur), and several
authorities read a real difference of scope into the pair. Do not render *lakah*
"hit" everywhere; outside the flogging sugyos it means impaired, not struck.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מכה | makkah | blow, wound, plague | Hebrew, active, the striking and its result (Ibn Janach, Radak) |
| מכות | makkot | lashes | the Torah's verb for flogging; rabbinic *makkos mardus* on one view (Taieb) |
| מלקות | malkut | flogging | Mishnaic, from Aramaic; Torah-ordained lashes on that same view (Taieb) |
| לקה | lakah | be struck, be impaired | passive (HaBachur), and broader than striking (Tiferes Yisrael) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which of the two is the wider word.** Two opposed readings, both prompted by
the question of why the masechta is called Makkos.

1. **Makkot is the narrower, so the better name.** Tiferes Yisrael (Rabbi
   Yisrael Lipschutz): *lakah* is not specifically "hit" but a general word for
   breaking, damaging, or rendering defective, hitting being only one way to
   produce that. An eclipse is the luminary being *lokeh* (Sukkah 29a), and
   black blood is red blood that was *lakah* (Sukkah 33b). *Makkot* is therefore
   the more precise title.
2. **Makkot is the wider, so the better name.** Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of
   Amsterdam (*Maaseh Rokeach*): *malkut* is only physical striking, while
   *makkot* covers any painful ordeal. The masechta treats not only flogging but
   galus, so *Makkos* is the name that takes in both punishments.

The Bottom line follows the Tiferes Yisrael on *lakah* itself, whose eclipse and
blood cases are decisive for the word's range; neither view becomes a
translation rule, since both agree the masechta's subject is flogging.

**Which term belongs to Torah lashes and which to rabbinic.** Rabbi Shmuel Taieb
(*Shemen Tov*): the Gemara commonly uses *malkut* for lashes ordained by the
Torah and *makkot* for rabbinic ones, as in *makkos mardus*, lashes of
rebellion. On his account the masechta, which treats Biblical lashes, "should"
have been called Malkus, which is the question Rabbi Naftali HaKohen Katz
answers.

**Why the name is Makkos.** Rabbi Naftali HaKohen Katz (*Semichas Chachamim*),
two answers: the name follows the Torah's own language, since cognates of
*makkah* occur five times in the flogging passage (Devarim 25:2-3) and *malkut*
nowhere in Tanach; and מכות has the letters of כמות, "like death," alluding to
the principle that flogging is in some respects a lesser death (Sanhedrin 10a).
Simpler still: the masechta has a Hebrew name and an Aramaic one, as Beitzah is
also Bei'ah, and Hungarian and chassidishe Jews commonly say Malkus.

**The root of makkah.** Menachem Ibn Saruk and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the
one-letter root כ, "hitting." Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, and Radak reject
monoliteral roots and give נ-כ-ה, the nun regularly dropping in conjugation.

## Distinctions

### makkah / makkot (מכה, מכות)
The primary sense is striking another. From the act the word passes to the wound
it leaves, and from there to a plague, so that the Ten Plagues are the *Eser
Makkos*, literally ten strikings. This is the word the Torah uses for the
court's flogging (Devarim 25:2-3), and hence the masechta's name.

### malkut / lakah (מלקות, לקה)
Neither *malkut* nor *lakah* occurs in Tanach; both are Mishnaic, from the
Aramaic root ל-ק-ה, "strike, damage." Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman* and
*Sefer HaTishbi*) gives the Targum's rule: wherever the Torah's *makkah* means
striking someone to punish or chastise him, the Targum renders it with a word
from *lakah*; where the striking is meant to injure or kill, the Targum uses
something else. He adds that *lakah* is not a clean equivalent of *makkeh*, the
one being struck and the other striking. The vocalization *malkus* is correct;
*malkos* is a common error.

## Rendering guidance

- מכה alone: "blow" for the act, "wound" for the mark, "plague" for a
  visitation. Eser Makkos = the Ten Plagues; "ten blows" only where a passage
  plays on the literal sense.
- מכות in a halachic context: "lashes." מלקות: "flogging" or "lashes."
- לקה: "was struck" of a person, but "was impaired," "was defective," or "was
  eclipsed" where no striking is meant. "The sun was struck" in Sukkah 29a
  imports a blow the sugya is not describing.
- Trap: English "lashes" covers *makkot* and *malkut* both, and usually should.
  Where it must not is a passage distinguishing Torah lashes from *makkos
  mardus*: on Rabbi Shmuel Taieb's reading the choice of word is the ruling, so
  keep the Hebrew and gloss it. When the two roots meet in one sentence the
  active/passive split is the guide: *makkeh* strikes, *lokeh* receives.
