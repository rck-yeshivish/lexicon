# Flogging: makkot / malkut (lakah)

## Bottom line

*Makkah* is a **blow**, and from there a **wound** and a **plague**; cognates of
*makkah* are the Torah's own language for judicial **lashes**, and the plural
*makkot* names them. *Malkut* is the
Mishnaic word for the same punishment, **flogging**, which the linguist Avraham
Even-Shoshan derives from the Aramaic root ל-ק-ה. On Even-Shoshan's account the
two mean essentially the same thing in different languages, and Rabbi Klein
posits from this that the masechta simply carries a Hebrew name and an Aramaic
one. Two other authorities do read a real difference of scope into the pair, in
opposite directions; and in any case *makkeh* is the one who strikes and *lokeh*
the one struck (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur). The Tiferes Yisrael holds that *lakah*
can mean impaired rather than struck.

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which of the two is the wider word.** Three readings, the first two opposed
and both bearing on the question of why the masechta is called Makkos, the
third holding that the two essentially mean the same thing.

1. **Makkot is the narrower, so the better name.** Tiferes Yisrael (Rabbi
   Yisrael Lipschutz): *lakah* is not specifically "hit" but a general word for
   breaking, damaging, or rendering defective, hitting being only one way to
   produce that. An eclipse is the luminary being *lokeh* (Sukkah 29a), and
   black blood is red blood that was *lakah* (Sukkah 33b). *Makkot* is therefore
   the more precise title.
2. **Makkot is the wider, so the better name.** Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of
   Amsterdam (*Maaseh Rokeach*): *malkut* is only physical striking, while
   *makkot* covers any painful ordeal. The masechta treats galus as well as flogging, so *Makkos* is the name that takes in both punishments.
3. **The two are one word in two languages.** Avraham Even-Shoshan, in his
   dictionary: *malkut* and *makkot* essentially mean the same thing, only in
   Aramaic and in Hebrew respectively.

**Which term belongs to Torah lashes and which to rabbinic.** Rabbi Shmuel Taieb
(*Shemen Tov*): the Gemara commonly uses *malkut* for lashes ordained by the
Torah and *makkot* for rabbinic ones, as in *makkos mardus*, lashes of
rebellion. On his account the masechta, which treats Biblical lashes, "should"
have been called Malkus, which is the question Rabbi Naftali HaKohen Katz
answers.

**Why the name is Makkos.**

- Rabbi Naftali HaKohen Katz (*Semichas Chachamim*), two answers: the name
  follows the Torah's own language, since cognates of *makkah* occur five times
  in the flogging passage (Devarim 25:2-3) and *malkut* nowhere in Tanach; and
  מכות has the letters of כמות, "like death," alluding to the principle that
  flogging is in some respects a lesser death (Sanhedrin 10a).
- Rabbi Klein proposes: in light of HaBachur's and Even-Shoshan's understanding
  "we may posit" that the masechta has a Hebrew name and an Aramaic one, as
  Beitzah is also Bei'ah, and many Hungarian and chassidishe Jews indeed have
  the custom of saying Malkus.

**The root of makkah.**

- Menachem Ibn Saruk and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the one-letter root כ,
  "hitting."
- Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, and Radak reject monoliteral roots and give נ-כ-ה,
  the nun regularly dropping in conjugation.

## Distinctions

### makkah / makkot (מכה, מכות)
The primary sense is striking another. From the act the word passes to the wound
it leaves, and from there to a plague, so that the Ten Plagues are the *Eser
Makkos*, literally ten strikings. This is the word the Torah uses for the
court's flogging (Devarim 25:2-3), and, for Rabbi Naftali HaKohen Katz, hence
the masechta's name.

The Zohar (Bamidbar 145b): *makkah* is from *koh*, so. Rabbi Moshe Shapiro
builds on this: the prophets opened with *koh amar Hashem*, and the meaning of
what they saw was often unclear and left to their interpretation; so a *makkah*
always carries a Divine message to the one struck, whose meaning is not always
plain. Moshe's prophecy, by contrast, opens *zeh hadavar* (Shemos 16:16, 16:32,
35:4; Vayikra 8:5, 9:6, 17:2; Bamidbar 30:2, 36:6), since Hashem spoke to him
more clearly than to any other prophet (Bamidbar 12:6-8).

### malkut / lakah (מלקות, לקה)
Neither *malkut* nor *lakah* occurs in Tanach; both do appear in Mishnaic
Hebrew.

- Avraham Even-Shoshan, in his dictionary, derives the word from the Aramaic
  root ל-ק-ה, "hit" or "damage," and compares it to the Akkadian *laku*,
  "weakened"; he adds that the vocalization *malkut* is commonly mispronounced
  *malkot*.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgeman* and *Sefer HaTishbi*) gives the Targum's
  rule: wherever the Torah's *makkah* means striking someone to punish or
  chastise him, the Targum renders it with a word from *lakah*; where the
  striking is meant simply to hurt or even to kill, the Targum uses words
  unrelated to *lakah*. He adds that *lakah* is not a perfect translation of
  *makkeh*, the one being struck and the other striking.

Rabbi Klein adds an observation of his own on the English side: the verb
"lick" in "to lick one's enemies" (built on Bamidbar 22:4) means to smite or
defeat, and "to lick the whip" is to taste punishment, so the Germanic *lick*
covers both smiting and passing the tongue over something. He notes at once that
linguists would say the resemblance to the Semitic ל-ק-ה ("hitting") and ל-ק-ק
("licking") is simply chance.

Modern Hebrew says
*leharbitz* or *marbitz* for hitting, spanking, or beating someone up, from
ר-ב-ץ, which in Biblical Hebrew means crouching, stretching out, and lying down;
the sense was later extended to beating a man down so that he is left spread out
on the floor.

## Rendering guidance

- מכה alone: "blow" for the act, "wound" for the mark, "plague" for a
  visitation. Eser Makkos = the Ten Plagues; "ten blows" only where a passage
  plays on the literal sense.
- מכות in a halachic context: "lashes." מלקות: "flogging" or "lashes."
- לקה: "was struck" of a person, but "was impaired," "was defective," or "was
  eclipsed" where no striking is meant. "The sun was struck" in Sukkah 29a
  imports a blow the sugya is not describing.
- Trap: English "lashes" covers *makkot* and *malkut* both, and usually should.
  Where it must not is a passage distinguishing Torah lashes from *makkos
  mardus*: on Rabbi Shmuel Taieb's reading the choice of word is the ruling, so
  keep the Hebrew and gloss it. When the two roots meet in one sentence the
  active/passive split is the guide: *makkeh* strikes, *lokeh* receives.
