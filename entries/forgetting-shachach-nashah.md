# Forgetting: shachach / nashah

## Bottom line

*Shachach* is the ordinary word, found over a hundred times in Tanach, and the
word Devarim uses for every warning not to forget: **forget**. *Nashah* is rare,
and Malbim and Rav Hirsch both read it as the heavier word. For Malbim it is to
**forget entirely, put out of mind for good**: what is *shachach* can still be
recalled, and what is *nashah* cannot. Rav Hirsch gives it a moral note, that
*nashah* attaches to a man who held the thing lightly, so he bears more of the
blame for the forgetting. Rabbi Eliyahu Katz disagrees with both of them and
denies that there is any difference at all.

## Distinctions

### shachach (שכח)
Root ש-כ-ח. The standard verb. Devarim uses it for forgetting Hashem (6:12,
8:11, 8:14, 8:19, 32:18), Sinai (4:9), the Torah (31:21), the covenant with the
Avos (4:31) and with Yisrael (4:23), how the Jews angered Hashem in the
wilderness (9:7), the halachos of maaser (26:13), and what Amalek did (25:19).

- Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 8:1): *shachach* is forgetting inadvertently; elsewhere
  (Devarim 4:9) he defines it as forgetting because one's attention went to
  something else.
- Malbim (Iyov 11:6): what is forgotten by *shachach* can be retrieved later.

By way of metathesis, some connect ש-כ-ח to כ-ח-ש, deny or weaken, which uses
the same letters.

- Rabbi Yekusiel Yehudah Teitelbaum of Sighet (*Yitav Lev* to Shemos 10:1) is
  one of them: forget Hashem and your obligations to Him, and you will end by
  denying His existence outright.
- On the same interchange, Robert Alter and the editors of *Daas Mikra* explain
  *tishkach* in Tehillim 137:5 ("let my right hand be forgotten") as *tikchash*,
  let it be weakened.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro on the Aramaic *shachiach*, common or frequent, which shares
the root: one would expect the frequent thing to be the best remembered. Rather,
the mind files what recurs under "normal," and what is taken for granted is
easily lost; Chullin 75b says that a bizarre matter is the thing people surely
remember.

### nashah (נשה)
The rare word. The unambiguous case is Yosef naming Menashe: *ki nashani
Elokim es kol amali ve'es kol beis avi* (Bereishis 41:51). Rabbi Avraham
Menachem Rappaport (*Minchah Belulah*) claimed that this is the only cognate in
Tanach; Rabbi Klein answers that there seem to be a few more. Another possible
case is Devarim 32:18, which pairs both words in one pasuk, *tzur yeladcha teshi
vatishkach Kel mecholelecha*, where *teshi* is the same root with the *nun*
dropped in favor of a *tav* (Rashi there). Further cases: Eichah
3:17; Yirmiyahu 23:39; Yeshayahu 44:21; Iyov 39:17 and 11:6. The *gid hanasheh*
(Bereishis 32:33) is read by the Zohar (Bereishis 170b) as food for the yetzer
hara that makes a man forget his duties to Hashem, so on the Zohar's reading
the name itself is from *nashah*. The Gemara's *bei nasha* for a married
woman's family of origin was explained in the same sense, that on marrying she leaves the first household
behind and joins her husband's: so *Tosafos Chachmei Anglia* to Kiddushin 24a
first, then Rabbi Yehuda Chalava (a son of the Maharam Chalava) and his cousin
Rabbeinu Bachya ibn Chalava (each to Bereishis 41:51), and later
Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Musaf HaAruch*) and Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi (*Nachalas
Shivah*).

On the root itself:
- Menachem Ibn Saruk reduces *nashani* and *teshi* to the single letter *shin*;
  others, including Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach and Radak, hold that it may be נ-ש-ה
  or נ-ש-ש.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) gives the biliteral נ-ש, moving
  something out of its natural condition, and forgetting is information moved
  out of its place in the mind; on that root he also puts *gid hanasheh* (the
  sinew that moves from its place), *nosheh* (a lender, who moves his money out
  of his hand), *anush* (a sickly man, whose health has been displaced) and
  *enosh* (mankind, weak beside higher beings). Elsewhere he derives *teshi*
  from ת-ש, weakening.
- Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Hoil Moshe*) makes weakening the primary
  sense of נ-ש itself.

### The distinction, and the dissent
- Malbim (Iyov 11:6): *shachach* is recoverable, *nashah* is irretrievable.
- Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 8:1): *shachach* is inadvertent, *nashah* is what
  happens to a man who treated the matter flippantly, so he bears more blame for
  having forgotten; and (Devarim 4:9) *shachach* is displacement by another
  focus while *nashah* is a weakened memory.
- Rabbi Eliyahu Katz, formerly Chief Rabbi of Slovakia and later of Be'er Sheva,
  disagrees with both: the Targumim tend to render *shachach* and its cognates
  with *nashah*-words, which he takes as evidence that *nashah* is simply the
  Aramaic way of saying *shachach* and that no distinction exists.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *shachach* = **forget**. Reserve the heavier English for *nashah*.
- *Nashah* standing alone (Bereishis 41:51): "Hashem has made me **forget**
  entirely," or "has put out of my mind." On Malbim's reading Menashe's name carries the finality, though Rabbi Eliyahu Katz holds that *nashah* is simply the Aramaic way of saying *shachach*.
- The two in one pasuk (Devarim 32:18): the Hebrew moves from *teshi* to
  *vatishkach*, so do not render both with the same English verb. "You grew
  unmindful of the Rock that bore you, and forgot the God Who brought you
  forth" keeps them distinct without importing a claim about order.
- Trap: English "forget" will absorb both words, and "neglect" will quietly
  smuggle in Rav Hirsch's moral reading where the source has not made it.
  Use "forget" for *shachach*; choose the *nashah* rendering by which
  authority the passage follows, and say so in a note.
- Do not render *shachach* as "lose" or "blank on"; the register is wrong, and
  Devarim's warnings are commands about a state of mind, not lapses.
