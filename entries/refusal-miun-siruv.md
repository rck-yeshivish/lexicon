# Refusal: mi'un / siruv

## Bottom line

*Mi'un* is the biblical word for **refusing**, its cognates appearing forty-six
times in Tanach; *siruv* is Aramaic and rabbinic, at most once in Tanach, and
even that one instance is disputed. Rabbi Klein gives two approaches to the
difference and does not choose between them. On the first, resting on Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur's observation that the Targumim render most instances of
*meridah*, *meri* and *mi'un* alike by *siruv*, it *seems* the two are simply
synonyms sourced in different languages: *mi'un* genuine Hebrew, *siruv* a
borrowed Aramaic loanword that grew popular in post-biblical Hebrew. On the
second, Rabbi Yaakov Dienna's, they are not perfect synonyms: *mi'un* is
declining to comply with something asked of one, *siruv* is the utter rejection
of the proposition itself, and it *somehow* implies the more intense refusal.
Both words also have technical rabbinic senses: *mi'un* is the minor's refusal
of her husband, *siruv* is contempt of beis din.

## Distinctions

### mi'un (מיאון)
Cognates of *mi'un* appear forty-six times in Tanach. *Vayima'en* of Edom, who
when Yisrael appealed for permission to cross their territory on the way to Eretz
Yisrael flatly refused and even threatened military action (Bamidbar 20:21); of
Yaakov refusing to be comforted for Yosef (Bereishis 37:35), of Yosef refusing
Potifar's wife (Bereishis 39:8), of Yaakov refusing to put his right hand on
Yosef's firstborn (Bereishis 48:19), of Amnon deviously feigning sickness by
refusing to eat (Shmuel II 13:9), and in several other places. Nonetheless,
the more familiar Hebrew word for refusal is not *mi'un* but *siruv*.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814) derives *mi'un* from *ein* /
*ayin*, "no," "is not," "has not": one who refuses does not engage with what he
has rejected but stubbornly tries to ignore it.

### siruv (סירוב)
A hapax at best, and even that instance is not universally agreed upon. When
Hashem tells Yechezkel not to fear repercussions from the people over his
prophecies, He calls them *saravim* and *salonim* (Yechezkel 2:6). The
commentators agree that *salonim* are a kind of thorn; on *saravim* there are
two approaches.

- Menachem Ibn Saruk (920-970), Rashi (1040-1105), Radak (1160-1234) and others
  explain that the people were "rebels" and "refusers," and Menachem goes so far
  as to define *meridah* by *sarvanus* and *saravanim* by *mardus*; on their
  reading this is the only instance in Tanach of the root ס-ר-ב in the sense of
  refusing.
- Menachem's famous interlocutor Donash Ibn Labrat (920-990) disagrees with that
  assessment, proffering instead that *saravim* is a kind of thorn, just as
  *salonim* is.
- Rashi, Mahari Kara and others compare *salonim* to *silon* (Yechezkel 28:24),
  which they likewise take as a thorn.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, however, distinguishes the two. He traces both to the
  two-letter root ס-ל, repeated action: *salonim*, said of the people, are those
  who repeatedly bother and provoke others, while a *silon* is a thistle whose
  many prickly spikes stab one who holds it again and again.

In that same perek Hashem uses forms of *meri* /
*meridah* six times and Targum renders each with a cognate of *siruv*; and
where Yechezkel 2:3 uses *pesha* for rebellion, Targum renders it with an
Aramaicized *meridah*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549) points out in *Sefer
HaTishbi* and *Meturgaman* that most instances of the Hebrew *meridah*, *meri*
and *mi'un* in the Torah are rendered by the Targumim as *siruv*. In light of
this, Rabbi Klein writes, it *seems* that the two are indeed synonyms but are
sourced in different languages: *mi'un* and its cognates, appearing many times
in Tanach, *seem* to be genuine Hebrew, while *siruv*, which at most appears
once, *seems* to be a borrowed Aramaic loanword, and so became more popular in
post-biblical Hebrew when Aramaic had a stronger influence on the language.
There is, however, another approach to the question, given below.

In rabbinic Hebrew *sarvan* is settled: the Mishnah (*Berachos* 5:3) rules that
a chazan who errs is replaced at once, and warns that the replacement must not
be a *sarvan*. The Rambam and Bartenura: ordinarily one asked to lead should
show humility and decline at first, but here, replacing a chazan mid-tefillah,
refusing the honor is out of place. One who badgers another and will not let up
is *misarev* (*Nedarim* 8:7), because he refuses to give up on changing the
other's mind (see *Tosafos Yom Tov*); the form sometimes takes an extra hei,
*mis'harev*, for pestering (Rashi to Bava Kamma 28a, 32b, Chullin 94a).

### Where they part
The early commentary to Targum Onkelos *ascribed* to Rabbi Yaakov Dienna
(published under the various names *Patshegen*, *Tzintzenes HaMan*, and *Sefer
HaYair*) asks why Onkelos, who typically renders *mi'un* as *siruv*, does not do
so at Shemos 22:16, the verse that if the father of a seduced girl refuses to
give her to the seducer for marriage the seducer must pay a penalty. The Hebrew
reads *im ma'en yema'en*, yet Onkelos translates it as though it said, "and if
the father does not wish." The meaning does not necessarily change;
it is the wording that is unexpected.

Rabbi Dienna resolves this by positing that *mi'un* and *siruv* are not perfect
synonyms and that their meanings do not always line up. *Mi'un* refers
specifically to a case where somebody was asked to do something, perhaps a
favor, and refused to comply; *siruv* implies a stronger refusal than simple
non-compliance, the utter rejection of a proposition. Since the father declares
publicly, before the court and before his daughter, that he does not want her to
marry the seducer, he can be expected to use more toned-down verbiage, and so
Onkelos did not want to render *mi'un* by *siruv*, which would imply more
intense language than the father would likely use in public.

Similarly at Bamidbar 22:13, where Bilaam reports that Hashem *me'en* to let him
go with Balak's men, Targum again avoids *siruv*, rendering "there is no will
before Hashem to allow." Rabbi Dienna accounts for the unexpected phrasing by
explaining that Onkelos felt the more vulgar *siruv* to be *somewhat*
disrespectful when the refusal is Hashem's, and so rendered the passage
creatively, a bit differently. On this approach the difference between the two
lies not just in their etymological roots but in their meanings, *siruv*
*somehow* implying a more intense form of refusing than *mi'un* does. (On the
difference between *ma'en* and *me'en*, see Midrash *Sechel Tov* to Shemos
7:27.)

### The two technical senses
*Mi'un* in the Mishnah (*Moed Katan* 3:3, *Yevamos* 13:1-6, *Bava Metzia* 1:8,
*Sanhedrin* 1:3, *Chullin* 1:7) is the procedure by which a girl married off
rabbinically rather than by Torah law (for example, if she was orphaned of her
father and her mother or brothers married her off) refuses her husband and is
allowed to marry another. *Siruv* is refusing to heed a summons to beis din or
refusing to listen to its ruling; such a man, held in contempt of the court, may
be subject to sanctions resembling excommunication, and the dayanim may give the
opposing litigant permission to petition a non-Jewish court.

As for the English suffix in "refusenik": according to
the *Oxford English Dictionary* the *-nik* of *beatnik*, *no-goodnik*,
*peacenik* and *kibbutznik* is borrowed from Yiddish and Russian, and is said to
have come into vogue especially after the Russians launched the Sputnik
satellite.

## Rendering guidance

- Default both to "refuse." This follows Rabbi Klein's first approach, on which
  they are the same act in two languages, and is not a ruling against Rabbi
  Dienna, whom Rabbi Klein leaves standing.
- Where a source is arguing the difference (Rabbi Yaakov Dienna on Shemos 22:16
  and Bamidbar 22:13), render *mi'un* "declined" and *siruv* "flatly refused"
  or "rejected outright," and say which word is which.
- Trap: English "refuse" absorbs *mi'un*, *siruv*, and also *meri* /
  *meridah*, which the Targum renders by *siruv* but which is rebellion, not
  refusal. Keep "rebel" for *meri* even where the Targum uses the refusal word.
- The technical senses are never translated: *mi'un* of a ketanah and *siruv*
  of beis din both stay transliterated, with a gloss at first occurrence. A
  bare "refusal" for either is a mistranslation, not a simplification.
- *Sarvan* in *Berachos* 5:3 is "one who declines," and needs the Rambam and
  Bartenura's point in a note, that declining is right in the ordinary case and
  wrong in this one. Rabbi Klein himself glosses *sarvan* as "refusenik," but
  that is too modern a register.
- Bamidbar 22:13, *me'en Hashem*: "Hashem has refused to allow me," and if the
  Targum's phrasing is under discussion, quote it as "there is no will before
  Hashem," which is the whole point of the question.
