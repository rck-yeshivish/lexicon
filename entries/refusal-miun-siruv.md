# Refusal: mi'un / siruv

## Bottom line

*Mi'un* is the biblical word for **refusing**, forty-six occurrences; *siruv*
is Aramaic and rabbinic, at most once in Tanach. Treat them as synonyms by
default, differing only in language of origin. Where a source presses the
difference, follow Rabbi Yaakov Dienna: *mi'un* is declining to comply with a
request, *siruv* is the flat rejection of the proposition itself, the stronger
word. Both have technical rabbinic senses that must never be translated as
plain refusal: *mi'un* is the minor's annulment of her marriage, *siruv* is
contempt of beis din.

## Distinctions

### mi'un (מיאון)
Forty-six occurrences. *Vayima'en* of Edom refusing Yisrael passage (Bamidbar
20:21), of Yaakov refusing to be comforted for Yosef (Bereishis 37:35), of
Yosef refusing Potifar's wife (Bereishis 39:8), of Yaakov refusing to put his
right hand on Yosef's firstborn (Bereishis 48:19), of Amnon feigning illness by
refusing to eat (Shmuel II 13:9), and elsewhere.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives *mi'un* from *ein* / *ayin*, "there is not":
one who refuses does not engage with what he has rejected but stubbornly
ignores it.

### siruv (סירוב)
A hapax at best. Yechezkel 2:6 calls the people *saravim* and *salonim*.
All agree *salonim* are thorns; on *saravim* the Rishonim split. Menachem Ibn
Saruk, Rashi and Radak read it as rebels and refusers, and Menachem goes so far
as to define *meridah* by *sarvanus* and *sarvanim* by *mardus*; on their
reading this is the only occurrence of ס-ר-ב in Tanach. Donash Ibn Labrat
disagrees: *saravim* is a kind of thorn, parallel to *salonim*.

Its home is Aramaic. In that same perek Hashem uses forms of *meri* /
*meridah* six times and Targum renders each with a cognate of *siruv*; and
where Yechezkel 2:3 uses *pesha* for rebellion, Targum renders it with an
Aramaicized *meridah*. Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*, *Meturgaman*)
notes that the Targumim generally render Hebrew *meridah*, *meri* and *mi'un*
alike by *siruv*, which is the ground for treating the two as synonyms sourced
in different languages: *mi'un* native Hebrew, *siruv* an Aramaic loanword that
came into its own in post-biblical Hebrew.

In rabbinic Hebrew *sarvan* is settled: the Mishnah (*Berachos* 5:3) rules that
a chazan who errs is replaced at once, and warns that the replacement must not
be a *sarvan*. The Rambam and Bartenura: ordinarily one asked to lead should
show humility and decline at first, but here, replacing a chazan mid-tefillah,
refusing the honor is out of place. One who badgers another and will not let up
is *misarev* (*Nedarim* 8:7), because he refuses to give up on changing the
other's mind (see *Tosafos Yom Tov*); the form sometimes takes an extra hei,
*mis'harev*, for pestering (Rashi to Bava Kamma 28a, 32b, Chullin 94a).

### Where they part
Rabbi Yaakov Dienna, in the early commentary to Targum Onkelos printed as
*Patshegen* (also *Tzintzenes HaMan*, *Sefer HaYair*), asks why Onkelos, who
normally renders *mi'un* as *siruv*, declines to do so at Shemos 22:16, where
the seduced girl's father refuses to give her to the seducer: there Onkelos
renders it "and if the father does not wish." His answer: the two are not
perfect synonyms. *Mi'un* is refusing to comply with something asked of one;
*siruv* is the outright rejection of a proposition, and is stronger. The father
is declaring himself before beis din and before his daughter, so the milder
verb suits what he would actually say in public.

The same at Bamidbar 22:13, where Bilaam reports that Hashem *me'en* to let him
go, and Targum again avoids *siruv*, rendering "there is no will before Hashem
to permit." Rabbi Yaakov Dienna: Onkelos held the coarser *siruv* unfitting
when the refusal is Hashem's. (On *ma'en* against *me'en*, see *Sechel Tov* to
Shemos 7:27.)

### The two technical senses
*Mi'un* in the Mishnah (*Moed Katan* 3:3, *Yevamos* 13:1-6, *Bava Metzia* 1:8,
*Sanhedrin* 1:3, *Chullin* 1:7) is the procedure by which a girl married off
rabbinically rather than by Torah law, her father having died and her mother or
brothers having married her off, refuses her husband and is free to marry
another. *Siruv* is refusing a summons to beis din or refusing to accept its
ruling; the man in contempt may face sanctions resembling cherem, and the
dayanim may permit the other litigant to take the case to a non-Jewish court.

## Rendering guidance

- Default both to "refuse." Since they are the same act in two languages, an
  invented English contrast will mislead more often than it helps.
- Where a source is arguing the difference (Rabbi Yaakov Dienna on Shemos 22:16
  and Bamidbar 22:13), render *mi'un* "declined" and *siruv* "flatly refused"
  or "rejected outright," and say which word is which.
- Trap: English "refuse" absorbs *mi'un*, *siruv*, and also *meri* /
  *meridah*, which the Targum renders by *siruv* but which is rebellion, not
  refusal. Keep "rebel" for *meri* even where the Targum uses the refusal word.
- The technical senses are never translated: *mi'un* of a ketanah and *siruv*
  of beis din both stay transliterated, with a gloss at first occurrence. A
  bare "refusal" for either is a mistranslation, not a simplification.
- *Sarvan* in *Berachos* 5:3 is "one who declines," and needs the Rambam and
  Bartenura's point in a note, that declining is right in the ordinary case and
  wrong in this one; "refusenik" is a modern joke and does not belong in a
  sefer.
- Bamidbar 22:13, *me'en Hashem*: "Hashem has refused to allow me," and if the
  Targum's phrasing is under discussion, quote it as "there is no will before
  Hashem," which is the whole point of the question.
