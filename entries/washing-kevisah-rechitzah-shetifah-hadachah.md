# Washing: kevisah / rechitzah / shetifah / hadachah / merikah (kinuach)

## Bottom line

*Kevisah* is **laundering**. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim says it is used only of
objects, never of a body, and, in his second formulation, that the water goes
right through the cloth. *Rechitzah* is **washing** a body or a part of one, by
immersion or by pouring (Pappenheim); Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer allows it
outside or inside. *Shetifah* is **sluicing**, under flowing water for
Pappenheim and with much water for Wertheimer; Radak calls it the stronger
washing. *Hadachah*, typically translated **rinsing**, works the exterior as
*shetifah* does, but with little water (Wertheimer). *Sefer HaShem* instead
distinguishes it from *rechitzah* by what is washed away, *hadachah* for blood
and *rechitzah* for filth. *Merikah* is **scouring**. For Rabbi Avraham Bedersi
it is buffing off rust or tarnish rather than cleaning with water; Rashi and
Rambam keep it within the washing of a vessel, Rashi making it the inside as
*shetifah* is the outside, Rambam a matter of thoroughness. *Kinuach*, Rabbinic
only, is **wiping**. Vayikra 6:21 (*merikah*, *shetifah*) and Yeshayahu 4:4
(*rechitzah*, *hadachah*) each put two of them in a single verse.

## Distinctions

### kevisah (כביסה)
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *kevisah* is never used of the body, only of objects,
  above all clothing. It can be a whole garment immersed (Shemos 19:10, Vayikra
  11:25, 13:6) or one part of one cleaned (Vayikra 6:20), and it works either by
  bringing the garment to standing water or by pouring water on it. In his
  second formulation the contrast with *rechitzah* is depth: *rechitzah* rinses
  a surface, *kevisah* drives the water through and through, as it must with
  cloth.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843 to 1916) theorizes that כ-ב-ס means both launder and
  young sheep ([*keves*](sheep-and-goats-seh-keves-ayil-eiz.html#%D7%9B%D7%91%D7%A9)), alluding to the washing of the sheep's wool. He further
  relates *rachel*, ewe, to *rechitzah*, since Hebrew lamed apparently becomes
  tzadi in Akkadian, in allusion to the same process, and finds a hint in Shir
  HaShirim 6:6, which uses both words in one pasuk. Rabbi Klein writes that he
  has been unable to substantiate the lamed/tzadi interchange, but notes that
  the *Pesikta Rabbasi* expounds *keves* as though related to *kevisah*, the
  korban washing away sin.

On Shemos 19:10, *v'chibsu*: *Yevamos* 46b first tries to prove from it that
Klal Yisrael immersed at Sinai, then rejects the proof, since the washing may
have been for cleanliness alone.

- Rabbi Shlomo HaKohen of Vilna (1828 to 1905) finds the same implied in Targum
  Onkelos, who renders *v'chibsu* as *v'yichavrun*, "they shall whiten," not his
  usual rendering for laundering that purifies.
- The Netziv (Rabbi Naftali Tzvi Yehudah Berlin, 1817 to 1893) makes the same
  point, adding that the pasuk says [*simlah*](clothing-beged-levush-kesus-simlah.html#%D7%A9%D7%9E%D7%9C%D7%94), not *beged*, implying outer
  clothes only; tumah would not stop at the outer layer, so he infers that this
  was laundering in honor of the day.

### rechitzah (רחיצה)
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740 to 1814), for whom *rechitzah* and *kevisah*
  differ in what is cleaned while *shetifah* names a method: the body, whole
  (immersion in a mikveh, Vayikra 15:13, Bamidbar 19:7) or in part (feet,
  Bereishis 18:4; face, Bereishis 43:31), and by either method, entering
  existing water (Shemos 2:5, Bamidbar 19:7) or having water poured on one
  (Bereishis 43:31, Shemos 30:21).
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935) allows it both outside (the
  kiyor poured over hands and feet, Shemos 30:19) and inside (water put into an
  animal's innards to cleanse them), where *shetifah* is exterior only;
  alternatively, he says, the difference is the quantity of water (see
  *shetifah*).

### shetifah (שטיפה)
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the root ש-ט-ף means flow or stream; *shetifah* is
  holding a body or an object under running water and shaking it so the current
  does the cleaning. Unlike the other two it always brings the item to the
  water, never the water to the item, and it covers both bodies (Vayikra 15:11)
  and objects (Vayikra 6:21, 15:12).
- Wertheimer: as an active verb it cleans the exterior only, but in the passive
  it implies inside and out both, since with so much water both sides are almost
  inevitably cleansed; and, in his alternative formulation, it means more water
  than *rechitzah*, being associated with *mayim rabim* (Tehillim 32:6).
- Radak (to Melachim I 22:38) likewise takes it as the stronger washing.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi: *shetifah* runs an already clean item under a strong
  current to make it look cleaner still.

### hadachah (הדחה)
- Menachem Ibn Saruk and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, both working from two-letter
  roots, take it from ד-ח, to push away: *hadachah* washes off whatever water
  can carry away.
- Wertheimer: *hadachah* and *shetifah* both work the exterior, but *hadachah*
  uses a minimum of water. His proof is Yeshayahu 4:4, *rechitzah* for the filth
  of the daughters of Tzion and *hadachah* for the blood of Yerushalayim, blood
  coming away with little water and filth needing much.
- Bedersi: *rechitzah* is scrubbing off dirt in earnest, *hadachah* a plain
  rinse.
- *Sefer HaShem* (ascribed to Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms) makes the
  difference the substance removed, from Divrei HaYamim II 4:6, Mishlei 30:12
  and Yeshayahu 4:4: *hadachah* for blood, *rechitzah* for filth.

### merikah (מריקה) and tamruk (תמרוק)
Vayikra 6:21 requires a metal vessel used for a chatas to undergo *merikah* and
*shetifah* in water.
- Rashi (to *Zevachim* 96b): *merikah* is the inside of the vessel, *shetifah*
  the outside.
- Rambam (commentary to *Zevachim* 11:7) makes it a matter of degree instead:
  *merikah* purges clinging particles, *shetifah* is filling it with water and
  scrubbing further.
- Bedersi separates *merikah* from the whole group as buffing and polishing rather than water-cleaning, softening a surface and taking off rust, which is how
  *tamruk* in Esther 2:3 and 2:12 is a cosmetic regimen that smooths the skin.
  This fits, Rabbi Klein notes, with Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's derivation of
  both from ר-ק, emptying a thing of its blemishes and imperfections.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk files *merikah* and *tamruk* under מ-ר-ק in separate
  categories, without implying a link; Rashi (to Vayikra 6:21) does link them.
- Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 21:25 and Vayikra 6:21) compares *merikah* to *morag*, a
  threshing tool, by the interchange of kuf and gimmel, making it a penetrating
  cleaning that goes below the surface.
- *Machberes Menachem* seems to understand that *merikah* even entails
  scratching off the surface layer.

### kinuach (קינוח)
Rabbinic only: *Berachos* 8:3, *Shabbos* 21:2-3, *Bava Basra* 5:10, *Keilim*
28:2, *Parah* 3:9. Cleaning by wiping off what soils. Rashi (to Yeshayahu 47:11)
takes it as an expression of finality: what is wiped away is gone for good. It
is probably in this sense, Rabbi Klein suggests, that *kinuach seudah*, the
*kinuach* of the meal, came to mean dessert.

## Rendering guidance

- Vayikra 6:21, ומרק ושטף במים: "it shall be scoured and sluiced with water."
  Two words for two acts. Rashi has them as inside and outside, Rambam as two
  grades of thoroughness, so avoid an English pair that picks one reading.
- Yeshayahu 4:4: "when Hashem will have washed away the filth of the daughters
  of Tzion and rinsed out the blood of Yerushalayim." Keep "washed" for
  *rechitzah* and "rinsed" for *hadachah*; the distribution is the basis of
  Wertheimer's proof, and *Sefer HaShem* reads the same pasuk.
- כביסה: "launder" or "wash [clothes]," never "bathe." רחיצה of a person:
  "wash" or "bathe." Pappenheim's rule is object against body; an English "wash"
  for both loses it.
- Trap: "rinse" will take both *shetifah* and *hadachah*. Reserve "rinse" for
  *hadachah* and use "sluice," "flush" or "wash under running water" for
  *shetifah*, which carries *mayim rabim* with it.
- תמרוק in Esther: "beauty treatment" or "cosmetic regimen," not "purification";
  Bedersi and Rashi's tie to *merikah* make it a smoothing of the skin.
- קינוח: "wipe." Not for water-washing; for Rashi the point is that what is
  wiped is finished with.
