# Lice: kinnim / kinam / kinah (kalmta, parosh)

## Bottom line

*Kinnim* is **lice**, the plural; *kinam* is the same creature named as a
**species**; *kinah* is a single **louse**, a word of the Mishnah and Gemara
rather than of Tanach. The Aramaic is *kalmta*, and in the Gemara *kalmei*. A
*parosh* is a **flea**, for *Metzudas Tzion* a black *kinah* that jumps. At
Yeshayahu 51:6 and Bamidbar 13:33 the standard reading of *kein* is "so,
likewise"; certain commentators read it as "louse", a possibility first proposed
at Yeshayahu 51:6 by the seventeenth century Dutch Protestant scholar Ludovicus
de Dieu and taken up by others, Malbim among them.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| כנים | kinnim | lice | plural, many individual lice (Ibn Janach, Rashbam, Ibn Ezra) |
| כנם | kinam | the lice | the species as a whole, not a count (the same lexicographers) |
| כנה | kinah | a louse | the singular, Mishnaic and Talmudic only; at *Parah* 9:2, a grain pest |
| קלמתא | kalmta | lice (Aramaic) | the Targum's rendering; the Gemara's *kalmei* is the same word (Rashi to Berachos 51b); Akkadian *kalmatu*, Canaanite and Arabic *qml* |
| פרעש | parosh | flea | jumps and is black (*Metzudas Tzion*) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Where kinnim comes from.** Both readings run through Pappenheim's biliteral
כ-ן, whose core sense he gives as "base", the root of *kein* and *nachon* (a
claim resting on a valid basis), *tochnis* and *hachanah* (the groundwork of
any undertaking), *kohen* (who sees to the preparations for the *avodah*),
*kavanah*, and *kan*, a stand or base on which a thing rests.

1. **From kinui, an epithet.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*): a
   *kinui* is a linguistic base for referring to something without saying its
   name, and lice are so repellent that people would not give them a proper
   word, naming them obliquely instead.
2. **From kan, a base.** Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 8:12), more simply: a *kan* is
   what a thing rests on and is secondary to it, and lice are parasites resting
   on a host's body or hair, secondary to him.

Hirsch's is the simpler explanation.

**Whether the mem is part of the root.**

- Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), Rashbam, Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 8:12), and Ibn
  Parchon (*Machberes HeAruch*) all take pains to say it is not: the root is the
  biliteral כ-ן, *kinnim* is the plural, *kinam* the species. Ibn Saruk and
  Radak likewise trace the word to כ-ן.
- Against them stands Rabbi Klein's own thesis: he believes that *kinnim* and
  the two Aramaic words are not synonyms at all but three forms of one word. His
  reasoning is that no cognate of the Aramaic word turns up in Ugaritic or
  Phoenician, and that just as the kuf of Imperial Aramaic shifts to a kaf in
  Talmudic Aramaic, the lamed of the Aramaic forms may answer to the nun of
  Hebrew *kinam*, on the phonetic interchange of lamed, mem, nun and reish; so
  KAF-LAMMED-MEM would equal KAF-NUN-MEM, the mem in *kinam* would be a radical
  after all, contrary to the classical lexicographers, and Biblical Hebrew would
  hold two distinct words, *kinnim*, the plural of *kinah*, and *kinam*, cognate
  with the Aramaic. Rabbi Klein consulted Rabbi Dr. Yitzchak (Jared) Greenblatt
  and Rabbi Shaul Goldman, who agreed the supposition is tenable; and Dr.
  Alexander Kohut, in *Aruch HaShalem*, cites the maskilic scholar Peretz
  Smolenskin that Talmudic Aramaic KAF-LAMMED-MEM is cognate with Biblical
  Hebrew KAF-NUN-MEM. Rabbi Klein puts his conclusion conditionally: if the
  supposition is correct, then KAF-NUN-MEM (Biblical Hebrew), KUF-MEM-LAMMED
  (Canaanite, Imperial Aramaic and Arabic), KUF-LAMMED-MEM (Targumic Aramaic and
  Akkadian) and KAF-LAMMED-MEM (Talmudic Aramaic) are one word in different
  forms.

**Whether kein ever means "louse".** Yeshayahu 51:6, "and its inhabitants will
die *kein*", and Bamidbar 13:33, "and so were we *kein* in their eyes".

- Targum Yonasan and the classical reading: "likewise", "so".
- At Yeshayahu 51:6 the seventeenth century Dutch Protestant scholar Ludovicus
  de Dieu first proposed that *kein* possibly means "louse": the earth's
  inhabitants will die like lice. Shadal cites it, noting that Rosenmuller and
  Gesenius followed the same approach, and Malbim cites it too in his commentary
  there; Avraham Even-Shoshan favors it in his concordance of Biblical Hebrew,
  as does Rabbi Dovid Tzvi Hoffmann (to Shemos 8:12).
- At Bamidbar 13:33 it is Malbim who, in light of all that, suggests reading
  *kein* as "louse": the *meraglim* felt themselves grasshoppers in their own
  eyes and as insignificant as lice in the giants'.

## Distinctions

### kinnim (כנים) and kinam (כנם)
Six undisputed occurrences: five in Shemos 8:12 to 14, once in Tehillim 105:31.
Two of the five (Shemos 8:13 to 14) are vocalized *kinam*. The Torah's
consistency in keeping the mem is exactly what prompted the *rishonim* to note
that it is not a radical.

### kinah (כנה)
Not in Tanach. Once in the Mishnah (*Parah* 9:2), and repeatedly in the Gemara
(Shabbos 12b, 107b; Chullin 105b; Eruvin 65a; Chagigah 5a; Bava Metzia 107b;
Zevachim 19a). The Mishnah's instance is a *kinah* of the grain, which is a
field pest rather than a body louse.

### kalmta (קלמתא) and kalmei (כלמי)
Targum Onkelos and Targum pseudo-Yonasan render *kinnim* and *kinam* as *kalmta*
(so too the Targum to Tehillim 105:31), spelled with a kuf in our editions. The
Gemara's form is spelled with a kaf: "from rags come *kalmei*" (Berachos 51b),
where Rashi, following the notion of spontaneous generation, explains *kalmei*
as *kinnim*, the Gemara meaning that worn-out scraps of cloth generate lice, and
adduces this from the Targum's *kalmta*, which he treats as a cognate; and at
Niddah 20b, where Ifra Hormiz, mother of the Sassanian king Shapur II, used to
send Rava questions about menstrual blood, and Rava sent her a lice-comb to hint
that the blood she asked about was the blood of crushed lice. The Aramaic word
has a counterpart in Akkadian *kalmatu*, also lice. *Sefer HeAruch* of Rabbi
Nosson of Rome is itself inconsistent about the kuf and the kaf (see his entries
on the roots ס-ר-ק and כ-ל-ם).

A third form of the word may stand behind both. In 2022 an international group of
archaeologists led by Dr. Daniel Vainstub of Ben-Gurion University reported an
ivory comb bearing a Canaanite inscription reading "May this tooth root out the
lice (*kamal*) of the hair and the beard." Canaanite is closely related to
Biblical Hebrew, so a cognate there is unsurprising; what is surprising is that
the last two consonants are transposed, KUF-MEM-LAMMED rather than
KUF-LAMMED-MEM. Being Canaanite, the text is almost certainly older than the
Targumim, so it seems to Rabbi Klein that KUF-MEM-LAMMED is the original
Semitic spelling and KUF-LAMMED-MEM a later metathesis; the Canaanite form survives in Arabic
*quml*, still lice. The Sefire treaty texts, Aramaic inscriptions found in
northern Syria and dating to the end of the First Temple period, recording a
treaty between two Syrian kings with its oaths and curses, spell the word the
same way, KUF-MEM-LAMMED, in a curse calling for lice to do something to a
violator, though the text breaks off before saying what. Professor Chaim Tawil
questions rendering that word "lice" at all, since in context it is a
crop-consuming pest rather than a creature that stays on the body, and reads it
"vermin of the field," which lines up with the Mishnah's lone *kinah*, the
*kinah* of the grain (*Parah* 9:2). Biblical Hebrew also has a
triliteral ק-מ-ל meaning rotting, molding or withering (Yeshayahu 19:6, 33:9),
and Rabbi Klein thinks it quite possible that the Sefire word is a form of that
root instead.

### parosh (פרעש)
David tells Shaul it is beneath a king to pursue him, comparing himself to a
*parosh* (Shmuel I 24:15, 26:20).

- *Metzudas Tzion* (to 24:15): a black *kinah* that jumps, which in ordinary
  speech is a flea.
- Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, and Radak treat the root as the four-letter פ-ר-ע-ש.
- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 25:6) includes it among four-letter roots that are
  really three-letter roots with a final shin added.

Parosh also occurs in Tanach as a man's name, in lists of people from the start
of the Second Temple period (Ezra 2:3, 8:3, 10:25; Nechemiah 3:25, 7:8, 10:15).
On the possible differences between a *parosh* and a *kinah*, see Tosafos and
*Tosafos HaRosh* to Shabbos 12a.

## Rendering guidance

- The makkah is "lice". Where the Torah writes *kinam*, "the lice" or "the
  vermin" reads better than "a louse"; the form is collective, not singular.
- Trap: English "lice" will take in *kinnim*, *kinah*, *kalmta*, and *parosh*
  together. Keep "flea" for *parosh*; when David's line becomes "a single
  louse" the image of a jumping, uncatchable creature is lost.
- *Kein* in Bamidbar 13:33 stays "so" or "likewise" in the body. If Malbim's
  reading is being taught, give it as his and keep it out of the pasuk itself.
- In a Gemara passage, translate *kalmei* as "lice" and note that it is the
  Targum's word; do not invent a distinct English creature for it.
