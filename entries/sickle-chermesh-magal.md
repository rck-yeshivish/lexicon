# Sickle: chermesh / magal

## Bottom line

Both mean **sickle**, and the weight of the sources treats them as one tool
under two names; the Targumim suggest that *chermesh* is the Hebrew word and *magal*
a Hebraicized form of the Aramaic *magla*. That is why the Torah says *meihachel chermesh bakamah* (Devarim
16:9) while the Mishnah's ceremony of cutting the Omer asks the crowd twice
*magal zeh?* (Menachos 10:3). The proposal that one is a "sickle" and the other a
"scythe" was made by some scholars and rejected by Dr. Shmuel Yeivin.

## Why the Mishnah says magal

The Torah begins Sefiras HaOmer "when the sickle (*chermesh*) commences upon the
standing grain" (Devarim 16:9), yet the Mishnah, describing the public affair of
cutting the Omer, uses a different word for the implement: the harvester holds up
a *magal* and asks the assembled crowd, "Is this a *magal*?" and they answer
"Yes!", and he asks again and they answer again (Menachos 10:3). So why does the
Mishnah call the tool a *magal* where the Torah calls it a *chermesh*? Are they
synonyms, or is there a difference? Rabbi Klein's answer, in short, is that the
evidence suggests they are one tool, *chermesh* the older Hebrew word and *magal* the later one taken in
from Aramaic.

## Distinctions

### chermesh (חרמש)
Twice in Tanach, both in Devarim: the start of the Omer count (16:9) and the
prohibition on a hired worker using a *chermesh* to harvest the owner's produce
for his own eating (23:26). It never appears in the Mishnah, and only once in
Tosefta (Bava Metzia 2:14).

Roots proposed:

- Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 25:6) theorizes that Hebrew words with a seemingly
  four-letter root whose final letter is a shin are really from three-letter
  roots, the added shin being a radical unrelated to the core root. Here the root
  is ח-ר-ם, *cherem*, destroy or ban, and the added shin makes *chermesh* the
  tool used for destroying or cutting. Dr. Shlomo Mandelkorn (1846-1902), in his
  concordance of Biblical Hebrew *Heichal HaKodesh*, also suggests *chermesh*
  derives from *cherem*.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche (1920-2016), the late Chief Rabbi of Netanya:
  the quadriliteral root reflects a merger of two biliterals, ח-ר ("hole") and
  מ-ש ("move"), since the tool cuts, that is, creates a hole in the grain, and
  lets it be moved from where it stood.
- Dr. Shmuel Yeivin (1896-1982), the Israeli archaeologist, further suggests
  that *chermesh* is related to *chalamish*, flintstone, given the
  interchangeability of reish and lammed, and proposes on that basis that
  perhaps the *chermesh* was a cruder harvesting tool made of stone, from the
  earlier Stone Age, while the *magal* was a more advanced version of the same
  tool made of metal, from the Bronze or Iron Age.
- Rabbi Moshe David Valle (1697-1777), the Italian mekubal: a portmanteau of
  *cherem* and *aish*, associating *chermesh* with the realm of din, as against
  *magal*, which he takes as alluding to the realm of rachamim. Alternatively he
  reads *chermesh* as an acronym for *chafetz retzono marbeh sheilaso*, that the
  more a man tries to attain all his wants the needier he ends up.
- Rabbi Chaim Vital (1543-1620) gives an esoteric reading of the cutting of the
  Omer (Devarim 16:9), interpreting it homiletically of a man's lifespan: once he
  reaches twenty and becomes liable to Divine punishment for his sins, he remains
  so for the next fifty years, until seventy, the archetypal lifespan in Tanach.
  In support he notes that *chermesh* has a gematria of 548, the same as
  *umalach hamaves*, "and the Angel of Death"; Rabbi Klein remarks in passing that
  this is reminiscent of the popular depictions of the Grim Reaper with his
  scythe. Rabbi Vital then asserts that the Torah used *chermesh* here rather
  than *magal* precisely to teach that lesson.

### magal (מגל)
Twice in Tanach, both of the harvest tool (Yirmiyahu 50:16, Yoel 4:13), and
several times in the Mishnah (Sheviis 5:6, Menachos 10:3, Keilim 13:1 and 15:4).

That it is the Aramaic form is what the evidence suggests: wherever *chermesh*
appears the Targumim render it *magla*, and the *Sifrei* (to Devarim 23:26)
explains *chermesh* to mean *magal* outright. On that showing the two would be
synonymous, *chermesh* Hebrew in origin and *magal* Aramaic, which accounts for
Devarim, written earlier, using the purely Hebrew term while the later nevi'im
(Yirmiyahu and Yoel) and the Mishnah, from after Aramaic had spread, use the
Aramaic-influenced one. Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) writes that *magal* is a
cognate of the Arabic *almunajil*, sickle, and the Rambam uses the same Arabic
word to define *magal* in his commentary to Peah 4:4 and Sheviis 4:6; on that
reckoning the form should technically be *mangal*, with a nun in the middle,
dropped as often happens. The Rambam to Sheviis 5:6 glosses the Mishnaic *magal
yad* as "a small *chermesh*," again showing that the two words name one thing.

**The sickle-and-scythe proposal, and its rejection.** Dr. Shmuel Yeivin reports
that some scholars have proposed that *chermesh* means a sickle while *magal*
means the larger scythe. Yeivin rejects the proposal on three grounds: there is
no basis in Tanach for such a distinction; archaeologically it is untenable,
there being no evidence that the scythe existed in Biblical times; and in
Mishnaic Hebrew *magal* covered both a sickle and a scythe, the two being told
apart by a modifier on *magal*, the smaller called *magal yad* and the larger
*magal kotzer*. Yeivin concludes instead that *chermesh* and *magal* name the
exact same harvesting tool, *magal* being the later usage, which is why it turns
up in the nevi'im, while Devarim uses the more archaic *chermesh*.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740-1814): the root is ג-ל, whose primary
sense is "round," whence *gal* (a heap of stones in a circular formation),
*galgal* (wheel) and *megillah* (a scroll, which is rolled up); the *magal*
belongs there because the sickle's blade is curved and almost forms a circle. He
traces *galus* and *geulah* to the same two-letter root. Rabbi Klein calls
Pappenheim's own way of connecting those two to the core sense of ג-ל a bit
complex, suggests instead that the connection lies in the cyclical nature of
the exile-redemption-exile pattern that prevails until the final geulah, and
further suggests that Chazal preferred *magal* to *chermesh*
because *magal* fits etymologically with the Pesach theme of the passage from
galus to geulah.

## Rendering guidance

- Devarim 16:9: "from when the sickle is first put to the standing grain."
  Devarim 23:26: "you shall not raise a sickle against your fellow's standing
  grain." One English word for both, since the Sifrei and the Targumim treat
  them as one.
- Menachos 10:3: keep *magal* transliterated inside the ceremony, since the
  crowd is being asked to identify the very implement by name and the ritual
  reads flat as "Is this a sickle? Yes." A gloss on first appearance is enough.
- Trap: English "sickle" and "scythe" invite a distinction the Hebrew does not
  make; Yeivin's three arguments are the reason. If a passage sets *chermesh*
  opposite *magal* it is almost certainly drawing a homiletic contrast (Rabbi
  Valle's din against rachamim, Rabbi Chaim Vital's malach hamaves), not naming
  two tools; render both "sickle" and carry the contrast in the transliteration.
- *Magal yad* is "hand sickle," *magal kotzer* the larger reaping tool; use the
  modifiers, not two different nouns. This is Yeivin's own point about how
  Mishnaic Hebrew handled the two sizes.
