# Exchange: temurah / chalifah

## Bottom line

*Temurah* is **substitution**: putting a second thing in the place of the first,
with the eye on the man who does it (Malbim) and, in several readings, on a
downgrade (Rabbi Shapira-Frankfurter, Wertheimer, Rav Hirsch). *Chalifah* is
**exchange** or **swapping out**: the eye is on the thing being gotten rid of
(Rav Hirsch), and it is the neutral (Wertheimer) or even upgrading
(Shapira-Frankfurter) word. Vayikra 27:10 uses
both of the same prohibited act; Vayikra 27:33, stating the consequence, uses
only *hamer yamir*. *Chalifah* carries a sense of its own.

## Distinctions

### temurah (תמורה)
The Torah's name for the prohibition at the close of Vayikra: transferring the
kedushah of a consecrated animal onto another, with the result that both become
consecrated (Vayikra 27:10, 27:33).

- Rabbi Yisrael Yaakov Algazi: *temurah* is one man swapping one of his own
  animals for another of his own.
- Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter: *temurah* is trading something better
  for something worse. The Torah forbids the transfer either way, better for
  worse or worse for better.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer follows this basic approach, with one slight
  tweak on *chalifah* (below): *temurah* is
  exclusively good-replaced-by-bad, and he works this through a series of
  pesukim.
- Rav Hirsch takes his great-uncle's reading of *temurah* and draws the
  consequence: once the transfer is forbidden, any attempt at it counts as "for
  the worse" even when the second animal is objectively better, which is why the
  pasuk stating the outcome says *hamer yamir* and does not return to *chalifah*.
- Malbim: *temurah* looks at the person who effects the switch and *chalifah* at
  the objects switched, which Rabbi Klein casts as a classic *cheftza*/*gavra*
  construct. The same event can be described either way.
  Hence Tehillim 106:20 on the eigel, *vayamiru es kevodam*: Hashem was
  not in fact replaced, since from His side nothing changed, so the object-word
  *chalifah* would not fit; from the people's side there was an exchange, so a
  *temurah* cognate is used.

Root: Ibn Chayyuj מ-ו-ר or מ-י-ר; Ibn Janach and Radak מ-י-ר; Menachem Ibn Saruk,
a biliteralist, simply מ-ר. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) defines
מ-ר as "getting rid of a thing so that another can take its place," and derives
*mohar* (given in exchange for a woman's maidenhood), *maher* (what does not
stay in one state), *mar* (a taste the palate rejects and wants replaced),
*morah* (the razor that clears the way for new hair), and *amirah* (speech,
where the parties alternate between listening and speaking).

### chalifah (חליפה)
The Torah's second verb in the same prohibition.

- Algazi: *chalifah* is two people swapping animals, a transactional transfer.
- Shapira-Frankfurter: *chalifah* is trading up, worse for better.
- Wertheimer: *chalifah* is neutral, covering both directions.
- Rav Hirsch: *chelef*/*chalifah* is at bottom moving a thing out of one place or
  state into another, whether or not anything takes its place. Yeshayahu 2:18
  uses *yachalof* for idols simply vanishing. The slaughtering knife is a
  *chalaf* because it moves an animal from living to dead. (Others maintain that *chalaf* in the sense of knife comes from the
  Arabic *chalaf*, "sharp," which is itself likely derived from the Hebrew
  *charif*, the r-sound and the l-sound being often interchangeable.) And because
  the word
  fits what people tend to discard and replace, it is the verb for changing clothes
  (Bereishis 35:2, 41:14) and then the name of the outer garments themselves
  (Bereishis 41:14, Shmuel II 12:20); in Modern Hebrew a *chalifah* is a suit.
- Malbim: *chalifah* looks at the objects switched, not the switcher.

Root: most read ח-ל-ף. Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) builds it from two biliterals,
ח-ל (circle, turning) and א-ף (face, present), so *chalifah* is pivoting away
from the present situation. This somewhat echoes Rav Hirsch on what one is getting
rid of, and it runs against what Pappenheim himself writes in *Cheshek Shlomo*,
where *temurah* is "out with the old" and *chalifah* "in with the new";
Pappenheim concedes that in practice the two have become largely interchangeable.

*Chemdas HaChemdah* (to Vayikra 27:10): *lo yachalifenu* forbids letting a
particular consecrated animal be lost with the plan of simply replacing it, and
*lo yamir* forbids swapping something already consecrated to move its holiness
onto another animal, better or worse.

*Toras Gavriel*, by Rabbi Gavriel Wolf Margolios, son-in-law of Rabbi Nachum of
Horodna: *chalaf* is bad for good and *temurah* good for bad.

## Rendering guidance

- Where the two stand together in the parashah of *temurah*, render *temurah*
  "substitute" and *chalifah* "exchange." English "swap" and "change" will
  absorb both without a trace, so keep them out of a passage that turns on the
  pair.
- *Hamer yamir* (Vayikra 27:10, 27:33): "if he shall indeed substitute." Rav
  Hirsch's point rides on the fact that the second pasuk uses only this root, so
  a translation that renders both roots "exchange" destroys the derashah.
- Tehillim 106:20 *vayamiru*: "and they exchanged their glory," but if the
  Malbim's reading is being cited, "and they traded away" or "and they put in
  its place" carries the *gavra* sense better; the point is that the change was
  on their side, not on Hashem's.
- *Chalaf* as the slaughtering knife: leave it transliterated. Translating it
  "knife" loses Rav Hirsch's derivation from moving an animal from one state to
  another, which is the whole reason the word is discussed.
- Trap: "replace" reads naturally for both. Reserve it for *temurah*, where the
  substitute is the point, and never use it for the Yeshayahu 2:18 *yachalof*,
  where nothing takes the idols' place.
