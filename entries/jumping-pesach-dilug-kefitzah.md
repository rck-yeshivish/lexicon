# Jumping: pesach / dilug / kefitzah

## Bottom line

On Rabbi Klein's account, *dilug* is **skipping**: the focus is on what got
passed over. *Kefitzah* is **leaping**: the focus is on covering ground fast.
*Pesach* holds both, and that, he explains, is what Rashi means when he calls it
an expression of *dilug* and *kefitzah* together.

## Distinctions

### dilug (דילוג)
Root ד-ל-ג. On the Vilna Gaon's reading of Yerushalmi Beitzah 5:2, *dilug* is
the skip taken with one foot still on the ground while the other springs the
body forward; Rambam and the Rosh cite the same in their commentaries to Mishnah
Ohalos 8:5. Wertheimer suggests measuring it by height rather than form: in Shir
HaShirim 2:8 *midaleg* is over mountains and *mikapetz* over hills, so *dilug*
is the higher of the two. Rabbi Klein posits, in his own voice, that the
controlling sense is what is skipped, and that *dilug* could therefore more
accurately be called "skipping" and *kefitzah* "jumping": English "skip" carries
both the gait and the evasion ("he skipped the boring parts"), and the two are
in truth conceptually similar, since a skipping step is a way of not treading on
what lies underfoot, and the Hebrew verb covers both. Rabbi Yaakov Lorberbaum of Lisa allows that
*dilug* may in fact be the faster motion, but holds that its focus is still on
what is skipped and not on the speed.

### kefitzah (קפיצה)
Root ק-פ-ץ. The Vilna Gaon: both feet leave the ground. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim:
covering the distance of several steps in one, sometimes off the ground and
sometimes not, the whole point being to arrive faster. One who is *kofetz* is
not avoiding anything underneath him; he is shortening the trip. Hence *kefitzas
haderech*, the miraculous shortening of a journey, and hence the idiomatic use
for anything done in haste and without proper thought, as when the Gemara says
of a man who swore on the spot that he "jumped and swore."

### the subject-based reading, and its rejection
Rabbi Shimshon of Shantz (to Ohalos 8:5): the difference is not the manner of
the jump but who is jumping, *dilug* for an animal and *kofetz* for a person.
Bartenura cites both views and openly accepts Rambam's; Rabbi Yisrael Lipschitz
takes issue with the Rash. Bartenura's counter-cases, which he says seem to
contradict the Rash: a goat that jumps off a roof is *kofetz* (Bava Kama 2:3),
and so is the marten that jumps from the ladder to the dovecote (Bava Basra
2:4), while a man permitted to jump over graves to greet a king is *midaleg*
(Berachos 19b). These suggest that *doleg* is not confined to animals nor
*kofetz* to people, and Rabbi Klein adds that Shir HaShirim 2:8 proves it, since
there both verbs apply to one subject.

### pesach (פסח)
Root פ-ס-ח. Rashi (Shemos 12:11 and 12:13, Yeshayahu 31:5): an expression of
*dilug* and *kefitzah* both. The korban carries the name because it commemorates
Hashem's passing over the Jewish houses at makkas bechoros, and the Yom Tov is
named after the korban. Rabbi Avigdor Nevenzahl, Chief Rabbi Emeritus of the Old
City of Yerushalayim, supplies the *dilug*: Hashem has no body and did not
literally jump; the Torah speaks from the vantage of the aftermath, in which the
Egyptian firstborn died and the Jewish ones did not, so that in hindsight it
seemed *as if* He jumped over them. What was skipped was the general
requirement, that a man first perform some act of commitment sealing his bond
with Hashem before Hashem lets him come close; at the Exodus, though they had
not yet shown that desire, He did miracles for them and took them out anyway.
Rabbi Nachshon Schiller focuses on the haste, the element Rabbi Klein ties to
*kefitzah*: kabbalistic sources assert that
Klal Yisrael had fallen to the forty-ninth level of *tumah*, and another moment
in Egypt would have brought them to the fiftieth, from which there is no
recovering, so the redemption had to be immediate, and the Korban Pesach is
eaten *b'chipazon* (Shemos 12:11) in imitation of that haste.

The same root gives *piseach*, the lame or immobile man. Rabbi Klein draws an
insight of his own from this: two conceptually opposite meanings sharing one
spelling is a common phenomenon in Lashon HaKodesh, and it illustrates that
Hebrew words are not the product of human whim but carry inherent meaning, on a
Divine intuition not found in other languages.

## Rendering guidance

- Shir HaShirim 2:8, מדלג על ההרים מקפץ על הגבעות: "leaping over the mountains,
  bounding over the hills." Two English verbs, and keep the taller obstacle with
  *midaleg*.
- *Pesach* and *pasach*: "pass over," in the makkah, the korban, and the name of
  the Yom Tov alike. "Jump over" loses the skipping sense and imports a body.
- *Kefitzah* in an idiom of haste ("*kafatz v'nishba*"): "he blurted out an
  oath," not "he jumped." The word has gone over to speed and rashness.
- *Kefitzas haderech* stays transliterated with a gloss; "shortening of the way"
  is the sense, but the phrase is a fixed term.
- Trap: English "jump" will swallow *dilug*, *kefitzah* and *pesach* together.
  Assign "skip" to *dilug*, "leap" or "bound" to *kefitzah*, and "pass over" to
  *pesach*, then hold the assignment across the passage.
- Trap: in a *derashah* on Rashi's comment, both components have to survive into
  English, or the whole point (that *Pesach* is the two at once) collapses.
