# Telling: haggadah (maggid) / sippur (misaper)

## Bottom line

*Haggadah* is **telling** in the strong sense: something new, said forcefully,
and, at its core in Rabbi Klein's summary, drawing a past event into the
listener's present. *Sippur* is the more general **relating** of a story, old or
new, and for Rav Moshe Shapiro it carries the sense of many details joined into
one whole. Ramban (to Devarim 26:3) seems to treat the two as synonyms; most
commentators do not. Why the Torah commands *v'higadta l'vincha* while Chazal
call the mitzvah *sippur yetzias Mitzrayim* is answered more than one way: for
Rabbi Klein, when the Torah spoke the story was still new, and by the time of
Chazal it had been handed down for generations; for Rabbi Yechiel Michel
Feinstein, Chazal took the word the Torah uses for the purpose of the miracles
(*l'maan tesaper*, Shemos 10:2), where *v'higadta* only teaches how to tell;
Rabbi Nosson Gestetner concludes that they are two separate mitzvos.

## Distinctions

### haggadah / maggid (הגדה, מגיד)
**Force.** Rashi (to Shemos 19:3), on *v'sageid livnei Yisrael*: Moshe was to
present the matter forcefully to the men, the detailed expectations and the
punishments for failing them, and *tageid* is related to *gid*, sinew, something
strong and durable, not soft. From this Rabbi Klein infers that *haggadah* is
presenting an idea in a strong, assertive way.

**Novelty.** Cognates of *haggadah* attach to information not known before it
was revealed: "who *higid* to you that you are naked" (Bereishis 3:11); Esther
not *magedes* her origins (Esther 2:20); testimony in court (Vayikra 5:1), where
the witnesses bring the court new information. In accordance with these
sources, Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 19:9) writes that *haggadah* is specifically saying
something new that has not yet been told.

Rabbi Klein finds both implications, force and novelty, in the Haggadah's use
of Shemos 13:8 for two of the four sons: one speaks strongly and harshly to the
wicked son, and anything told to the son who does not know how to ask is new.

**Roots.**

- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) also has *haggadah* telling something new, and
  takes the root as נ-ג-ד, *neged*, opposite.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, Rav Hirsch and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg explain
  that one who tells his fellow something new sets it opposite, in front of,
  him, and Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel similarly that the new information *opposes*
  what the listener already knows.
- Menachem Ibn Saruk takes the root as the two-letter ג-ד, which Rabbi
  Pappenheim understands as connection: the telling lets the listener connect to
  the new information.

On either root, Rabbi Klein notes, *haggadah* is linked with new information.

**Drawing out.**

- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*): *aggadah* is an expression of
  pulling or drawing, since aggadic teaching draws in the heart of its audience;
  he adduces Targum to Bereishis 37:28, which renders Yosef's being drawn from
  the pit *negidu*.
- In the same vein, the Netziv writes that *haggadah* applies specifically to
  revealing something by explaining it at great length, a purposely drawn-out
  narrative and not a brief statement.

(Modern Hebrew, Rabbi Klein notes, has unfairly redefined *aggadah* as a
fairytale or fictional legend.)

**Thanks.**

- Rabbi Dovid Abudraham reads *haggadah* as an expression of admission or
  thanks.
- Rabbi Mecklenburg expresses a similar sentiment: the two-letter root *gad* is
  success and fortune, so *lehagid* is praising Hashem in recognizing His role
  in one's success and fortune.

**Relevance.**

- Rabbi Dr. Ari Bergmann, a student of Rav Moshe Shapiro: *haggadah* takes a
  past event and draws it into the present by demonstrating its relevance (see
  also Alshich to Bereishis 24:23), on the model of *hagadas eidus*, testimony
  about a past event shown to bear on the court's deliberations now. In his
  estimation *v'higadta* requires showing how the story remains relevant to us, beyond recounting it.
- Rabbi Aryeh Pomeranchik heard from the Brisker Rav in the name of Rav Chaim
  Soloveitchik that the Pesach-night *v'higadta* differs from the daily mention
  of yetzias Mitzrayim in that it entails explaining the connection between the
  story and the mitzvos of matzah and marror; Rabbi Klein suggests this may be
  derived from the word *v'higadta* itself.

Rabbi Klein's summary: *haggadah* implies new information; its roots convey both
opposition and connection; it implies a drawn-out narrative, meant to spark
dialogue; and at its core it gives a historical narrative contemporary
relevance, whether by thanking Hashem for the benefit of those events or by
tying the narrative to mitzvos in force.

### sippur / misaper (ספור, מספר)
**General.**
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*): *sippur* relates what already happened or was
  already told; others allow it for new information as well. Either way it does
  not *specify* novelty.
- Rabbi Bergmann: *haggadah* shows how a past event is still relevant, *sippur*
  simply relates a past event.
- In that spirit, Malbim: *haggadah* is telling something relevant to the
  listener, *misaper* telling a story not necessarily directly related to him.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer draws the line at how the speaker knows it:
  *haggadah* is relaying what he directly experienced, heard or saw himself,
  while *sippur* is the general word for relating any story, even a dream, that
  he did not physically perceive.

**Wholeness.**

- Rav Moshe Shapiro: everything from ס-פ-ר is interrelated, and *sippur* is
  joining many details into a single whole, the "story." A *sefer* binds its
  contents into one entity; a *sfar*, a border, of a country or a city, closes
  everything within it into one unit; *mispar* and *sofer*, number and counting,
  are the system in which the numbers are bound together in an orderly, logical
  way. Rabbi Klein adds that telling and counting are paired in other languages
  too: in German and Yiddish (*zahlen*, to count), and in English *tell*, *tale*
  and *recount*, and some etymologists take the *tell* of "to tell time" as
  counting.
- Rabbi David Golumb (*Targumna* to Shemos 9:29 and Vayikra 14:9) instead sees
  the core meaning of ס-פ-ר as "circle" or "round," relating the words to the
  Greek *sphere*: *l'saper* and *sippur* describe the way a story gains traction
  as people go around and around telling it; a city on the *sfar*, the border,
  is typically surrounded by a protecting wall; and a *sapar*, barber, cuts the
  hair from one ear to the other in a circular motion.

- Rabbi Pomeranchik also reports from the Brisker Rav in Rav Chaim's name two
  ways the Pesach-night mitzvah differs from the daily mention: it must take a
  question-and-answer form, and it requires the *entire* story, from the
  enslavement through the miracles and the exit to the praise of Hashem. Rabbi
  Klein finds both components in the word *sippur*: structured telling, its
  parts flowing one from the next in one smooth progression like numbers in a
  count.
- *Peirush HaRokeach*: *haggadah* is telling something new, *sippur* is "the
  first time and more." Rabbi Klein suggests he perhaps means a story intended
  to be told many times, one instance in a series, which fits *sippur yetzias
  Mitzrayim*, retold every year.

**Why Chazal chose it.**
- Rabbi Klein's answer: from the Torah's perspective the telling was of
  something new, the story still unfolding, hence *v'higadta*; from Chazal's, it
  had been repeated for generations and was to be passed on, hence *misaper*.
- Rabbi Yechiel Michel Feinstein, a son-in-law of the Brisker Rav, finds the
  precedent in Shemos 10:2, where the purpose of the hardening of Pharaoh's
  heart and the miracles is *l'maan tesaper* to your children, that they may
  know Hashem. Because that is the ultimate purpose, Chazal built the name of
  the mitzvah on it; the Torah's *v'higadta* teaches only *how* to tell, not the
  purpose.
- Rabbi Mecklenburg offers a similar explanation: a hallmark of *sippur* is
  explaining something clearly, and the word is related to *sapir*, the blue
  gem. Hashem's administration of the world is like the clear blue sky,
  sometimes clouded when the wicked obscure His role; spelling it out clearly
  clears the clouds away. According to this, the term *sippur* fits the telling
  of the Exodus, because presenting the whole story shows how Hashem oversees
  all of creation.

**Two mitzvos.** After a lengthy discussion Rabbi Nosson Gestetner concludes that
there are two separate mitzvos at the Seder: *sippur yetzias Mitzrayim*,
verbalizing the story and sequence of events, which binds even one who is
alone and already knows the story; and *v'higadta*, revealing to one's children
ideas or details they did not know.

## Rendering guidance

- *V'higadta l'vincha*: **and you shall tell your son**. Where the passage turns
  on the word, gloss it once: "tell, in the sense of making it new and making
  it his."
- *Sippur yetzias Mitzrayim*: **the telling of the Exodus**, or leave the phrase
  transliterated. *Misaper*: **relate**, **recount**.
- *Aggadah*: **aggadah**, not "legend" or "fairytale"; Rabbi Klein calls the
  modern usage an unfair redefinition.
- Trap: English "tell" absorbs both words, and Rabbi Klein's whole point is
  that the Torah's verb and Chazal's verb differ. Where a passage sets them side
  by side, render *haggadah* **declare** or **set before him** and *sippur*
  **recount**, so the reader can see two acts.
- Second trap: *higid* in Tanach usually carries the news value. "He told him"
  is thin; "he brought him word," "he disclosed," or "he declared" holds it.
