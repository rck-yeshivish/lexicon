# Dough: arisah / batzek / issah

## Bottom line

All three mean **dough**.
Where a passage distinguishes them: *arisah* is dough as a **mixture** just
kneaded together (the Torah's word in the mitzvah of *challah*), *batzek* is
dough already worked into one batch and **rising** (the Torah's word for
ordinary dough), and *issah* is the Mishnaic word, dough as the thing that is
**kneaded and pressed**. But the distinction is not agreed: Ibn Janach and Radak
hold *arisah* and *batzek* to be simply synonymous, and which reading prevails
is left open.

## Distinctions

### arisah (עריסה)
The word Tanach always uses for dough when the mitzvah of *challah* is in view
(Bamidbar 15:20-21, Nechemiah 10:38, Yechezkel 44:30).

- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*): the root ע-ר-שׂ/ע-ר-ס, from which
  *arisah* is ostensibly derived, is an expression of mixing or joining. On that
  basis Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg and Rav Hirsch take *arisah* to be dough
  precisely as a mixture of ingredients kneaded together (see Rashi to Berachos
  37b). Mecklenburg adds *eres*, "bed," to the same root: a mattress rests on
  interlaced planks, a mixture of parts; Rabbi Yachya ben Shalom Kohen of Sanaa
  offers the same idea.
- Rabbeinu Meyuchas bar Eliyahu takes a different line: *arisah* is another word
  for *areivah*, the **kneading trough**, so the word names the surface the
  dough is worked on rather than the dough itself.
- Rabbi Yitzchak Ratzabi suggests that perhaps this is why a betrothed woman is
  called an *arusah*, as one now tied or joined to the man who is going to marry
  her; he notes that although *arusah* is spelled with an *aleph* and not an
  *ayin*, those two letters can sometimes interchange, as seen from the Arabic
  cognate of *arus*, groom, and *arusah*, bride, which is spelled with the
  Arabic equivalent of *ayin*.

### batzek (בצק)
The ordinary word for dough in Tanach, five times (Shemos 12:34, 12:39,
Yirmiyahu 7:18, Hoshea 7:4, Shmuel II 13:8).

- Rabbi Mecklenburg: *batzek* is dough already kneaded and worked into a single
  batch, which has begun to rise or is about to. He notes that in a similar
  sense the root also means **swollen**: the Jews' feet did not swell (*batzek*)
  in the wilderness (Devarim 8:4, and the parallel at Nechemiah 9:21).
- Rabbi Yitzchak Ratzabi picks this up as a further suggestion: *arisah* might
  denote dough whose ingredients have simply been mixed together, while *batzek*
  might refer specifically to dough already kneaded and now beginning to rise or
  swell. He then proposes that if that is so, the halachah should be to take
  *challah* off the dough immediately after kneading, while it can still be
  called *arisah*, before it becomes *batzek*.
- Separately, Rabbi Aharon Marcus theorizes that every three-letter root
  containing *beis* and *kuf*, wherever the third letter falls, turns on
  breaking through (*boker*, *bezek*, *barak*, *bohak*, *bakesh*, *badek*,
  *bokea*, *bakar*), and assumes *batzek* belongs there too: the cracks that
  appear in leavening dough and make it look broken.

### issah (עיסה)
Not in Tanach as "dough" at all; it is the Mishnah's standard word (Challah
1:5, 2:2). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim, Rabbi Mecklenburg and Rabbi Marcus
understand it as a cognate of the *ayin*-*sin*/*samech* root, from
which come "squeeze" (Yechezkel 23:3) and "trample"
(Malachi 3:21), dough being something that must be kneaded and so is in a sense
squeezed and trampled; the same root gives *assis*, juice (Yoel 4:18, Yeshayahu
49:26, Shir HaShirim 8:2), and the Modern Hebrew *issui*, massage, where the
masseuse squeezes and kneads. Pappenheim and Marcus further connect the root to
*asiyah*, making. In short, these scholars hold that *issah* differs slightly
from *arisah* in being a cognate of the verb of kneading, where *arisah* comes
off words of mixing or joining.

Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein offers what Rabbi Klein calls a controversial theory:
that the two words are actually one and the same, *arisah* being *issah* with an
extra *reish*, a letter added to enhance the flowery beauty of the language
without changing the sense, in ways he says we can no longer discern, our long
exiles having dulled us to the nuances of the ancient language. He cites
Pseudo-Rashi to Divrei HaYamim I 18:5, that *Darmesek* and *Damesek* mean the
same thing and so do *sharvit* and *shevet*, and adds a bevy of further cases,
including the Aramaic *kursa*/*kisei*, *markoles*/*makoles* and *zutra*/*zuta*,
and the name of Uzziah king of Yehudah, also called Azariah (see also *Hagahos
Rashash* to Rosh Hashanah 26a).

## Rendering guidance

- Default: **dough** for all three. Reserve a distinguishing rendering for a
  passage that actually turns on the difference.
- Where the contrast is live, render *arisah* **the mixed dough** or **your
  batch of dough** and *batzek* **the risen dough** or **the dough as it
  swells**. Rabbi Ratzabi floats a halachic consequence, conditionally: if the
  distinction holds, *challah* should be separated immediately after kneading,
  while the dough is still *arisah*, before it becomes *batzek*. It is a
  proposal, not a ruling, and should not be quoted as one.
- Trap: English "dough" absorbs all three, and "batch" absorbs *batzek* and
  *issah* both. If a sugya quotes a pasuk with *arisah* and then discusses it
  in Mishnaic *issah*, keep the two English words distinct so the reader can
  see which register each sentence is in.
- *Batzek* outside the bakery is **swollen**, not "doughy": "their feet did not
  swell."
- *Arisah* under Rabbeinu Meyuchas is **kneading trough**, not dough. Flag the
  reading rather than smuggling it into the main text.
