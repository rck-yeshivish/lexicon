# Circumciser: mohel / gozer / rofeh / uman

## Bottom line

*Mohel* is the **circumciser**. *Gozer* is the **cutter**, the older title,
which in a name carries a second sense, halachic decisor. *Rofeh* is a
**physician** who performs the circumcision, *uman* a **practitioner** or
bloodletter who does; neither means "circumciser" as such. Rabbi Yissachar
Tamar's rule sorts them: a man who did only circumcisions was a *mohel*, one who
also bled and healed a *rofeh* or an *uman*.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מוהל | mohel | circumciser | the specialist, circumcision his whole profession (Rabbi Yissachar Tamar) |
| גוזר | gozer | cutter | "cutter"; Onkelos's word for *milah*, and also "decisor" (Rashi; Rabbeinu Chananel) |
| רופא | rofeh | physician | a doctor who circumcises, or the surgeon acting as one (Rashi) |
| אומן | uman | practitioner, bloodletter | a craftsman or bloodletter who circumcises among his trades (Rashi) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Whether mohel comes from the root of milah.** The Biblical verb is *mal*, root
מ-ו-ל, with no hei; *mohel* has a hei that behaves like a root letter, pointing
to מ-ה-ל, which occurs once in Tanach, at Yeshayahu 1:22, "your wine is *mahul*
in water." Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces *mal* and *milah* to the biliteral
מ-ל and *mahul* to the biliteral ה-ל, so the resemblance is accidental and
*mohel* belongs to a Rabbinic stratum of its own. Shadal (to Yeshayahu 1:22):
diluting wine is itself a cutting, since watering it cuts the wine's potency, so
the two are relatives and *mohel* is the agent noun of *milah*. Both agree on
what *mohel* means, so the Bottom line need not choose; for a derashah, Shadal's
reading makes *mohel* a cutter and Pappenheim's does not.

**What rofeh means in the sugya.** Rashi to Menachos 42a and the Mainz
commentary attributed to Rabbeinu Gershom: *rofeh* there is the surgeon serving
as circumciser, so the word does duty for "circumciser." Rashi to Avodah Zarah
26b: it means a physician who also knows how to perform one. Follow the second
where the passage separates the man's trade from the act; it is the more careful
statement.

**Why two thirteenth-century Ashkenazi authorities were called HaGozer.** Rabbi
Yissachar Tamar first suggests continuity, an Eretz Yisrael usage preserved in
early Ashkenaz, then withdraws it: no other medieval source titles a circumciser
*gozer*, and Rabbi Yaakov HaGozer and Rabbi Gershom HaGozer call the
practitioner a *mohel* throughout their own works. His conclusion: *gozer* had
lapsed and was revived for these two as a literary honorific playing on both
senses, since they were circumcisers and also the decisors who codified the laws
of milah.

## Distinctions

### mohel (מוהל)
Not in Tanach or the Mishnah as an agent noun. The Aramaic *mohala* appears in
Shabbos 156a, where one born under Maadim is destined to shed blood but chooses
whether that means bloodletter (*umana*), robber, butcher, or circumciser. The
verb *mohalin* appears in the Mishnah (Shabbos 19:2, 19:5); one Mishnah (19:6)
uses the shorter *mal*, the Biblical form (Devarim 30:6; Yehoshua 5:4, 5:7).

### gozer (גוזר)
Root ג-ז-ר, "cut," and by extension "decree," whence *gezeirah*. Rav Hoshiya
put a question about circumcision to Rabbi Yehudah HaGozer (Shabbos 131b;
Yerushalmi Shabbos 19:1), whom Rashi and Rabbeinu Chananel identify as a
circumciser; the Yerushalmi (Rosh Hashanah 3:9) has Rabbi Yehudah Guzraya.
Targum Onkelos regularly renders words of *milah* with words of *gozer*, and
renders Tzipporah's *vatichros* (Shemos 4:25) as *ugzeras*; some sources call
the mitzvah *mitzvasa digzeirasa*, the commandment of cutting (Koheles Rabbah
3:2). Rabbi David Zacut Modena: he is called *gozer* because the act cuts down
the forces of tumah that hold the uncircumcised. A *posek* is likewise
literally a cutter, which is what lets *gozer* mean decisor.

### rofeh (רופא) and uman (אומן)
Roots ר-פ-א, "heal," and א-מ-נ, "craft." A talmid chacham may not live in a
city that has no *rofeh* and no *uman* (Bava Basra 21a; Sanhedrin 17b); Rashi
there takes *rofeh* as a circumciser for Jewish children and *uman* as a
bloodletter or barber. Where no Jewish circumciser is at hand, the Gemara
weighs an Aramean *rofeh* against a Kusi one (Avodah Zarah 26b; Menachos 42a).
*Umana* is a bloodletter in Taanis 21b but plainly a circumciser in Shabbos
133b, which is why it counts as a fourth term.

## Rendering guidance

- מוהל: "mohel" in a frum register ("circumciser" is clinical), and
  "circumciser" only where the contrast with the other three is the point.
- גוזר: "cutter." Where it is a title (Rabbi Yehudah HaGozer, Rabbi Yaakov
  HaGozer) leave it transliterated and gloss it once; "the Circumciser" throws
  away the decisor sense the honorific was chosen for.
- רופא: "physician"; אומן: "practitioner" or "bloodletter" as the sugya
  requires. Do not render either "mohel."
- Trap: English "circumciser" will absorb all four, and in Bava Basra 21a and
  Avodah Zarah 26b that is exactly the mistake, since the question there is what
  the man's trade was. Keep *rofeh* and *uman* in their ordinary senses and let
  the context supply that he circumcises.
- Where *milah* and *gezeirah* name the same mitzvah in one passage, render the
  second "cutting," not "circumcision"; the Aramaic is making a point.
