# Cheese: gvinah / charitz / shfot

## Bottom line

*Gvinah* is the ordinary word and the one Chazal use: **cheese**. *Charitz*
(*charitzei hechalav*, Shmuel I 17:18) and *shfot* (*shfot bakar*, Shmuel II
17:29) each occur once, and Targum Yonasan renders both as *guvnin d'chalva*,
cheese; but each is disputed, *charitz* possibly meaning unweaned kids or milk
still in the udder, *shfot* possibly the udders themselves or butter. On Rabbi Avraham Maskileison's reading, that dispute is why
Bechoros 6b cites the *charitz* pasuk only as a possible proof.

## Distinctions

### gvinah (גבינה)
Root ג-ב-ן. One occurrence in Tanach: "Did You not pour me out like milk and
curdle me like cheese?" (Iyov 10:10), which Ralbag and others read of Hashem
taking the liquid matter of conception and setting it into a formed human
being. From the Mishnah on it is simply the word for cheese (*Berachos* 6:3,
*Shabbos* 17:2, *Nedarim* 6:5, *Eduyos* 5:2, *Avodah Zarah* 2:4-5, *Chullin*
8:1-3). The word was popular enough in the ancient world to be borrowed into
Akkadian as *gubnatu*, though Assyriologists argue that *gvinah* is originally
Aramaic and not Hebrew. The same root yields *giben* (Vayikra 21:20), a blemish
disqualifying a
kohen: some commentators, Rav Saadia Gaon, Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*), and
Abarbanel among them, say a
hunchback, which is the sense Modern Hebrew took; the Mishnah (*Bechoros* 7:2)
says one whose eyebrows are abnormally
long. Some explain the Midrash on Har Gavnunim as teaching that even a blemish
like *giben* was healed at Har Sinai.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reduces ג-ב-ן to the biliteral ג-ב, a bulge, something
sticking out and plainly seen. Its plainest derivative is *gav*, the back or
outside of a vessel or a hand, and also the eyebrow (Vayikra 14:9), conspicuous
on the face. So *giben* is one whose *gav* (back) is humped or whose *gav*
(brow) is misshapen; *gavnunim* are bulging mountains; *gvinah* is cheese
because it too heaps up. Under the same biliteral he puts *gavoah* (high,
visible from afar), *negev* (dry highland), *geveh* (surface pools, higher than
an underground spring), *govai* (grasshoppers, which haunt the *geveh*), and
*yogvim* (those who trap them).

- Rabbi Aharon Marcus works the same biliteral ג-ב, which he argues comes in
  turn from the monoliteral ג, but defines it as height: *gavoah*; *gibor*, who
  rises above his enemies; *gabachas* (Vayikra 13:41-42), the bald patch at the
  top of the forehead; *givah*, a hill; *elgavish* (Yechezkel 13:11, 13:13,
  38:22), hail, falling from on high. He also brings in the Arabic *jabal*,
  "mountain," from which the Hebrew *gvul*, "border," possibly derives, that too
  being a feature that runs upward. He adds ג-ב-ן here as a hump, without saying
  whether he means the cheese or the blemish.
- Alexander Kohut, in his *Aruch HaShalem*, suggests connecting *gavnunim*
  itself with *jabal*/*gvul* on the interchange of nun and lamed, so that
  *gavnunim* simply means mountainous or hilly.
- Rabbi Avi Kobernick reads ג-ב-ן against its metatheses ג-נ-ב (stealing) and
  נ-ג-ב (drying): in each, something integral is removed from where it belongs,
  the object from its owner, the moisture from the substance, liquid from curds.

Derashos, and the question of why dairy is eaten on Shavuos:
- Rabbi Shimshon of Ostropoli connects it to *Har Gavnunim*, an alternate name
  for Sinai (Tehillim 68:16-17), from *gvinah*.
- *Shemos Rabbah* 2:4, which assumes the name relates somehow to *gvinah* and to
  cheese-making, expounds it on the model of cheese, which draws the purest
  curds off from the dross: of all the mountains Hashem chose the one clean and
  pure of avodah zarah, and Klal Yisrael stood there in their purest state,
  every dross-like blemish healed.
- Rabbi Yosef Nissim Ben-Adahan: *gvinah* has the gematria of seventy, for the
  seventy planes of interpretation in every part of Torah.
- Rabbi Klein adds two further answers: David HaMelech died on Shavuos by
  tradition (Yerushalmi *Chagigah* 2:3, *Rus Rabbah* 3:2), and since most of
  Tanach's cheese occurs in his story, perhaps the custom marks him; or, since
  the heter of dairy is derived from a story about him, we honor his memory on
  the day of his petirah by eating milk foods.

### charitz (חריץ)
Yishai sends David with ten *charitzei hechalav* to the officer (Shmuel I
17:18). Targum Yonasan, Rashi, and *Machberes Menachem* read cheese, and
Bechoros 6b uses the pasuk as one of two proofs that milk and its products are
permitted and are not *eiver min hachai*. On why the root ח-ר-ץ should mean
cheese:
- Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) and Rabbi Avraham ben Shlomo, pieces of fresh
  cheese, whose making somehow involves a shaking that resembles threshing, and
  threshing is *charutz* (Yeshayahu 10:22, 28:27).
- Rabbi Yosef Kara (to Shmuel I 17:18), cheese was often gathered in
  *charitzim*, ditches dug in the ground (Daniel 9:25; *Eruvin* 7:3; *Bava
  Kamma* 5:5; *Mikvaos* 5:6).
- *Metzudas Tzion* (there) suggests that perhaps cheese was cut to fixed
  measures while still being processed, and *charitz* is cutting (Iyov 14:5).
- Rabbi Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Hoil Moshe* to Shmuel II 17:29), *charitz* as
  sharp, a cheese of fermented milk with a tangy flavor.

Against all of these, *Midrash Shmuel* 20:4 takes *charitzei hechalav* not as
cheese but as young kids, either not yet weaned (*Mahari Cohen*, *Eitz Yosef*,
Radal) or just weaned (*Yefeh Nof*), apparently a delicacy. Both further derivations are
put conditionally: Radal writes that *if* the phrase means animals still
nursing, then *charitz* comes from *charutz*, diligent, someone quick to take
them from their mothers while still young and tender; and Rabbi
Shmuel Yaffe-Ashkenazi that *if* it means animals recently weaned, then
*charitz* is cutting, their being cut off from their mother's milk. Ibn
Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) entertains a third possibility, thick coagulated
milk found in the animal's udder. Rabbi Avraham Maskileison uses these to
explain why Bechoros 6b
treats the pasuk as a possible and not a conclusive proof. Halachic aside: Rema
(Yoreh Deah 89:4) records marking the dairy knife; *Pri Megadim* (*Mishbetzos
Zahav* 89:7) finds the hint in *charitz* (a groove) beside *chalav*, that dairy
utensils carry a notch.

### shfot (שפות)
David in flight from Avshalom is brought *shfot bakar* (Shmuel II 17:29), which
Targum Yonasan again renders *guvnin d'chalav*.
- *Midrash Shocher Tov* (to Tehillim 3): cow's cheese so slick flies slid off it; Rabbi Chaim Dov Rabinowitz (*Daas Sofrim* there)
  reads that as heavily preserved cheese fit for a long journey.
- Radak and *Metzudas Tzion* tie the word to ש-ף, rubbing and massaging (Iyov
  33:21; Radak, *Sefer HaShorashim*).
- Rabbi Pappenheim defines the biliteral as movement by rubbing, shifting a
  thing without lifting it, and takes *shfot bakar* as soft, spreadable cheese.
- Rabbi Tedeschi-Ashkenazi (*Hoil Moshe* to Tehillim 127:5, Shmuel II 17:29,
  Yechezkel 40:43) ties it to the homonymous *shfot*, setting a pot by the fire
  (Melachim II 4:38), the shared sense being bringing things closer or joining
  them: pot to fire, the cheesy particles in the milk into one glob; he groups
  *ashpah*, the bin or container for arrows, and *shifshuf* with it, and
  alternatively makes *shfot* the wicker baskets used to filter fermenting milk,
  separating the curds from the whey. Josephus mentions a place in Yerushalayim
  called the Tyropoeon, the Valley of the Cheesemakers, which some scholars have
  identified with Shaar HaAshpah, on the strength of that link between *ashpah*
  and *shfot*.

Dissenting:

- Ibn Janach again offers milk in the udder.
- Radak the udders themselves.
- Rabbi Avraham ben Shlomo butter, which Rabbi Klein finds difficult, since
  *chemah* is already named in the pasuk.

See *Chasam Sofer* 6:22 on why Bechoros 6b did not bring this pasuk as its
proof.

## Rendering guidance

- Render all three "cheese" where the Targum's reading is followed, and say
  which authority you follow when it matters.
- Trap: English "cheese" absorbs *gvinah*, *charitz*, and *shfot*. Where a
  source builds on the difference (Rabbi Pappenheim's spreadable *shfot*
  against heaped *gvinah*; Bechoros 6b's choice of proof text), keep the
  Hebrew.
- *Charitzei hechalav*: "rounds of cheese" reads well, but a footnote must
  carry the *Midrash Shmuel* reading, since "cheese" silently decides a
  machlokes.
- *Giben* is "hunchback" to some commentators and "one with overgrown eyebrows"
  on the Mishnah's reading; do not render it "deformed," which loses both.
- *Gavnunim* in Tehillim 68:16-17: "many-peaked mountain"; keep the *gvinah*
  wordplay for a note, not the rendering.
