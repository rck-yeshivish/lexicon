# Underworld: Gehinnom / sheol / shachat / avadon

## Bottom line

*Gehinnom* is a place name borrowed for the sinner's afterlife. The article
notes that it is commonly translated "hell" or "purgatory," and itself uses
"purgatory" when giving Rashi's derashah on *sheol*; keeping *Gehinnom*
transliterated is this guide's rendering judgment, not the article's. *Sheol*'s
literal meaning is **the grave** (so the article, and so Ibn Ezra maintains),
though by derashah it can refer to the soul's purgatory after death (Rashi).
*Shachat* in many pesukim means simply a **pit**, and Bedersi also reads
"destruction" in it. *Avadon* is either the body's **destruction** after death
or the place where souls are **lost** for a time; the article gives both,
unattributed. Eruvin 19a gives seven Biblical terms for Gehinnom, and Maharsha
reads them as seven different places within it; keeping the seven distinct in
English, rather than "hell" seven times, is our judgment on the strength of
Maharsha.

## Distinctions

### The seven of Eruvin 19a
Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's list: *sheol*, *avadon*, *be'er shachat*, *bor
shaon*, *tit hayaven*, *tzalmaves*, and *eretz hatachtis*. Maharsha (Rabbi
Shmuel Eidels, 1555 to 1631): the seven names correspond to seven different
places within Gehinnom (the article adds "see Sotah 10b"). *Midrash Konen* (in
Rabbi J. D. Eisenstein's *Otzar Midrashim* p. 256) assigns places to different
types of sinners: Korach and his company in
*sheol*; the lost souls of the wicked in *avadon*; robbers, thieves, and those
who hold back a worker's wages in *be'er shachat*; those who transgressed in
matters of intimacy in *tit hayaven*; slanderers in *tzalmaves*; those who
quarrel with talmidei chachamim in *eretz tachtis*; "and so forth," as the
article's quotation ends (*bor shaon* is not assigned in it).

### sheol (שאול)
Close to seventy occurrences. Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis 37:35) holds the literal
sense is grave, and faults the Vulgate for rendering it *infernus* there. Rashi
(there): the plain sense is grave, but exegetically it can refer to the soul's
purgatory after death. Malbim: literally a deep pit from which it is impossible
to get out; this, the article adds, may apply to both the grave and Gehinnom. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: root ש-ל, something cast
away or negated, and death is a plane of existence away from the living. Rabbi
Tzvi Matisyahu Abrahams, whom the columnist calls his friend, takes what the
article calls "a more exhortative approach" (*Root Connections in the Torah* p.
274): the grave is *sheol* because when we are placed in the ground a great
question (*she'eilah*) will hang over us as to where we are headed.

### avadon (אבדון)
Tehillim 88:12. Either the body's ruin and rotting after death, or that souls
are lost (*avad*) there for a time.

### shachat (שחת), be'er shachat (באר שחת)
Tehillim 16:10, 55:24. In many pesukim *shachat* means simply a pit. Rabbi
Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (1230 to 1300): a pit dug to trap wild animals, which
he connects to Gehinnom by noting that the wicked sometimes set traps to ensnare
the righteous; he also explains *shachat* as an expression of *hashchasah*,
destruction, since the body rots in the grave. Rabbi Pappenheim: root ש-ח, bending, since
one stuck in so cramped a place must bend; from the same root *hishtachavah*
(bowing, bending one's posture) and *mashach* (anointing, since oil softens a
hard thing and makes it pliable).

### bor shaon (בור שאון)
Tehillim 40:3, literally "the Pit of Noise." Rabbi Yonah ibn Janach and others
explain that *shaon* (a ruckus of noise) and *sha'anan* (quiet) are related, an
example of the common phenomenon in which Hebrew words of diametrically opposed
meaning sometimes have related roots. In light of this, the column suggests,
*bor shaon* might actually mean the Pit of Silence, where one can no longer
complain or even speak after death; that step is the column's, not Ibn
Janach's. Rashi (to Yeshayahu 9:4) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) give *shaon*
the same root as *shoah*, destruction; this, the column notes, fits *avadon* and
*shachat*, which are also terms of destruction.

### tit hayaven (טיט היון)
Tehillim 40:3 (alongside *bor shaon*), literally "slimy mud." Gehinnom holds a man as quicksand does, and
the dead lie in the grave without moving. Rabbi Pappenheim: *hayaven* from י-ן,
trickery, a root related to *onaah* (profiting by cheating another) and to *yayin*
(wine, which deceives by tasting good and then taking a man's mind); quicksand
deceives likewise, looking like ground a man can walk on. Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 9:20) and Rabbi Aharon Marcus (*Keses HaSofer* to Bereishis 10:2) say
much the same.

### tzalmaves (צלמות)
Tehillim 107:10, Iyov 10:21. "Shadow of death"; the connection needs no
argument.

### eretz hatachtis (ארץ תחתית) or eretz chittis (ארץ חתית)
The Gemara finds no pasuk for *eretz hatachtis* and falls back on a received
tradition. *Sefer Russiana* and Rabbi Yitzchak Abuhab's *Menoras HaMaor* have
*eretz chittis*, the land of the Hittites, and Tosafos prefer that girsa: since
*eretz tachtis* does occur in Tanach (Yechezkel 31 in several places; see also
Devarim 32:22), the Gemara would not have needed a tradition, whereas *eretz
chittis* occurs nowhere, and the sugya then runs properly. The column draws the
consequence: *eretz tachtis* would be a scribal error for *eretz chittis*.

### Variant lists
*Midrash Hallel* (*Otzar Midrashim* p. 134) drops *bor shaon* and *eretz
hatachtis* and adds *gehinnom* and *tziyah* (place of desolation). *Midrash Din HaKever* (p.
94) has *be'er shaon* for *bor shaon*, *bor hatachtis* (the underpit) for *eretz
hatachtis*, and *chatzar maves* (Courtyard of Death) for *tzalmaves*. The Targumic Tosefta (start of Yechezkel)
has *dumah* (literally, quiet) for *bor shaon* (in kabbalistic sources *Dumah* is the angel set
over Gehinnom), *arka* for *eretz hatachtis* (Aramaic for earth, see Yirmiyahu
10:11), and *gehinnom* for *tzalmaves*.

### gehinnom (גיהנום) and tophet (תפת)
Neither explicitly refers to the netherworld in Tanach. *Gei ben Hinnom* and *Tophes* are
places south of Yerushalayim where the Baal was served, child sacrifice
included (Yirmiyahu 19). Both names were then borrowed for the sinner's
afterlife. The Gemara asks why Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi left them out and
answers that they are not names of the place or of quarters within it but
allusions to what lands a man there: *gehinnom*, the valley (*gei*) into which
those who occupy themselves with what is *chinam*, pointless, descend (Rashi:
sexual impropriety); *tophes*, the place into which fall those whom the yetzer
hara talks over (*mefateh*).

### alukah (עלוקה)
Tosafos cite sources reading *alukah*, the leech of Mishlei 30:15, as another
name for Gehinnom, though they also weigh its being a name for Shlomo HaMelech.
Maharal: as a leech draws out blood, Gehinnom draws out the soul. Rabbi Chaim
of Volozhin (*Nefesh HaChaim* 1:12): a leech draws out bad blood and then dies,
and so Gehinnom cleanses a man of his sins until what the sin made is gone.

## Rendering guidance

These are our rendering judgments; the article gives the meanings, not an
English policy.

- *Gehinnom* stays transliterated: it is a place name, and the Gemara's
  derashah on it works off its sound. The article records "hell" and
  "purgatory" as the common renderings without ruling on either, and itself
  uses "purgatory" for Rashi's derashah on *sheol*.
- *Sheol*: "the grave" as the default, on Ibn Ezra. Only where a source is
  reading it as the place of the soul should the English shift, and then say
  so.
- Trap: English "pit" will absorb *shachat*, *be'er shachat*, and *bor shaon*
  at once. In Eruvin 19a and in the *Midrash Konen* list, keep all seven distinct,
  transliterated with a gloss: "*be'er shachat*, the well of the pit," and so
  on. The moment two of them read alike the count of seven stops meaning
  anything.
- *Tzalmaves*: "the shadow of death," the article's literal rendering.
- *Tophes* stays a proper name in Yirmiyahu and transliterated in the Gemara's
  derashah, since the derashah works off the sound of the word.
