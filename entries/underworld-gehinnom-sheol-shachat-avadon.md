# Underworld: Gehinnom / sheol / shachat / avadon

## Bottom line

*Gehinnom* is a place name borrowed for the sinner's afterlife, commonly
translated "hell" or "purgatory." *Sheol* literally means **the grave**, as
Rabbi Klein says and as Ibn Ezra maintains; Rashi explains that by derashah it
can also refer to the soul's purgatory after death. *Shachat* in many pesukim
means simply a **pit**, and Bedersi also reads "destruction" in it. *Avadon* is
either the body's **destruction** after death or the place where souls are
**lost** for a time. Eruvin 19a gives seven Biblical terms for Gehinnom.
Maharsha reads them as seven different places within it, while Rabbi Avraham bar
Chiya holds that *sheol* and *avadon* mean the same thing.

## Where the authorities disagree

**One place or several.**

- Maharsha: the seven Biblical terms of Eruvin 19a are seven different places
  within Gehinnom.
- Rabbi Avraham bar Chiya HaNasi (*Hegyon HaNefesh* p. 3a): *sheol* and *avadon*
  mean the same thing.

## Distinctions

### The seven of Eruvin 19a
Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's list: *sheol*, *avadon*, *be'er shachat*, *bor shaon*,
*tit hayaven*, *tzalmaves*, and *eretz hatachtis*.

- Maharsha (Rabbi Shmuel Eidels, 1555 to 1631): the seven names correspond to
  seven different places within Gehinnom (see Sotah 10b).
- *Midrash Konen* (in Rabbi J. D. Eisenstein's *Otzar Midrashim* p. 256) assigns
  places to different types of sinners: Korach and his company in *sheol*; the
  lost souls of the wicked in *avadon*; robbers, thieves, and those who hold
  back a worker's wages in *be'er shachat*; those who transgressed in matters of
  intimacy in *tit hayaven*; slanderers in *tzalmaves*; those who quarrel with
  talmidei chachamim in *eretz tachtis*; and so forth.

### sheol (שאול)
Close to seventy occurrences.
- Ibn Ezra (to Bereishis 37:35) holds the literal sense is grave, and faults the
  Vulgate for rendering it *infernus* there.
- Rashi (there): the plain sense is grave, but exegetically it can refer to the
  soul's purgatory after death.
- Malbim: literally a deep pit from which it is impossible to get out; this,
  Rabbi Klein adds, may apply to both the grave and Gehinnom.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: root ש-ל, something cast away or negated, and death
  is a plane of existence away from the living.
- Rabbi Tzvi Matisyahu Abrahams, a friend of Rabbi Klein's, takes a more
  exhortative approach (*Root Connections in the Torah* p. 274): the grave is
  *sheol* because when we are placed in the ground a great question
  (*she'eilah*) will hang over us as to where we are headed.

### avadon (אבדון)
Tehillim 88:12. Either the body's ruin and rotting after death, or that souls
are lost (*avad*) there for a time.

Rabbi Avraham bar Chiya HaNasi (*Hegyon HaNefesh* p. 3a): *sheol* and *avadon*
mean the same thing.

### shachat (שחת), be'er shachat (באר שחת)
Tehillim 16:10, 55:24. In many pesukim *shachat* means simply a pit.

- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (1230 to 1300): a pit dug to trap wild animals,
  which he connects to Gehinnom by noting that the wicked sometimes set traps to
  ensnare the righteous; he also explains *shachat* as an expression of
  *hashchasah*, destruction, since the body rots in the grave.
- Rabbi Pappenheim: root ש-ח, bending, since one stuck in so cramped a place
  must bend; from the same root [*hishtachavah*](bowing-kidah-kriah-hishtachavaah.html#%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%AA%D7%97%D7%95%D7%95%D7%99%D7%94) (bowing, bending one's posture)
  and *mashach* (anointing, since oil softens a hard thing and makes it
  pliable).

### bor shaon (בור שאון)
Tehillim 40:3, literally "the Pit of Noise."

- Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach and others explain that *shaon* (a ruckus of noise) and
  [*sha'anan*](peace-shalom-sheket-shalvah.html#%D7%A9%D7%90%D7%A0%D7%A0) (quiet) are related, an example of the common phenomenon in which
  Hebrew words of diametrically opposed meaning sometimes have related roots. In
  light of this, Rabbi Klein suggests, *bor shaon* might actually mean the Pit
  of Silence, where one can no longer complain or even speak after death.
- Rashi (to Yeshayahu 9:4) and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) give *shaon* the same
  root as *shoah*, destruction; this, Rabbi Klein notes, fits *avadon* and
  *shachat*, which are also terms of destruction.

### tit hayaven (טיט היון)
Tehillim 40:3 (alongside *bor shaon*), literally "slimy mud." Gehinnom holds a man as quicksand does, and
the dead lie in the grave without moving. Rabbi Pappenheim: *hayaven* from י-ן,
trickery, a root related to *onaah* (profiting by cheating another) and to [*yayin*](wine-yayin-tirosh-chemer-shechar.html#%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0)
(wine, which deceives by tasting good and then taking a man's mind); quicksand
deceives likewise, looking like ground a man can walk on. Rav Hirsch (to
Bereishis 9:20) and Rabbi Aharon Marcus (*Keses HaSofer* to Bereishis 10:2) say
much the same.

### tzalmaves (צלמות)
Tehillim 107:10, Iyov 10:21. "Shadow of death"; the connection needs no
argument.

### eretz hatachtis (ארץ תחתית) or eretz chittis (ארץ חתית)
The Gemara finds no pasuk for *eretz hatachtis* and falls back on a received
tradition. *Sefer Russiana* and Rabbi Yitzchak Abuhab's *Menoras HaMaor* have
*eretz chittis*, the land of the Hittites, and Tosafos prefer that girsa: since
*eretz tachtis* does occur in Tanach (Yechezkel 31 in several places; see also
Devarim 32:22), the Gemara would not have needed a tradition, whereas *eretz
chittis* occurs nowhere. Rabbi Klein draws the consequence: *eretz tachtis*
would be a scribal error for *eretz chittis*, and on that assumption the whole
sugya makes more sense.

### Variant lists
- *Midrash Hallel* (*Otzar Midrashim* p. 134) drops *bor shaon* and *eretz
  hatachtis* and adds *gehinnom* and [*tziyah*](wilderness-midbar-aravah-tziyah.html#%D7%A6%D7%99%D7%94) (place of desolation).
- *Midrash Din HaKever* (p. 94) has *be'er shaon* for *bor shaon*, *bor
  hatachtis* (the underpit) for *eretz hatachtis*, and *chatzar maves*
  (Courtyard of Death) for *tzalmaves*.
- The Targumic Tosefta (start of Yechezkel) has *dumah* (literally, quiet) for
  *bor shaon* (in kabbalistic sources *Dumah* is the angel set over Gehinnom),
  [*arka*](earth-taivel-arka-cheled.html#%D7%90%D7%A8%D7%A7%D7%90) for *eretz hatachtis* (Aramaic for earth, see Yirmiyahu 10:11), and
  *gehinnom* for *tzalmaves*.

### gehinnom (גיהנום) and tophet (תפת)
Neither explicitly refers to the netherworld in Tanach. *Gei ben Hinnom* and *Tophes* are
places south of Yerushalayim where the Baal was served, child sacrifice
included (Yirmiyahu 19). Both names were then borrowed for the sinner's
afterlife. The Gemara asks why Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi left them out and
answers that they are not names of the place or of quarters within it but
allusions to what lands a man there: *gehinnom*, the valley (*gei*) into which
those who occupy themselves with what is *chinam*, pointless, descend (Rashi:
sexual impropriety); *tophes*, the place into which fall those whom the yetzer
hara talks over (*mefateh*).

### alukah (עלוקה)
- Tosafos cite sources reading *alukah*, the leech of Mishlei 30:15, as another
  name for Gehinnom, though they also weigh its being a name for Shlomo
  HaMelech.
- Maharal: as a leech draws out blood, Gehinnom draws out the soul.
- Rabbi Chaim of Volozhin (*Nefesh HaChaim* 1:12): a leech draws out bad blood
  and then dies, and so Gehinnom cleanses a man of his sins until what the sin
  made is gone.

## Rendering guidance

- *Gehinnom* stays transliterated: it is a place name, and the Gemara's
  derashah on it works off its sound. Rabbi Klein records "hell" and
  "purgatory" as the common renderings without ruling on either, and himself
  uses "purgatory" for Rashi's derashah on *sheol*.
- *Sheol*: "the grave" as the default, on Ibn Ezra. Only where a source is
  reading it as the place of the soul should the English shift, and then say
  so.
- Trap: English "pit" will absorb *shachat*, *be'er shachat*, and *bor shaon*
  at once. In Eruvin 19a and in the *Midrash Konen* list, keep all seven distinct,
  transliterated with a gloss: "*be'er shachat*, the well of the pit," and so
  on. The moment two of them read alike the count of seven stops meaning
  anything.
- *Tzalmaves*: "the shadow of death," Rabbi Klein's literal rendering.
- *Tophes* stays a proper name in Yirmiyahu and transliterated in the Gemara's
  derashah, since the derashah works off the sound of the word.
