# Here: halom / poh / heinah / zeh / koh (kan)

## Bottom line

*Poh* is the ordinary **here**, and for Luzzatto and Wertheimer the precise one:
a specific spot. *Halom* is
directional, **hither**, a step arrived at rather than a place stood in (Rav
Hirsch, as Dr. Lawrence Resnick sums him up), and Chazal read it as marking a
place of royal weight (Zevachim 102a). *Heinah* is **here** as a destination
reached from elsewhere (Luzzatto), and, unlike *poh* and *halom*, also **until
now** (Bedersi). *Zeh* for place is the loose one, a general area, and *koh* is
**thereabouts** (both Luzzatto).

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| הלום | halom | hither, to here | motion up to this point (Rav Hirsch); a place of consequence (Zevachim 102a) |
| פה | poh | here | common and specific, a spot within a place (Luzzatto) |
| הנה | heinah | here (destination) | end point of a journey; also "until now" (Bedersi) |
| זה | zeh | here, this place | a general area, not a spot (Luzzatto) |
| כה | koh | thereabouts | near here, not exactly here (Luzzatto) |
| כאן | kan | here | Mishnaic; absorbs both poh and halom (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What halom carries.**
- Chazal (Zevachim 102a) buttress their reading of the Burning Bush by
  postulating that *halom* always connotes something to do with royalty, so that
  *"al tikrav halom"* (Shemos 3:5) is a refusal of dynastic leadership to Moshe,
  on the strength of David's *"ki haviosani ad halom"* (Shmuel II 7:18).
  Maharsha adds that the exegesis is also supported by the choice of the rare
  *halom* over the common *poh*; *Bereishis Rabbah* 45:1 reads Hagar's *halom*
  (Bereishis 16:13) the same way. Maharsha (to Zevachim 102a) and *HaKtav
  VeHaKabbalah* (to Bereishis 16:13) work through further examples of *halom*
  carrying something royal or otherwise important about the place.
- Luzzatto extrapolates from the Gemara's discussion that *halom* means "here"
  as an important, special place, against the other words in the cluster, which
  can carry a banal or prosaic "here."
- In a similar vein, Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 16:13) pairs ה-ל-ם (breaking or
  beating, as with a hammer) with *paam*: as a *paam* is one beat in the
  continuum of time, *halom* is one step in the continuum of space, marking a
  place one may be marching to as finally "here." Dr. Lawrence Resnick sums up
  Rav Hirsch: *halom* never means "here," it means "hither," a single spatial
  step forward, up to here. As Rabbi Eliezer Cohn has pointed out, Rav Hirsch is
  in fact following his great-uncle Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter
  (*HaRechasim LeVikah* to Bereishis 16:13), who had already written that
  *halom* always means "to here."

**The roots.** *Poh* is spelled peh-hei usually, peh-vav twenty times in
Yechezkel 41-42, peh-aleph once (Iyov 38:11), so Ibn Janach and Rabbi Shlomo Ibn
Parchon give פ-ו, Radak gives פ-ה, and Menachem Ibn Saruk reduces to פ alone;
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim sides with Radak and reads *peh*, a mouth, "here" being
an opening onto elsewhere. For *halom*, Menachem, Ibn Janach and Radak give
ה-ל-ם, while Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim drops the mem and reads ה-ל, "spreading
out," giving *halah* (yonder) against *halom*, a narrow region spreading from
where one stands. Rabbi Yosef Grayever reads ה-ל as "separation," *halom* a place
set apart, which is why it is important.

## Distinctions

### halom (הלום) and poh (פה)
*Halom* has eleven appearances against eighty-two for *poh*: Shemos 3:5;
Bereishis 16:13; Shmuel II 7:18; Shoftim 18:3. Targum renders it *halcha* (Shemos
3:5, Shoftim 18:3), and renders *heinah* the same way (Bereishis 45:5, Yehoshua
2:2, 3:9); the Talmudic cognate is the *heilach* of Bava Metzia 4a, where a man
concedes part of a claim and pays it on the spot, "and *heilach*," and the sugya
turns on whether that counts as a partial admission.

- *Poh* is the default, and Luzzatto, drawing a distinction between a general
  term for a place and a more specific spot within it, has *poh* name the
  specific one: Avraham before the Akeidah, "sit *poh* with the donkey"
  (Bereishis 22:5); "stand *poh* with Me" (Devarim 5:27); the covenant binding
  those *poh* and those not (Devarim 29:14).
- Wertheimer: it fits a person already standing with the speaker; Targum renders
  it *hacha* (Bereishis 19:12, 22:5, Rus 4:1), and *heinah* too (Yehoshua 2:2).

### heinah (הנה)
Yosef: "bring my father down *heinah*" (Bereishis 45:13); the Yevusim to David,
"you shall not come *heinah*" (Shmuel II 5:6); Avraham's offspring promised a
return after the exile, "in the fourth generation they shall return *heinah*"
(Bereishis 15:16); the waters split *heinah v'heinah* (Melachim II 2:14); and
Yirmiyahu's exiles asking the way *heinah*, to Tzion (Yirmiyahu 50:5).
- Luzzatto asserts, with prooftexts, that it is used only where something has
  travelled from elsewhere and arrived, often of somebody *coming* here,
  ostensibly from somewhere else (Yehoshua 2:2; Shoftim 16:2; Shmuel II 5:6;
  Melachim II 8:7; Yechezkel 40:4; Divrei HaYamim I 11:5), while *poh* and *zeh*
  are static and need not imply any contrast with another place.
- Wertheimer: *poh* is the more specific "here" and *heinah* the more general,
  and *heinah* suits a listener not yet arrived.
- Bedersi (a thirteenth century Spanish scholar), in *Chosam Tochnis*: *poh* and
  *halom* are strictly spatial, while *heinah* is often borrowed for time as
  well. Bereishis 15:16 has both senses in one pasuk, the fourth generation
  returning *heinah* because the sin of the Emori is not complete *ad heinah*,
  "until now"; so too Yaakov, "I have not seen him *ad heinah*" (Bereishis
  44:28). Bedersi warns against confusing it with its homonym *heinah*, a
  feminine form of *hein*, "they" (Bereishis 6:2, 21:29; Shmuel II 17:28).

### zeh (זה) and koh (כה)
*Zeh* is "this," occasionally used for place: "stay *zeh* the night" (Bamidbar
22:19), "go up *zeh*" (Bamidbar 13:17), "slaughter *zeh*" (Shmuel I 14:34), on
the spot and not elsewhere. Luzzatto takes *zeh* in this sense as the less
specific place, a general area: "there was no kedeishah *bazeh*" (Bereishis
38:21-22). *Koh*, typically "so" or "thus," occasionally means here: "set them
*koh*" (Bereishis 31:37), "a day's journey *koh*" (Bamidbar 11:31), Bilam "stand
*koh*" by his offerings (Bamidbar 23:15; see also Shmuel II 18:30). Luzzatto claims *koh* is somewhere very close to "here" rather than here itself, seemingly on the
strength of the prefix kaf in the sense of "like/as." Targum often renders *koh*
with *kah*, spelled kaf-aleph. Mishnaic *kan* is understood to be its cognate,
its aleph merely separating it from *kein*, "yes/likewise" (Rabbi Chaim Yehoshua
Kasovsky).

Aramaic *ka*, *haka*, is "here." Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh (*Eim LaMikra* to
Bereishis 38:17) relates it to Latin *hic*, *hac* and Italian *qua*.

## Rendering guidance

- Bamidbar 22:8 against 22:19 is the worked case: Bilam lodges the first
  delegation *poh*, in his own house, and the second, too many and too grand to
  house, *zeh*, somewhere in his town (Luzzatto). Never render both "here."
- Bereishis 15:16 must not read "here" twice: "they shall return here, for the
  sin of the Emori is not complete until now."
- Trap: "here" absorbs the whole cluster; where two stand side by side, or a
  derashah rests on *halom*, mark the difference in the English.
