# Youth: naar (naarah) / elem (almah)

## Bottom line

*Naar* and *naarah* are the wide words: **youth**, **lad**, **young woman**, and
by extension anyone serving as an **attendant**, whatever his age. The strict
age sense (a girl from twelve until six months past maturity) is halachic, not
biblical usage. *Elem* and *almah* are the young person whose growth is still
**hidden**: render **young man** and **maiden**. The one thing not to get wrong
is that *naar* in Tanach is not an age bracket; it is applied to a
three-month-old Moshe and to a fifty-seven-year-old Yehoshua, and in the latter
sort of case it means an attendant.

## Distinctions

### naar / naarah (נער, נערה)
The Gemara (Kesubos 39a) defines a *naarah* as a girl from twelve until six
months after she reaches maturity, which would put a *naar* at thirteen. Rashi
(to Bereishis 25:27) reads *ne'arim* of Yaakov and Esav as thirteen years old,
and the same explains Yishmael being called a *naar* when the angels came to
Avraham (Rashi to Bereishis 18:7; see Bereishis 17:25).

The usage in Tanach will not sit inside that bracket. Rivkah is a *naarah* when
Eliezer chooses her, yet by *Seder Olam* (ch. 1) she was three and by *Sifrei*
(to Devarim 33:21) fourteen. Baby Moshe is a *naar* at three months (Shemos
2:6); Yishmael is one at sixteen (Bereishis 21:12, 21:17-20); Yosef at seventeen
(Bereishis 37:2) and still at thirty (Bereishis 41:12); Binyamin at thirty-one
(Bereishis 44:22, 44:33); Avshalom at twenty-one (Shmuel II 18:32); Rechavam at
forty-one (Divrei HaYamim II 13:7); Yehoshua at fifty-seven (Shemos 33:11).
*Midrash Mishlei* (to Mishlei 1:4) stretches the term to twenty, twenty-five,
even thirty, which still leaves the extremes unaccounted for.

Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi holds the halachic sense and explains the outliers as
resemblance: the Torah calls a person a *naar* when he is *naar*-like in some
respect. Yosef at seventeen busied himself with his hair and his eyes, so his
conduct earned him the word (Rashi to Bereishis 37:2). Rechavam was called a
*naar* at forty-one for being immature and weak in leadership. Yosef at thirty
is called a *naar* by Pharaoh's butler in order to disparage him, to say he was
unworthy of the greatness ahead (Rashi). Binyamin and Avshalom are "boys"
relative to their fathers, whatever their years. Baby Moshe's voice was like a
*naar*'s, or his mother had made him a sort of wedding canopy in the basket
(Sotah 12b). And Yehoshua is called a *naar* in the context of serving Moshe,
since anyone functioning as another's attendant can be called a *naar*
regardless of age (so too Radak to Yehoshua 6:23). Yitzchak at thirty-seven
(Bereishis 22:5) and Yishmael at fifty-one (Bereishis 22:3) fit that same
explanation, both attending Avraham.

Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak of Siedlce reads *naar* from the quality rather than
the years: the young man's drive, which lets him shake off (*l'na'er*) whatever
stands in the way of what he means to do.

Rashi (to Kesubos 44b) notes a spelling rule: *naarah* written deficiently, with
no final hei, can include a girl under twelve; written full with the hei, it
excludes her.

### elem / almah (עלם, עלמה)
Many meforshim relate *elem* and *almah* to *eilum* and *ne'elam*, hidden. Rav
Hirsch (to Bereishis 13:15): an *elem* is a young *naar* not yet developed, his
potential still hidden and unrealized. *Peirush HaRokeach* notes that the boy
who carried messages between David and Yonasan is called a *naar* throughout
(Shmuel I 20:1-42) and an *elem* at exactly the point where their pact had to be
kept hidden from Shaul (20:22).

From that link the meforshim distinguish the pair. *Peirush HaRokeach*: an
*almah* is less outgoing than a *naarah*, so Rivkah may already have passed the
stage of *naarah* and still be called an *almah* because she was hidden away
from people; he adds that the word teaches how sheltered she was, never even
having been propositioned. Rabbeinu Efrayim ben Shimshon (to Bereishis 24:43):
*almah* of Rivkah, and *elem* of David (Shmuel I 17:56), imply one who hides his
words, which is a mark of wisdom; so *naarah* would speak to her age and *almah*
to her mind. Ibn Ezra (to Shir HaShirim 1:3) holds simply that an *almah* is
younger than a *naarah*, which would let Rivkah be an *almah* in years and a
*naarah* in maturity and stature. Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak: *elem* names not
the drive but the sheer strength of that age, and is related to *alim* with an
aleph, the standard Targum for *ometz*/*amitz*.

*Chizkuni* (to Bereishis 24:44) denies any difference of meaning: *naarah* is
Hebrew and *almah* Aramaic. The narrator calls her a *naarah* (24:16) because
the Torah is in Hebrew, and Eliezer says *almah* (24:43) because he thought her
family in Charan knew only Aramaic; *Chizkuni* himself notes that they in fact
spoke Hebrew, since her mother and brother say *naarah* (24:57).

Miriam is called an *almah* at six years old (Shemos 2:8, *Shemos Rabbah* 1:13),
which is young for the word, and the Gemara (Sotah 12b) accordingly explains it
by derashah: she hid the fact that she was Moshe's sister, or she acted with the
strength and vigor of an older young woman.

## Rendering guidance

- *Naar*: **youth** or **lad** in a narrative, **attendant** where the person is
  serving someone (Yehoshua before Moshe, the lads accompanying Avraham). *Naarah*:
  **young woman**, or **girl** where she is plainly a child.
- In halachic context *naarah* is a technical stage, between *ketanah* and
  *bogeres*; leave it transliterated there rather than translating it, since
  "young woman" will not carry the twelve-to-twelve-and-a-half definition.
- *Almah*: **maiden**. *Elem*: **young man**. Where a source is working the
  hidden sense, say so in the note; the English word cannot carry it.
- Trap: "young man" and "girl" will absorb *naar* and *elem*, *naarah* and
  *almah*, without a seam. The Rivkah narrative is the test: she is a *naarah*
  in Bereishis 24:16 and an *almah* in 24:43, and every explanation above turns
  on the two words being visibly different in the English. Give her "young
  woman" in the one and "maiden" in the other, and let a note carry why.
- Do not render *naar* by an age ("a boy of thirteen") in Chumash. The pesukim
  do not support it, and Rabbi Mizrachi's defense of the age sense works by
  resemblance, not by literal age.
