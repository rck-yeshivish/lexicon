# Youth: naar (naarah) / elem (almah)

## Bottom line

*Naar* and *naarah* are the wide words: **youth**, **lad**, **young woman**. The
Gemara's *naarah* is a girl from twelve until six months past maturity (Kesubos
39a), and Rashi reads Yaakov and Esav as *ne'arim* at thirteen (Bereishis
25:27), yet Tanach calls a three-month-old Moshe and a fifty-seven-year-old
Yehoshua a *naar*. *Midrash Mishlei* stretches the age to thirty; Rabbi Klein
infers that the words may not name an age group at all; Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi
keeps the age sense and explains the outliers by resemblance, among them anyone
serving as an **attendant** (so too Radak); Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak reads
it as the tenacious drive of adolescence; and Rabbi Klein relates *naar* to
revealing. *Elem* and *almah*, **young man** and **maiden**, are tied by many
commentators to *ne'elam*, hidden (Rav Hirsch: potential still undeveloped).
Against *naarah*, Ibn Ezra makes *almah* younger, *Peirush HaRokeach* less
outgoing, and Rabbeinu Efrayim wiser, one who hides his words; Rabbi Analak
makes *elem* sheer strength; *Chizkuni* finds no difference of meaning, only
Hebrew against Aramaic. Tanach uses *naar* far outside any age bracket.

## Distinctions

Rabbi Klein starts from Rivkah, whom the Torah calls a *naarah* (Bereishis
24:14, 24:16, 24:28, 24:55, 24:57), a *besulah* (24:16) and an *almah* (24:43).
With *yaldah*, the most common word for a girl, that makes four words, each with
a masculine counterpart (*naar*, *bachur*, *elem*, *yeled*), and he sets out to
show that they are not true synonyms.

### naar / naarah (נער, נערה)
The Gemara (Kesubos 39a) defines a *naarah* as a girl from the age of twelve
until six months after she reaches physical maturity, which would suggest that
a *naar* is specifically a boy of thirteen. Indeed Rashi (to Bereishis 25:27)
explains that when the Torah calls Yaakov and Esav *ne'arim*, they were
thirteen; and this explains why Yishmael is called a *naar* when the angels
visited Avraham (see Rashi to Bereishis 18:7), since he was then thirteen (see
Bereishis 17:25).

Yet it is quite difficult to assign *naar* and *naarah* to an age bracket, since
Tanach uses them of girls who were not twelve and boys who were not thirteen.
Rivkah is a *naarah* when Eliezer chooses her, but none of the commentators says
she was twelve: by *Seder Olam* (ch. 1) she was three, too young for the
definition, and by *Sifrei* (to Devarim 33:21) fourteen, too old. Baby Moshe is a
*naar* at three months (Shemos 2:6). Yishmael is a *naar* at thirteen and again
three years later at sixteen (Bereishis 21:12, 21:17-20). Yosef is one at
seventeen (Bereishis 37:2) and still at thirty (Bereishis 41:12); Binyamin at
thirty-one (Bereishis 44:22, 44:33); Avshalom at twenty-one (Shmuel II 18:32);
Rechavam at forty-one (Divrei HaYamim II 13:7); and Moshe's attendant Yehoshua
at fifty-seven (Shemos 33:11). Possibly because of these questions, *Midrash
Mishlei* (to Mishlei 1:4) extends the term to twenty, twenty-five and even
thirty, which resolves most of them but not baby Moshe, Rechavam or Yehoshua.
Taken together, Rabbi Klein suggests, these passages indicate that *naar* and
*naarah* refer not to a specific age group but to something else.

Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi (1455 to 1526) doubles down on the age sense, a boy
between thirteen and thirteen and a half, but adds that in context the word can
apply to someone outside that bracket who somehow resembles an actual *naar*.
Rashi (to Bereishis 37:2) comments that the seventeen-year-old Yosef engaged in
seemingly childish activities, fixing his hair and tending his eyes; Mizrachi
explains that the Torah was not branding Yosef a *naar* but describing his
behavior as *naar*-like, reason enough for the word though he was older.
Rechavam at forty-one was called a *naar* because he was immature and weak in
leadership, as if he were a young boy. Binyamin and Avshalom will always be
"boys" to their fathers, even in their twenties and thirties. Baby Moshe either
had a voice like that of an actual *naar*, or his mother had enclosed him in the
basket with a sort of miniature wedding canopy, such as an actual *naar* would
have, because she expected to miss his wedding (see Sotah 12b). And Yehoshua is
called a *naar* because the pasuk speaks of his serving Moshe, and anyone who
functions as a servant of others can be called a *naar* regardless of his actual
age (see also Radak to Yehoshua 6:23, who makes this point).

Yosef at thirty (Bereishis 41:12) is a different case, since nothing immature
about him is meant: as Rashi explains, Pharaoh's butler called him a *naar* to
disparage him and imply that he was unworthy of the greatness that awaited him.

Rabbi Klein adds two cases that Mizrachi does not mention: Yitzchak is called a
*naar* at thirty-seven (Bereishis 22:5) and Yishmael at fifty-one (Bereishis
22:3). Both can be accounted for as attending Avraham, essentially following his
lead as a child follows his father.

Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak of Siedlce (1848 to 1907) reads *naar* and *elem* as
two qualities characteristic of adolescence. *Naar* is the young person's
tenacious industriousness, the resolve to shake off (*l'na'er*) anything that
stands in the way of his ambitions.

Rashi (to Kesubos 44b) notes a spelling rule: *naarah* written deficiently,
without the final hei, can include a girl under twelve; written in full with the
hei, it excludes her.

### elem / almah (עלם, עלמה)
Why is Rivkah both a *naarah* and an *almah*? Ibn Ezra (to Shir HaShirim 1:3)
explains that an *almah* is a girl younger than a *naarah*. On that basis Rabbi
Klein suggests that Rivkah's physical age was that of an *almah*, while her
emotional or intellectual maturity, or her spiritual stature, matched that of an
older *naarah*, so that both words fit her; this works best if she was three
when she was chosen for Yitzchak.

According to many commentators, *elem* and *almah* are related to *eilum* and
*ne'elam*, hidden. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 13:15) explains that an *elem* is a
young *naar* who has not yet matured or developed, his potential still hidden
and unrealized. *Peirush HaRokeach* points out that throughout the story of the
secret pact between David and Yonasan, the lad who served as their go-between is
called a *naar* (Shmuel I 20:1-42), but once an *elem* (20:22), in allusion to
their need to keep the pact hidden from Yonasan's father, King Shaul.

From that link the commentators distinguish *almah* from *naarah* in various
ways. *Peirush HaRokeach* explains that *almah* describes a girl less outgoing
than *naarah* would indicate. So, Rabbi Klein suggests, Rivkah may already have
reached the age of *naarah*, and even passed that technical stage (if she was
fourteen), and still be an *almah*, because she was hidden from people.
*Peirush HaRokeach* adds that the word teaches how innocent and sheltered she
was: she had never even been propositioned, something apparently uncommon for a
girl her age at that time and place. Rabbeinu Efrayim ben Shimshon (to
Bereishis 24:43) explains that *almah* said of Rivkah, and *elem* said of David
(Shmuel I 17:56), imply one who hides his words, typically a sign of wisdom; so
*naarah* might describe Rivkah's physical age, while *almah* speaks more to her
intelligence.

For Rabbi Shimon Dov Ber Analak, *elem* names not the adolescent's tenacity but
his sheer power and strength. In that sense of "energetic" it is related to
*alim*, with an aleph, the standard Targum for *ometz* and *amitz*, strong or
resilient.

*Chizkuni* (to Bereishis 24:43) contends that *naarah* and *almah* mean exactly
the same thing, *naarah* being Hebrew and *almah* Aramaic. The narrator calls
her a *naarah* (24:16) because the Torah is written in Hebrew; Eliezer, speaking
with her family, calls her an *almah* (24:43) because he thought that in Charan,
in Aram, they understood only Aramaic. In fact, *Chizkuni* points out, her
family did speak Hebrew, since when her going with Eliezer came up, her brother
and mother called her a *naarah* (24:55).

Miriam too is called an *almah* (Shemos 2:8), when she watched over Moshe at the
Nile and he was saved by Pharaoh's daughter. She was about six (*Shemos Rabbah*
1:13 gives her age as five at Pharaoh's earlier decree to the midwives). That
seems too young for the technical sense of *almah*, where *yaldah* would fit
better, as the Gemara (Sotah 12b) evidently felt in reaching for derashos: she
was called *almah* because she hid the fact that she was Moshe's sister, or
because she acted with the strength and vigor expected of an older young woman.

Rabbi Klein relates *naar* and *naarah*, for their part, to revealing, which
contrasts neatly with *elem* and *almah* as hiding.

## Rendering guidance

- *Naar*: **youth** or **lad** in a narrative, **attendant** where the person is
  serving someone (Yehoshua before Moshe, the lads accompanying Avraham).
  *Naarah*: **young woman**, or **girl** where she is plainly a child.
- In halachic contexts *naarah* is a technical stage, from twelve until six
  months past maturity (Kesubos 39a); "young woman" will not carry that
  definition, so transliterate it or define it.
- *Almah*: **maiden**. *Elem*: **young man**. Where a source is working the
  hidden sense, or Rabbeinu Efrayim's wisdom or Rabbi Analak's strength, say so
  in a note; the English word cannot carry it.
- Trap: "young man" and "girl" will absorb *naar* and *elem*, *naarah* and
  *almah*, without a seam. The Rivkah narrative is the test: she is a *naarah*
  in Bereishis 24:16 and an *almah* in 24:43, and each explanation above,
  *Chizkuni*'s included, turns on the two being different words. Give her
  "young woman" in the one and "maiden" in the other, and let a note carry why.
- Do not supply an age for *naar* ("a boy of thirteen") unless the commentary
  being followed gives one, as Rashi does for Yaakov and Esav (Bereishis 25:27).
  The same word covers a three-month-old and a fifty-seven-year-old, and Rabbi
  Mizrachi, who keeps the age sense, reaches the outliers only by resemblance.
