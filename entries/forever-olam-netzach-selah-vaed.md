# Forever: olam / netzach / selah / va'ed

## Bottom line

Rashi to Shemos 21:6 caps *le'olam* at the fifty-year Yovel cycle, and the
Maharsha builds on that in reading Eruvin 54a. For him *olam* is a very long but
bounded span, **for all time**, and for the Hebrew bondsman "until the Yovel,"
not literally forever. So for the Maharsha, when *olam* stands beside *netzach*,
*selah*, or *va'ed*, the second word is doing work, and those three are the
truly unbounded words: **forever**, **everlasting**, **for all eternity**. That
reading is not the only one. Rashi reads the Gemara simply, that the words mean
the same because they appear together; Ibn Saruk and Rabbi Yosef Kimchi treat
them as flat synonyms, though Kimchi leaves *selah* off his list; and Rabbi
Avraham ben Ezriel of Vienna limits the Gemara's finding to the verses where
these words stand beside *olam*.

## Distinctions

### olam (עולם)
Root ע-ל-ם.

- Rashi to Shemos 21:6: the pierced bondsman serves *le'olam*, meaning only to
  the Yovel, not without limit. Maharsha therefore reads *olam* as a long period
  that nonetheless ends, which is why the pairing in Eruvin 54a is not redundant.
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg ties *olam* to *al*, "above": all of space and
  time is the field on which each creature strives to rise above where it
  stands, and Yovel is called *le'olam* because in it the slave rises to his
  master's free status.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reads *ne'elam*, "hidden," from the same *al*: what is
  above one's range of perception is hidden from him.
- Shadal (to Shemos 15:18): *olam* is a point in time that is unknown,
  *ne'elam*, whether future or past.

### netzach (נצח)
Root נ-צ-ח.

- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: *netzach* is the **continuous**, unchanging
  permanence, a state that never alters.
- Mecklenburg derives it from *tzach*, "pristine, pure," the final state of
  being the soul reaches for.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo* s.v. צח): from *netzach* as
  remaining in one's state comes *nitzuach*, victory, remaining in strength over
  another, as *umenatzchim aleihem* (Divrei HaYamim II 2:1, 2:17). It differs
  from *oz*: *oz* is passive, not being undone by another's force, so two
  opposing sides can both have *oz*; *nitzuach* is active, undoing the other's
  force and bringing it under one's own, so there cannot be two victors.

### selah (סלה)
Root ס-ל-ה.
- Eruvin 54a, followed by Rashi, Meiri and other classical *meforshim* to
  Tehillim 3:3: *selah* means forever, denoting what continues without end. The
  earliest translations of Tanach render it so consistently: Targum Yonasan in
  Aramaic, Aquilas in Greek, and the Peshitta in Syriac. Wertheimer sharpens it
  to the **continual**, the ever-repeating, as against *netzach*'s continuous.
- Radak (to Tehillim 3:3 and in *Sefer HaShorashim*) and Malbim (Tehillim
  3:3, Chavakuk 3:3): *selah* is an interjection closing an idea, and for Radak
  a musical direction to lift the voice at the close; Malbim adds that it can
  mark off a parenthetical remark from the main line. Rabbi Chaim Friedlander:
  it signals that an important idea has just ended and one should pause and take
  it in. Rabbi Aviad Sar-Shalom Basilea faulted Radak for seeming to set aside
  the Gemara's reading; Rabbi Yaakov Emden defended him, that Radak gives the
  *pshat* and the tradition gives the deeper sense.
- Some scholars, cited in the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* (13:1322) and in
  Mandelkern's concordance, derive *selah* from *sal*, a basket, and read it as
  the direction to beat a basket-shaped drum.
- Ibn Ezra: *selah* affirms that what was just said is true, roughly "so it is."
- Mecklenburg himself ties *selah* to *suleh* (Iyov 28:16), which denotes
  something of higher value, and cites one of the earlier commentators who
  apparently wrote that *Selah* is one of the Names of Hashem.

### va'ed / ad (ועד, עד)
Root ע-ד.

- Wertheimer: *ad* literally means "until," and left hanging with no object it
  is an ellipsis, "until... (a time that cannot be named)," so it denotes
  infinite duration.
- Mecklenburg links *va'ed* to *adi'im*, "adornments," the eternal ornaments of
  the soul in the World to Come.

Whether the three words carry "forever" everywhere or only beside *olam* is
disputed:
- Rabbi Avraham ben Ezriel of Vienna limits the Gemara's ruling to verses where
  they stand next to *olam*.
- Maharsha takes it as a general lexical finding.
- Rabbi Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) and Rabbi Yosef Kimchi
  (*Sefer HaGalui*) treat them as flat synonyms; Kimchi leaves *selah* off the
  list.
- Rashi's own account of Eruvin 54a is the simple one: they mean the same
  because they appear together.

## Rendering guidance

- *Le'olam* in a *din*: render by the actual span ("for as long as he lives,"
  "until the Yovel") or write "forever" and gloss it, since Rashi's cap is the
  standard reading. Never let it stand as bare "forever" where the halachah
  turns on the limit.
- The triple in the berachah said just before Shema in the morning, נצח סלה ועד
  ("Your mercy and Your kindness shall not forsake us"): English "forever"
  will swallow all three. Wertheimer's grid gives the way out: "unceasingly,
  continually, for all eternity." All of them treat the words as synonyms; the dispute is over saying them all:
  Rabbi Avraham ben Natan HaYarchi and Abudraham held the phrase redundant and
  would say only *va'ed*; Rabbi Yitzchak Abuhab kept all three, the piling-up
  being the point.
- *Selah* in Tehillim: leave it as *selah*. Choosing "forever" (the Gemara) or a
  pause marker (Radak, Malbim) hides a real *machlokes*, and no English word
  holds both.
- Trap: "eternal" absorbs *olam* and *netzach* alike. Reserve it for *netzach*
  and give *olam* a phrase with time in it ("for all time," "for generations").
