# Staff and tribe: mateh / shevet / mishenes / makel / sharvit / choter

## Bottom line

*Mateh* is a **staff** of office, the stick of a dignitary or a man in authority
(Malbim, Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer), and also a **tribe**, almost
exclusively in Bamidbar and Yehoshua. *Shevet* is a **rod**, wound up with force
to strike (the Vilna Gaon), the instrument of authority (Rashi) and of flogging,
and equally a **tribe**. *Mishenes* is a **cane**, leaned on. *Makel* is a **rod
for striking**, in Tanach generally the shepherd's stick. *Sharvit* is the royal
**scepter**. *Choter* is probably a Hebraized form of Aramaic *chutra*, staff. On
how the two words for tribe differ: citing Shadal, Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi
Mecklenburg holds that *shevet* first named the tribe's leader and *mateh* its
members; Malbim reads *shevet* as a branch, each tribe a branch of Yaakov's
family tree; and Rabbi Zev HaKohen Hoberman calls Yisrael *shevatim* for their
authority and responsibility over the nations and *matos* as Yaakov Avinu's cane,
the support he leans on to carry his legacy. *Mateh* and *shevet* are both stick
and tribe.

## Distinctions

### mateh (מטה)
- Malbim (Yirmiyahu 1:11): the stick a dignitary holds, displaying the honor
  owed to his exalted station.
- Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935), similarly: a stick held by one
  in authority, which is why, of all the people in Tanach who carried a stick,
  only Moshe Rabbeinu's is called a *mateh*, his authority as prophet and king
  together being unparalleled. He relates the word to *mateh*, inclined, after
  the curve at the top by which it is gripped, and holds that a *mateh* (like a
  *makel*) is made only of wood.
- Rabbi Yehuda ibn Kuraish (a ninth-century Spanish grammarian) writes that a
  *mateh* is specifically a stick of dry wood, though rabbinic tradition teaches
  that Moshe's *mateh* was made of blue rock, either sapphire or lapis lazuli.
- On the naming of the tribe, Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785 to 1865),
  citing Shadal, holds that *mateh* first meant the members of a tribe, as
  against its leader, and as a stick the bottom part, *mateh* being seemingly
  related to *lematah*, underneath, as the tribesmen live under their chief.

### shevet (שבט)
- Rashi (Sanhedrin 5a): *shevet* denotes authority and the responsibility that
  comes with it.
- In a legal context the *shevet* or *shotim* is the instrument of flogging for
  those whose offenses deserve it. Wertheimer: such terms first named a stick
  for hitting animals, and their use for hitting people is borrowed.
- The Vilna Gaon (1720 to 1797): *mateh* and *shevet* are both for hitting but
  differ slightly; with a *mateh* one need only raise one's hand a bit for the
  impact to be felt, while with a *shevet* one must wind up the blow with
  energy.
- Wertheimer adds that a *shevet* may be wood or metal, as in the iron *shevet*
  of Tehillim 2:9, whereas *mateh* and *makel* are wood only.
- Malbim (Bereishis 49:28): *shevet* does not literally mean stick but *branch*,
  the commonest thing used as a stick, and so it means tribe because each tribe
  is a branch of the family tree of Yaakov's descendants.
- Mecklenburg, citing Shadal: *shevet* and *mateh* have different meanings but
  became interchangeable because they are conceptually related. *Shevet* first
  named the tribe's leader, like a mast on which a flag is mounted, since the
  tribe rallies around him; it came to cover the tribesmen because the leader
  embodies the tribe. As sticks, both can name the same stick, *shevet* its top
  (as the leader sits atop the tribe) and *mateh* its bottom.

### mishenes (משענת)
A walking stick on which an elderly person can lean (*mishan* means "lean" or
"rely"): a cane.

- Wertheimer: also of wood, and necessarily an especially thick branch, since a
  mere twig cannot support a walker.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim derives *mishan* from the biliteral ש-ע, enjoyment and
  pleasure, *mishan* being the satisfaction one achieves when supported by
  another, and relates the *mishenes* to the same root because elderly people
  who have trouble walking receive much satisfaction from having something to
  lean on.

### makel (מקל)
A rod used for hitting, which in Tanach generally appears with a shepherd who
hits the animals in his charge; probably related to *malkos* / *makkos*,
lashes.

### sharvit (שרביט)
The royal scepter held by the Persian king Achashverosh in Megillas Esther. Some
explain that *shevet* and *sharvit* are the same object, Tanach sometimes adding
an extra *r*-sound, as it generally calls Damascus *Damesek* but sometimes
*Darmesek*.

### choter (חוטר)
Probably a Hebraized form of the Aramaic *chutra*, which is what the Targumim
use for both *mateh* and *shevet*.

## The connotations

Wertheimer in the name of Rabbi Yaakov Lorberbaum of Lissa (1760 to 1832): the
three words carry different connotations. *Makel* is negative, generally tied to
meting out punishment; *mishenes* is positive, supporting the elderly in
walking; *mateh* is neutral. On that basis Wertheimer explains why the staff
with which Moshe worked the wonders in Mitzrayim and at the sea is a *mateh*:
its effects were good for Klal Yisrael and bad for the Egyptians, so the word
that implies neither fits.

Rabbi Zev HaKohen Hoberman (1930 to 2012) uses the synonyms to sum up the dual,
paradoxical role of the tribes of Yisrael: they are *shevatim* because Hashem
granted them certain forms of authority and responsibility over the nations of
the world, and *matos* (which he equates with *mishenes* and *chutra*) because
they serve as Yaakov Avinu's "cane" in death, on whom he "leans" to carry on his
legacy; in that way they are not autonomous but follow their forefather's
tradition.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "staff" or "rod" will absorb *mateh*, *shevet*, and *makel*.
  Keep "staff" for *mateh*, "rod" for *shevet*, and "stick" or "rod for
  striking" for *makel*, and do not let one carry two.
- *Mateh* as "tribe" and *mateh* as "staff" cannot be held together in one
  English word. Where a derashah turns on the double sense, translate the
  operative one and gloss the other in a bracket or a note; do not force
  "branch" to do both jobs.
- *Mateh Moshe*: "the staff of Moshe." On Wertheimer's application of Rabbi
  Yaakov of Lissa's scheme the neutral *mateh* fits wonders that were good for
  Yisrael and bad for Mitzrayim, so avoid English that colors it as an
  instrument of punishment.
- *Shevet* in Tehillim 2:9 (iron) is a rod of rule and of shattering, not a
  tribe; check the material and the verb before choosing.
- *Mishenes* is "cane" or "support," and in a metaphor the leaning is the whole
  point, so keep a verb of leaning in the English.
- *Sharvit* is "scepter" and only that; do not level it to "rod" even where a
  source equates it with *shevet*.
