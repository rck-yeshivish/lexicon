# Morning: shachar / boker (tzafra, kritzuta)

## Bottom line

*Shachar* is **dawn** or **daybreak**, the first light breaking the night before
the sun is up. *Boker* is **morning**, beginning at *haneitz hachamah* and
running on into the day. *Shachar* is a point; *boker* is a stretch, and
*shachar* comes first. In Aramaic, Onkelos renders both as *tzafra*, but in
Iyov, Mishlei and Tehillim the Targum separates them: *boker* is *tzafra* and
*shachar* is *kritzuta*. Both Hebrew roots also carry the sense of seeking out
and discerning, which is why *shachar* and *boker* can each mean "search."

## Distinctions

### boker (בוקר)
Root ב-ק-ר. Malbim and Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer: *boker* runs from sunrise
until four hours into the day, or until midday (see Berachos 27a); it is the
whole morning, not a moment in it. Wertheimer himself pulls back somewhat, since
Pesachim 4a praises Avraham's zeal at the Akeidah by noting that he rose *before*
sunrise, while the pasuk (Bereishis 22:3) says he rose *baboker*.

Ibn Ezra and Radak (both to Bereishis 1:5, and Radak in *Sefer HaShorashim*):
the root ב-ק-ר means to seek out, discern, investigate. Only by daylight can one
tell one thing from another; at night everything runs together, which is why
evening is *erev*, from *eiruv*, "mixture." Hence the family: *bikores*
(inspection), *bikkur cholim* (finding out how the sick man is and what can be
done for him), *bakar* (cattle, which need looking after), and *boker* in the
sense of a herdsman seeking out pasture.

### shachar (שחר)
Root ש-ח-ר. Rabbi Shlomo Alkabetz: not a synonym for *boker* at all, but the
first rays that break through the darkness of night, which is why English says
daybreak. He reads Tehillim 22:1, *ayeles hashachar*, as a comparison to a doe
that springs suddenly and without warning: dawn arrives all at once, whereas
nightfall comes on gradually. (The morning star, Venus, is called *ayeles
hashachar* because it shows before sunrise.)

Two further senses of the root:
1. **Seeking.** Rashi to Tehillim 22:1 gives "morning," then cites Rabbi Menachem
   ibn Saruk that the root's core sense is examining and discerning, as in
   *shocher tov* (Mishlei 11:27), one who seeks out good. Malbim: the searching
   of *shachar* is the persistent, consistent kind, like the sun coming up every
   single morning without fail. Ibn Ezra (Tehillim 63:2) says outright that
   *shachar* and *boker* both mean morning and both mean probing.
2. **Blackness.** Rabbi Wolf Heidenheim: *shachar* is a cognate of *shachor*,
   black, because the light of dawn drives out the night's blackness; Yoma 29a
   likewise treats *shachar* as the *end* of the night. *Peirush HaRokeach*:
   dawn is called *shachar* because the sky is still partly black, especially in
   the west. The Zohar (Beshalach 46a) makes the darkness the point: just before
   dawn the night is at its blackest and the sun's light must cut through it. The
   Vilna Gaon applies it to the exile, darkest immediately before the Final
   Redemption. Rabbi Moshe Shapiro explains the phenomenon: the first ray of
   light washes out the faintest stars while giving no usable light of its own,
   so the sky seems to darken at the very moment morning is announced. Others
   restrict the Zohar to the nights of the month when the moon sets before
   sunrise.

Two side-forms of the root: *shacharus* (Koheles 11:10), one's youth, which
Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) reads as the morning of a life and Rashi as the
years when the hair is still black; and Yeshayahu 8:20, where what is
inexplicable is what has no *shachar*, no light shed on it.

### tzafra (צפרא) and kritzuta (קריצתא)
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur: throughout Chumash, Onkelos renders both *shachar* and
*boker* as *tzafra*; in Iyov, Mishlei and Tehillim (which share their own
cantillation system) the Targum splits them, *boker* as *tzafra* and *shachar* as
*kritzuta*. *Tzafra* is the standard Aramaic for morning in the Gemara; Rashi's
greeting was *tzafra d'mareh tav*.

*Tzafra* and *tzipor* (bird) are linked, but the direction is disputed. Ramban
(Vayikra 14:4): *tzipor* comes from *tzafra*, since birds wake early and chirp.
Rabbi Aharon Marcus: the reverse, *tzafra* from *tzipor*. Rabbi Yaakov Zev Lev
(*Me'at Tzari*) notes that the *Rokeach* himself has it both ways: to Bereishis
1:5 he derives *tzafra* from *tzipor* (dawn is *shachar*, akin to *shachor*, and
black suggests the blackbird; also birds are about early), and to Bereishis 1:20
he derives *tzipor* from *tzafra*, since birds fly up before sunup.

Targum Yonasan (Bereishis 19:15 and 32:27) gives *kritzin* for *shachar*; Targum
Neofiti simply Aramaicizes it to *shachara*. Rabbi Moshe Kosover of Beit
Chilkiyah connects *kritzuta* to *kritzah*, winking (Tehillim 35:19, Mishlei
6:13, Yoma 19b): a wink is a small sign of something larger, and dawn is the
small sign that daylight is coming.

## Rendering guidance

- Bereishis 32:27, כי עלה השחר: "for dawn has broken," not "for morning has
  come." The angel's release turns on first light, before sunrise.
- *Shacharis* stays *Shacharis*. Do not translate the tefillah's name as "morning
  prayers" in a context that is distinguishing *shachar* from *boker*.
- *Boker* in a halachic or narrative pasuk: "morning," and let it hold a span. If
  the passage is about how early someone rose, Wertheimer's own hesitation over
  Bereishis 22:3 is worth a note rather than a confident rendering.
- Trap: English "morning" absorbs both words. Keep "dawn" or "daybreak" for
  *shachar* and "morning" for *boker*; where they stand together, the ascending
  order is dawn, then morning.
- Second trap: the same two roots mean "seek out." *Shocher tov* is not about
  morning, and *bikores* is not about cattle. Let the context, not the root,
  choose; and where a derashah plays on both senses at once (Ibn Ezra to
  Tehillim 63:2), say so in a note rather than forcing one English word to do
  both jobs.
