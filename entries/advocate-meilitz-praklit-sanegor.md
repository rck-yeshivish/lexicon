# Advocate: meilitz / praklit / sanegor (and kategor)

## Bottom line

*Meilitz* is the only native Hebrew word of the three: a **go-between**, in
Bereishis an **interpreter**, and in the phrase *meilitz yosher* a **defending
advocate** who speaks up for a man's uprightness. *Praklit* is the patron or
**character witness** whose presence and testimony to good character help the
defendant, not necessarily by arguing the case. *Sanegor* is the **defense
counsel** who does argue it, the standing antonym of *kategor*, the **accuser**
or **prosecutor**. Do not flatten all three into "defender"; where a source
pairs *sanegor* against *kategor* the point is courtroom advocacy, and where it
says *praklit* the point is who vouches for the man.

## Distinctions

### meilitz (מליץ), meilitz yosher (מליץ יושר)
Rashi (to *Avos* 4:11, *Zevachim* 7b, *Bava Basra* 10a), *Machzor Vitri* (*Avos*
4:11), and the Rambam (to *Avos* 4:11) define *praklit* as *meilitz yosher* or
*meilitz tov*; Rashi (to *Chagigah* 13b) and Rabbeinu Chananel (to *Rosh
Hashanah* 26a, *Kiddushin* 5a) define *sanegor* the same way, so *meilitz yosher*
is the Hebrew term the others are explained by. Its plain sense in Bereishis
42:23 is an interpreter standing between two parties, and more broadly a
middle-man. The full phrase comes from Iyov 33:23-24, where a single *meilitz*
angel out of a thousand tells of a man's *yashro*, his straightness, and he is
spared. Ibn Janach and Radak trace *meilitz* either to מ-ר-צ, *meretz* (fluency,
persistence), by the interchange of lammed and reish, so the word names the
smoothness of the advocate's speech, or to ל-ו-צ (thinking, explaining), so it
names his reasoning. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the core of ל-צ as logical
verbalization, usable for good (*meilitz*) or for ill (*leitzanus*, mockery); he
holds that a prefixed mem reverses a two-letter root, so *leitz* (scorn) becomes
*meilitz* (justification, defense).

### praklit (פרקליט)
A loanword, not Hebrew. It appears in Targum as the Aramaic rendering of
*meilitz* (Targum to Iyov 16:20 and 33:23), and Targum to Iyov 33:23 reads the
pasuk as one *praklit* against a thousand *kategorin*. *Melechet Shlomo* (to
*Avos* 4:11) vowelizes it *paraklet*, and MS Kaufmann *parakilit*; the *Tiferet
Yisrael* (to *Avos* 4:11, *Yachin* 51) connects it to the Greek *parakletos*, one
called from outside to stand by a man. Hence the force of *Shabbos* 32a, that a
man led out to be sentenced is saved if he has great *praklitin*, and these are
teshuvah and good deeds, and of *Bava Basra* 10a, that tzedakah and chessed are a
man's *praklitin*: they testify to his character rather than argue his case. Rabbi
Shimon bar Yochai (*Zevachim* 7b, Tosefta *Parah* 1:1, *Toras Kohanim* to Vayikra
14:20): the chatas precedes the olah because the *praklit* enters first to
appease, and only then is the gift accepted.

### sanegor (סניגור)
Also a loanword: an advocate who argues before the assembly. The exegetical
reading, cited by Rashi (on *Ein Yaakov*, *Chagigah* 13b), *Machzor Vitri* (to
*Avos* 4:11 and in the laws of Rosh Hashanah), Rabbeinu Elyakim (quoted in
*Imrei Noam* to Vayikra 16:4), and *Moshav Zekanim* (to Vayikra 16:4), takes it
as *sani* (to hate) plus *tigra* (fighting), the one who hates strife and quiets
the prosecutor's accusations.

### kategor (קטגור)
The fixed antonym: the accuser. Rabbeinu Chananel (*Rosh Hashanah* 26a,
*Kiddushin* 5a), the Rambam, the Rashbatz, and Rabbi Yaakov bar Shimshon (all to
*Avos* 4:11) understand it as a slanderer, one who informs to get a man into
trouble; on that basis Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) tied it to Latin
*interrogator*, which the column shows does not hold. *Machzor Vitri* reads it as
*ketata* (dispute) plus *tigra* (fighting); others as *magur ketat*, instigator
of dispute (*Sefer Kushyot* 346, *Moshav Zekanim* to Vayikra 16:4), or *kara
tigra*, calling for a fight. Later Hebrew *kitrug* is a metathesis of *kategor*.
The pair is load-bearing in halachah: a cow's horn is invalid for shofar, and the
Kohen Gadol's golden vestments are set aside on Yom Kippur, because "the *kategor*
cannot become a *sanegor*" (*Rosh Hashanah* 36a; Yerushalmi *Rosh Hashanah* 3:2
and *Yoma* 7:3).

### meltzar (מלצר)
Appears twice in Daniel (1:11, 1:16). The Rashbatz (to *Avos* 4:11) explains an
appended reish as forming an agent noun, as in Rabbi Yochanan HaSandler the
shoemaker and Mishnaic *palter*, the professional baker; on that pattern the
*meltzar* is one who does what a *meilitz* does, standing between kitchen and
diners, or between his charge and those who would harm him. Rabbi Yaakov Yehuda
Zilberberg links it instead to sweetness of speech (Tehillim 119:103 with Rav
Hirsch there): the *meilitz* makes his words tasty, the *meltzar* tastes the food.

## Rendering guidance

- *Meilitz yosher* in a mussar or tefillah context: "defending advocate" or
  "advocate for good," never "translator," which belongs only to Bereishis 42:23.
- Where *praklit* and *sanegor* stand in one passage, render *praklit*
  "intercessor" or "character witness" and *sanegor* "defense counsel." English
  "advocate" will otherwise swallow both.
- *Kategor* against *sanegor*: "prosecutor" and "defender," and keep the pairing
  visible, since the derashos on shofar and the golden vestments turn on the one
  becoming the other.
- Trap: "defender" alone absorbs *meilitz*, *praklit*, and *sanegor* at once. If
  the passage lists two of them, or explains one by the other (as Rashi and the
  Rambam do), differentiate in English.
- *Praklitin* of *Shabbos* 32a: "advocates" reads well, but the sense is those
  who vouch for him, so "those who speak for him" is safer where the context is
  teshuvah and maasim tovim rather than a courtroom.
