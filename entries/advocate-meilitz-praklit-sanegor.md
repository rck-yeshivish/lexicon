# Advocate: meilitz / praklit / sanegor (and kategor)

## Bottom line

*Meilitz* is the only native Hebrew word of the three: a **go-between**, in
Bereishis an **interpreter**, and in the phrase *meilitz yosher* a **defending
advocate** who speaks up for a man's uprightness. *Praklit* and *sanegor* are
both Greek loanwords for a defender, and the traditional sources cited below
define each of them by *meilitz yosher*, so the difference between them
comes not from those sources but from what historians of Greek law say the two
offices were: the *praklit* the **patron** or **character witness** whose
standing and vouching help the defendant, the *synegoros* the **defense counsel**
who argues the case before the assembly (and some scholars go as far as saying
the *praklit* helped silently, by showing up). *Kategor* is the **accuser** or
**prosecutor**. The mishnah that anchors the cluster is Rabbi Eliezer ben
Yaakov's, that one who performs a single mitzvah acquires for himself one
*praklit* and one who commits a single transgression acquires one *kategor*
(*Avos* 4:11); in other cases the antonym of *kategor* is *sanegor*. In Modern
Hebrew a *praklit* is a state attorney, who is actually a prosecutor, not a
defender.

## Distinctions

### meilitz (מליץ), meilitz yosher (מליץ יושר)
Rashi (to *Avos* 4:11, *Zevachim* 7b, *Bava Basra* 10a), *Machzor Vitri* (*Avos*
4:11), and the Rambam (to *Avos* 4:11) define *praklit* as *meilitz yosher* or
*meilitz tov*; Rashi (to *Chagigah* 13b) and Rabbeinu Chananel (to *Rosh
Hashanah* 26a, *Kiddushin* 5a) define *sanegor* the same way, so *meilitz
yosher* is the Hebrew term the others are explained by. Its plain sense in
Bereishis 42:23 is an interpreter standing between two parties, and more broadly
a middle-man. The full phrase comes from Iyov 33:23-24, where a single *meilitz*
angel out of a thousand tells of a man's *yashro*, his straightness, and he is
spared. *Meilitz* can be traced either to מ-ל-ץ or to ל-ו-ץ.

- Ibn Janach and Radak relate the first to מ-ר-ץ, *meretz* (fluency,
  persistence), by the interchange of lammed and reish, and on that reading the
  word seems to name the smoothness and consistency the advocate's speech
  requires; they also trace it to ל-ו-ץ (thinking, explaining), so that it names
  his reasoning.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes the core of ל-ץ as logical verbalization, usable
  for good (*meilitz*) or for ill (*leitzanus*, mockery); elsewhere he holds
  that in many three-letter roots a prefixed mem turns the meaning of the
  two-letter core into its exact opposite, the letters standing as they are, so
  *leitz* (scorn) becomes *meilitz* (justification, defense). Modern Hebrew's
  *leitzan*, a clown, comes from *leitz* but has lost the pejorative edge.

### praklit (פרקליט)
A loanword, not Hebrew. It appears in Targum as the Aramaic rendering of
*meilitz* (Targum to Iyov 16:20 and 33:23), and Targum to Iyov 33:23 reads the
pasuk as one *praklit* from among a thousand *kategorin*. *Melechet Shlomo* (to
*Avos* 4:11) vowelizes it *paraklet*, and MS Kaufmann, the oldest vowelized
manuscript of the Mishnah, *parakilit*; the *Tiferet Yisrael* (to *Avos* 4:11,
*Yachin* 51) connects it to the Greek *parakletos* (*paraclete* in Latin).
*Parakletos* is often understood as a Greek legal term meaning variously
supporter, helper, sponsor, advocate and intercessor; the word breaks into
*para*, outside or beside, and *kalein*, to call: one called in from outside
to defend a man. Historians, rather than the traditional sources, explain what
sort of defender he was: not a lawyer for hire but one's patron, who doubled as
standing counsel and interceded in court when needed; some scholars argue he did not speak at all but helped by showing up and
lending moral support, while others explain him as a character witness who
attests to the defendant's virtue without arguing the case at hand. Rabbi Klein
reads *Shabbos* 32a in that light, that a man led out to be sentenced is saved if
he has great *praklitin*, and these are teshuvah and good deeds, and likewise
*Bava Basra* 10a, that tzedakah and chessed are a man's *praklitin*: they testify
to his character rather than argue his case. Rabbi
Shimon bar Yochai (*Zevachim* 7b, Tosefta *Parah* 1:1, *Toras Kohanim* to Vayikra
14:20): the chatas precedes the olah because the *praklit* enters first to
appease, and only then is the gift accepted.

### sanegor (סניגור)
Also a loanword.

- Rabbi Klein notes that it might be more accurately spelled *synagor*, being
  made of the Greek *syn* (together, same), the element in English *synonym*,
  *syntax*, *synagogue* and *synthesis*, and *agor* (gathering place, assembly),
  which he says is possibly related to the Hebrew *agur*, gathering. The
  *synegoros* in Ancient Greece was a legal expert who argued before the
  assembly on a defendant's behalf.
- From an exegetical perspective many sources take it as a contraction of *sani*
  (to despise, hate) plus *tigra* (fighting, argument), the one who hates strife
  and quiets the prosecutor's accusations; this explanation is cited by Rashi
  (on *Ein Yaakov*, *Chagigah* 13b), *Machzor Vitri* (to *Avos* 4:11 and in the
  laws of Rosh Hashanah), Rabbeinu Elyakim (quoted in *Imrei Noam* to Vayikra
  16:4), and *Moshav Zekanim* (to Vayikra 16:4).

### kategor (קטגור)
The accuser, and also from Greek: *kata* (down, reverse, against) plus *agora*
(assembly), so one who speaks against another before an assembly. *Encyclopedia
Britannica* explains that although *kategoria* originally meant an accusation,
Aristotle (384-322 BCE) already used it in the philosophical sense of an
assertion, and that the English *category*, a class or group into which one can
assert that an item belongs, descends from it.

- Many commentators, among them Rabbeinu Chananel (*Rosh Hashanah* 26a,
  *Kiddushin* 5a), the Rambam, the Rashbatz, and Rabbi Yaakov bar Shimshon (the
  last three to *Avos* 4:11), understand *kategor* as a slanderer, one who
  informs to get a man into trouble; on that basis Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur
  (1469-1549) in *Sefer HaTishbi* claims it is related to the Latin
  *interrogator*, which Rabbi Klein says does not quite pan out etymologically,
  *interrogator* being *inter* (between) plus *rogare* (to ask), which has
  nothing to do with the origins of *kategor*.
- *Machzor Vitri* reads it as a portmanteau of *ketata* (dispute) plus *tigra*
  (fighting); others as *magur ketat*, instigator of dispute (*Sefer Kushyot*
  346, *Moshav Zekanim* to Vayikra 16:4), or *kara tigra*, calling for a fight.

The later Hebrew *kitrug*, accusation, seems to be a metathesis of *kategor*,
the reish and gimmel switching places. The pair is load-bearing in halachah: a
cow's horn is invalid for shofar, since the bovine recalls the Golden Calf,
which draws Heavenly accusation, and likewise the Kohen Gadol's golden vestments
are set aside for the Yom Kippur service, because "the *kategor* cannot become a
*sanegor*" (the shofar in *Rosh Hashanah* 36a and Yerushalmi *Rosh Hashanah*
3:2, the vestments in *Rosh Hashanah* 36a and Yerushalmi *Yoma* 7:3).

### meltzar (מלצר)
Appears twice in Daniel (1:11, 1:16), where it means a guard or a waiter.
- Rabbi Klein suggests it might come from the same core root as *meilitz*, with
  a reish appended as a suffix doing the work of the English *-er*, forming an
  agent noun from a verb or another noun, as a *baker* is one who *bakes* and a
  *butler* one who deals with *bottles*. There is some precedent for that
  pattern in the Rashbatz, Rabbi Shimon ben Tzemach Duran (1361-1444), to *Avos*
  4:11, who explains the appended reish in Rabbi Yochanan HaSandler as marking
  his trade of shoemaker (a *sandal* being a shoe in Greek and Mishnaic Hebrew
  alike), and who offers as a second example the Mishnaic *palter*, professional
  baker (*Demai* 5:4, *Avodah Zarah* 4:9), from the Greek *plateia*, open
  street, since such a baker would typically sell his goods on the street
  (*plateia* is the ancestor of English *place* and *plaza*). On that pattern
  the *meltzar* is one who does what a *meilitz* does, standing between kitchen
  and diners, or between his charge and those who would harm him.
- Rabbi Yaakov Yehuda Zilberberg (1914-2003) links it instead to sweetness of
  speech (Tehillim 119:103 with Rav Hirsch there): the *meilitz* makes his words
  tasty, the *meltzar* tastes the food.
- Dr. Chaim Tawil takes *meltzar* to be unrelated to *meilitz* altogether, a
  borrowing from the Akkadian *massaru*/*mansaru*, with the nun and lammed
  interchanging.

(The Ashkenazic surname Meltzer is unrelated to the Biblical *meltzar*: it comes
from the German and English *malt*, for the maltster who prepares grain for
brewing.)

## Rendering guidance

- *Meilitz yosher*: "defending advocate" or "advocate for good," never
  "translator," which belongs only to Bereishis 42:23.
- Where *praklit* and *sanegor* stand in one passage, render *praklit*
  "intercessor" or "character witness" and *sanegor* "defense counsel." English
  "advocate" will otherwise swallow both.
- *Kategor* against *sanegor*: "prosecutor" and "defender," and keep the pairing
  visible, since the derashos on shofar and the golden vestments turn on the one
  becoming the other.
- Trap: "defender" alone absorbs *meilitz*, *praklit*, and *sanegor* at once. If
  the passage lists two of them, or explains one by the other (as Rashi and the
  Rambam do), differentiate in English.
- *Praklitin* of *Shabbos* 32a: "advocates" reads well, but the sense is those
  who vouch for him, so "those who speak for him" is safer where the context is
  teshuvah and maasim tovim rather than a courtroom.
