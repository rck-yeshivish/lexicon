# Basket: tene / sal / keluv

## Bottom line

*Sal* is the ordinary **basket**, and for Rabbi Pappenheim a **bread basket**.
*Tene* is the **fruit basket** of bikkurim, which for him was typically woven
loosely so the air could move through it. *Keluv* holds something alive or ripening, so a **basket** for figs and a **cage** for birds; *dud* is a basket or a pot by context, and *kalkalah* is the
large Mishnaic **hamper**.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What the root ס-ל means.**
- Rav Hirsch (to Bamidbar 19:20; Tehillim 3:3): *sal* is literally "to lift," a
  basket being what helps a man lift and carry, and he places *sulam*, *solelah*
  (a siege mound) and *mesilah* (a road that climbs) in the same ס-ל family.
  Rabbi Aharon Marcus's theory of *sela* fits this: he proposed that in every
  word whose root is the biliteral ס-ל the samech stands in for an ayin that
  follows it, so the root is really ע-ל, what is above (*al*, *lemalah*) or
  ascends (*oleh*, *aliyah*), and *sela* is a rock that comes up out of the
  ground.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reads ס-ל as repeated action instead: *mesilah* is a
  road because many have trodden it, and one who twists his hair over and over
  is *misalsel* (Rosh Hashanah 26b), so *sal* is a bread basket, an item in
  constant daily use (Bereishis 40:16, Vayikra 8:2). Pappenheim himself admits
  that *sal* can mean any basket in a borrowed sense, even a basket of meat
  (Shoftim 6:19) or grapes (Yirmiyahu 6:9).
- Against all of this, Dr. Chaim Tawil points out that the Hebrew *sal* seems in
  fact to derive from the Akkadian *sallu*, which likewise means basket.

**Where tene comes from.**
- Rabbi Pappenheim: the aleph is not root, the root being ט-נ, and the word
  names a basket typically woven with gaps so that air reaches the fruit and
  keeps it from spoiling; from the same root he takes *eitun* (Mishlei 7:16),
  cloth woven loosely enough to breathe, and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg adds
  that the ט-נ in *shaatnez* likewise carries weaving.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus keeps the link to *eitun* but holds both are of Egyptian
  origin, and Rabbi Ernest Klein likewise treats *tene* as an Egyptian loanword.
- Rabbi David Chaim Chelouche connects it instead to *beten*, a stomach, a
  basket being an open cavity.

The origin remains open.

## Distinctions

### sal (סל)
Fifteen appearances, the default word for a basket. It carries the bread of the
royal baker's dream (Bereishis 40:16) and the bread of the miluim (Vayikra 8:2),
which stands behind Pappenheim's reading, but also Gidon's meat (Shoftim 6:19)
and grapes (Yirmiyahu 6:9). The Aramaic *sala* is what the Targum uses for
*tene* in all four places.

### tene (טנא)
Four appearances, all in Ki Savo (Devarim 26:2, 26:4, 28:5, 28:17), about
bikkurim and about the blessing and curse on one's produce; in the language of
Chazal it appears as *teni*. That it is reserved to that parashah marks it as a non-standard word for a
basket, though the Targum renders it by *sal*.

### keluv (כלוב), dud (דוד)
*Keluv* twice: a basket of *kayitz*, summer figs, in Amos 8:1, and a basket in
which birds were kept to be fattened in Yirmiyahu 5:27, from which Modern Hebrew
made *keluv* a cage. *Dud* means a basket in Yirmiyahu 24:1-2 but a pot in
Shmuel I 2:14 and Divrei HaYamim II 35:13, the vessels being much the same shape.

### the Mishnaic and Talmudic words
*Kalkalah* is frequent in the Mishnah (Peah 7:3; Demai 7:6; Terumos 4:6; Maasros 1:5, 4:2; Shabbos 20:3, 21:1; Eruvin
3:8; Kiddushin 2:7; Keilim
16:2, 22:9). Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi: an especially large *sal* people would
typically use to store all
kinds of food, its name a cognate of *kalkal*, to sustain (Bereishis 47:12),
because its contents generally provide sustenance and nourishment.
*Kefifah*, sometimes spelled with a kuf and sometimes with a kaf, seems to mean
specifically a wicker basket (Shabbos 2:2; Sotah 2:1, 3:1; Keilim 26:1).

Eight more appear in the Gemara. How they differ is not readily apparent and no
commentator addresses it, so Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein reads them from context clues:

- *dikula* (Chullin 32b) seems to be a basket made from the bast of a *dekel*, a
  palm tree (see Rashi to Shabbos 90b).
- *gridia*, a vegetable basket (Rashi to Sotah 10a).
- *traskal*, a wide basket typically filled with barley and hung at an animal's
  neck so it can eat more easily (Rashi to Shabbos 5a, 53a and Eruvin 33b).
- *tzana* (Rashi to Chullin 57a; Rashbam to Bava Basra
  126b), which Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein explains as a basket made out of thorns,
  related to the biblical *tzan* (Bamidbar 33:55, Mishlei 22:5, Amos 4:2).
- *kelet*, a vase-shaped basket women used to wear on their heads (Kesubos 72b,
  82b; Gittin 77a; Bava Basra 85b; Bava Metzia 9b).
- *sharkafa*, which seems to be a basket in which birds were placed (Chullin
  53b, with Rashi and Tosafos there).
- *tuvila*, apparently a basket used for harvesting dates (Rashi and Rabbeinu
  Gershom to Bava Basra 33b).
- *tirina* (Pesachim 88a), which seems to have been a special basket for dates.

## Rendering guidance

- טנא in Ki Savo: "basket," and where the passage turns on the word, "fruit
  basket." Do not write "wicker basket," which belongs to *kefifah*.
- Trap: English "basket" absorbs *sal*, *tene*, *keluv*, *dud* and *kalkalah*.
  If two stand in one passage, give *sal* "basket," *tene* "fruit basket,"
  *kalkalah* "hamper," and let *keluv* and *dud* take their contents.
- כלוב: "basket" in Amos 8:1, "cage" in Yirmiyahu 5:27; do not carry the modern
  "cage" back into the figs. דוד: "basket" for the figs of Yirmiyahu 24, "pot"
  for Shmuel I 2:14.
- The Talmudic words are best transliterated with a gloss on first use.
