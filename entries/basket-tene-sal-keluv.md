# Basket: tene / sal / keluv

## Bottom line

*Sal* is the ordinary **basket**, and for Rabbi Pappenheim a **bread basket**.
*Tene* is the **fruit basket** of bikkurim, loosely woven so the air can move
through it. *Keluv* holds something alive or ripening, so "basket" for figs and
"cage" for birds; *dud* is a basket or a pot by context, and *kalkalah* is the
large Mishnaic **hamper**. The trap: English "basket" covers all of them.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| טנא | tene | fruit basket | loosely woven so fruit will not spoil (Pappenheim); only in Ki Savo |
| סל | sal | basket | the standard word; bread basket in daily use (Pappenheim); "lifting" (Rav Hirsch) |
| כלוב | keluv | basket, cage | unripe figs (Amos 8:1) or birds being fattened (Yirmiyahu 5:27) |
| כלכלה | kalkalah | hamper | a large sal for storing food, from kalkal, "to sustain" (Rabbi Tanchum) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What the root ס-ל means.** Rav Hirsch (to Bamidbar 19:20; Tehillim 3:3): *sal*
is literally "to lift," a basket being what helps a man lift and carry, and he
places *sulam*, *solelah* (a siege mound) and *mesilah* (a road that climbs) in
the same ס-ל family. Rabbi Aharon Marcus's rule fits this, that in every ס-ל
word the samech stands in for an ayin, so the root is really ע-ל, what is above
or goes up, as in *sela*, a rock that comes up out of the ground. Rabbi Shlomo
Pappenheim reads ס-ל as repeated action instead: *mesilah* is a road because
many have trodden it, and one who twists his hair over and over is *misalsel*
(Rosh Hashanah 26b), so *sal* is a bread basket, an item in daily use (Bereishis
40:16). The Bottom line takes "basket" plainly, since Pappenheim himself concedes
*sal* for a basket of meat (Shoftim 6:19) or grapes (Yirmiyahu 6:9).

**Where tene comes from.** Rabbi Pappenheim: the aleph is not root, the root
being ט-נ, and the word names a basket woven with gaps so that air reaches the
fruit and keeps it from spoiling; from the same root he takes *eitun* (Mishlei
7:16), cloth woven loosely enough to breathe, and Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg
adds that the ט-נ in *shaatnez* likewise carries weaving. Rabbi Aharon Marcus
keeps the link to *eitun* but holds both are of Egyptian origin, and Rabbi
Ernest Klein likewise treats *tene* as an Egyptian loanword; Rabbi David Chaim
Chelouche connects it instead to *beten*, a stomach, a basket being an open
cavity. The Bottom line follows Pappenheim on the sense and leaves origin open.

## Distinctions

### sal (סל)
Fifteen appearances, the default word for a basket. It carries the bread of the
royal baker's dream (Bereishis 40:16) and the bread of the miluim (Vayikra 8:2),
which stands behind Pappenheim's reading, but also Gidon's meat (Shoftim 6:19)
and grapes (Yirmiyahu 6:9). The Aramaic *sala* is what the Targum uses for
*tene* in all four places.

### tene (טנא)
Four appearances, all in Ki Savo (Devarim 26:2, 26:4, 28:5, 28:17), about
bikkurim and about the blessing and curse on one's produce; in the language of
Chazal it appears as *teni*. That it is reserved to that parashah, and that the
Targum reaches for *sal* to render it, is what makes it worth marking as a
different kind of basket rather than a stylistic variation.

### keluv (כלוב), dud (דוד)
*Keluv* twice: a basket of *kayitz*, summer figs, in Amos 8:1, and a basket in
which birds were kept to be fattened in Yirmiyahu 5:27, from which Modern Hebrew
made *keluv* a cage. *Dud* means a basket in Yirmiyahu 24:1-2 but a pot in
Shmuel I 2:14 and Divrei HaYamim II 35:13, the vessels being much the same shape.

### the Mishnaic and Talmudic words
*Kalkalah* is frequent in the Mishnah (Peah 7:3; Demai 7:6, which the article
prints as *Dema*; Terumos 4:6; Maasros 1:5; Shabbos 20:3; Kiddushin 2:7; Keilim
16:2). Rabbi Tanchum HaYerushalmi: an especially large *sal* for storing every
kind of food, its name a cognate of *kalkal*, to sustain (Bereishis 47:12).
*Kefifah*, with a kuf or a kaf, is a wicker basket (Shabbos 2:2; Keilim 26:1).
Eight more appear in the Gemara, distinguished by context and not by any
commentator: *dikula*, of palm bast (Chullin 32b); *gridia*, for vegetables
(Rashi to Sotah 10a); *traskal*, the wide barley basket hung at an animal's neck
(Rashi to Shabbos 5a); *tzana* (Rashi to Chullin 57a; Rashbam to Bava Basra
126b), which Rabbi Ernest Klein takes as a basket of thorns, from *tzan*;
*kelet*, the vase-shaped basket women carried on their heads (Kesubos 72b);
*sharkafa*, for birds; *tuvila* and *tirina*, for dates (Pesachim 88a).

## Rendering guidance

- טנא in Ki Savo: "basket," and where the passage turns on the word, "fruit
  basket." Do not write "wicker basket," which belongs to *kefifah*.
- Trap: English "basket" absorbs *sal*, *tene*, *keluv*, *dud* and *kalkalah*.
  If two stand in one passage, give *sal* "basket," *tene* "fruit basket,"
  *kalkalah* "hamper," and let *keluv* and *dud* take their contents.
- כלוב: "basket" in Amos 8:1, "cage" in Yirmiyahu 5:27; do not carry the modern
  "cage" back into the figs. דוד: "basket" for the figs of Yirmiyahu 24, "pot"
  for Shmuel I 2:14.
- The Talmudic words are best transliterated with a gloss on first use.
