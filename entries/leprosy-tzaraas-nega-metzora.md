# Leprosy: tzaraas / nega / tzarua / metzora

## Bottom line

*Tzaraas* is the **affliction itself**, the impurity that has taken hold;
*nega* is the **spot**, the point of contact where it strikes, and where the
Torah pairs them, *nega tzaraas*, it is naming two things and not one. *Tzarua*
and *metzora* both mean **leper**, but *tzarua* is the man while the affliction
is on him and *metzora* still fits him until the purification is complete
(Perlmutter). Do not render *tzaraas* "leprosy" as a medical identification; the
affliction has been rendered leprosy, Hansen's disease, epilepsy, mold and mere
skin discoloration, and Rav Hirsch holds that an affliction that is merely
pathological is by definition not a *nega* at all.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| צרעת | tzaraas | leprosy, the affliction | the impurity clinging to the sinner (Arizal); "disease" generally (Ibn Ezra to Vayikra 13:19) |
| נגע | nega | spot, plague-spot | the meeting point of the man and what strikes him (Pappenheim); the Divine blow that exposes the impurity (Arizal) |
| צרוע | tzarua | leper, while afflicted | used only of one actually bearing the affliction (Perlmutter); a single spot (Shadal) |

| צרעה | tzirah | hornet, pestilence | sent against the Canaanites; a malady rather than an insect (Ibn Ezra), "destruction, pestilence" (Ibn Janach) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Are tzaraas and nega synonyms?** The Arizal (*Shaar HaMitzvos*, *Tazria*) says
firmly not. *Tzaraas* is a spirit of impurity that clings to a man because of a
spiritual defect, a real contamination that then shows itself on the body. *Nega*
is the opposite in origin: a holy force, a Divine judgment from the side of
purity, whose work is to drive the *tzaraas* out by forcing it to the surface
where it can be dealt with. On this reading *nega tzaraas* (Vayikra 13:2-3, 13:9,
13:20, 13:25, 13:27, 13:47, 13:49, 13:59, 14:32, 14:34) names both the impurity
and the mechanism that exposes it, and each word appears alone where only one of
the two is in view. The Bottom line follows this.

**What tzirah is.** Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 23:28, Devarim 7:20): not a hornet but a
disease, of a piece with *tzaraas*. Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) traces both
to צ-ר-ע and defines *tzirah* as destruction and pestilence. Menachem Ibn Saruk
lists the two as separate categories under one root and entertains no thematic
or semantic connection between them. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*)
keeps the standard "hornet" and surmises that the two are connected by content:
a hornet's sting is venomous and can prove lethal, as *tzaraas* can be poisonous
and detrimental to a man's health. Rabbi Yehuda Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei
Yehudah*) reads *tzirah* instead as a fusion of *tzar*, enemy, and *ra*, bad,
swarms of wasps or bees being a peculiarly formidable enemy in that they cannot
be fought with sword and spear.

**Why the Torah alternates tzarua and metzora.** Four answers:

1. **Homiletic.** *Peirush HaRokeach* (to Vayikra 13:45), as the editors of
   *Tosafos HaShaleim* read him: *metzora* is *tzarua* plus an initial *mem*,
   whose *gematria* forty points to the fortieth day of Moshe's ascent and the
   sin of the Golden Calf, for which one punishment was *tzaraas* (*Vayikra
   Rabbah* 17:3). The article notes the weakness: it does not explain which word
   goes where.
2. **The source of the derashah.** Rabbi Menachem Deutsch, quoted by Rabbi
   Shlomo Tzvi Shick (*Torah Shleimah*, *Metzora* 1): the very choice of
   *metzora* over *tzarua* is what prompted Chazal to expound *metzora* as
   *motzi shem ra* (*Erachin* 15b).
3. **Stage of the affliction.** Rabbi Eliezer Perlmutter (*Even Moshe*):
   *tzarua* is only for one still actively afflicted, while *metzora* covers a
   man whose spot has healed but who has not yet finished the purification, since
   he remains on the cusp of the affliction returning.
4. **Number of spots.** Shadal (to Vayikra 13:5): *tzarua* is one who has only a
   single *nega*, and *metzora* is one who... The published text breaks off
   mid-word there, so what Shadal said of *metzora* is not recoverable from the
   article; the article itself introduces the distinction as hard to
   substantiate.

The Bottom line follows Perlmutter. That is our choice, not a ranking the
article makes, though the article does say of the first answer that it fails to
account for where each word is used, and introduces the fourth as hard to
substantiate.

## Distinctions

### tzaraas (צרעת) and the root צ-ר-ע
The noun and its inflections appear thirty-five times in Tanach, most of them in
Vayikra 13-14, plus Devarim 24:8 (one may not evade the consequences by cutting
the spot away), Naaman in Melachim II 5:3, 5:6-7, 5:27, and Uziyahu, struck on
the forehead for offering the *ketores*, in Divrei HaYamim II 26:19. Gesenius
and other philologists, the column reports, hold the Hebrew root related to the
Arabic *sara'a*, being thrown down to the ground, which is where a possible
connection to epilepsy comes from; the column takes that literal sense to
suggest, in its own words, that Hashem throws a man down and humbles him by
afflicting him with *tzaraas*. Rav Hirsch
(to Vayikra 13:2) links צ-ר-ע to ז-ר-ע (*zorea*, sowing) and ז-ר-ה (*zoreh*,
winnowing or throwing), by the interchange of tzadi with zayin and ayin with hei:
a seed hidden underground bursts out visibly, and so a spot on the skin is the
outward showing of a rot rooted within. Rabbi Moshe David Valle (*Kise Nachon*
to Melachim I 11:26) parses *tzaraas* as *eis*, time, with *tzar*, constriction.
Rabbi Avi Kobernick reads the root against its metathesised neighbours: against
ע-צ-ר (*aritz* and *ne'eratz*, powerful, awesome and esteemed, but also, in
*ta'arotz*, broken and subdued, the two senses reconciled on his reading because
a man who views his enemies as powerful is thereby broken in his own eyes),
*tzaraas* is its opposite, since sickness is bodily weakness; and with צ-ע-ר
(*tzaar*, pain),
since the affliction is a burden borne, which fits *Toras Kohanim* to Vayikra
13:2, that the sufferer is pained by it and so are those who see him; the column
adds that Chazal's use of *tzaar* there may hint that they too linked the words.
Mr. Moshe Ossi (*Al Katzeh HaLashon*) suggests a connection to שׂ-ר-ע,
expansion, the root of *sarua*, the
overextended limb that disqualifies a Kohen or a korban (Vayikra 21:18, 22:23)
and of *meihistarea* in Yeshayahu 28:20, since *tzaraas* spreads.

Two names seem to come from the root: the city of Tzarah in Dan's territory,
named eleven times in Tanach, and Tzeruah, mother of Yeravam ben Nevat (Melachim
I 11:26). Alfred Jones, *The Proper Names of the Old Testament Scriptures
Expounded and Illustrated* (London, 1856), p. 376, notes that her name is
etymologically related to *tzaraas* but does not explain why she should bear so
odd a name. *Midrash Tadshe* (*Otzar Midrashim* p. 481) does: her name alludes
to her son's bringing a spirit of *tzaraas* on the nation and turning their
hearts from Hashem. Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Ferber of London, explaining that midrash:
Yeravam put a rebellious spirit in the air that induced his generation to sin
with him. Rabbi Moshe David Valle speculates that perhaps she was literally
afflicted and passed her impurity to him. Pseudo-Rashi (to Divrei HaYamim II
4:17): she was a widow, and when her husband died he spread impurity through the
tent to all in it, as a *metzora* is a conduit of impurity.

### nega (נגע)
From נ-ג-ע (Ibn Janach, Radak) or the two-letter ג-ע (Ibn Saruk). The root
carries touching (*nogea*), reaching (*hagaah*), and striking (*naga*). Rabbi
Shlomo Pappenheim takes contact as the core: the meeting point of two things;
reaching is bringing that contact about, and striking is contact of the harmful
kind, whether from a man's hand or from a malady sent upon him. A *nega* is thus
the point where the man and the affliction meet. Rav Hirsch (to Vayikra 13:2)
makes the Divine element definitional: a leprous disease that is merely
pathological, and not plainly an affliction from Above, falls outside the
definition of a *nega*. He also (to Bereishis 20:5) pairs נ-ג-ע with נ-ג-ח, ayin
interchanging with ches: *negichah*, goring, is a violent impact, *negiah* can be
the lightest touch, and a *nega* is the nexus between a man and a Divine message
meant to rouse him to teshuvah. The chassidishe adage on *nega* and *oneg*, the
same letters reordered, turns on where one puts the *ayin*, that is, on how the
eye sees what has come.

### tzarua (צרוע) and metzora (מצורע)
Both are declensions of צ-ר-ע. *Tzarua* appears five times (Vayikra 5:2, 13:44-45,
14:3; Bamidbar 5:2), for instance where a leper may not eat *terumah* (Vayikra
22:4) or must be sent from the camp; *metzora* appears fifteen, including the
opening of the purification laws (Vayikra 14:2), Naaman (Melachim II 5:1),
Geichazi (5:27), and Uziyahu (Melachim II 15:5, Divrei HaYamim II 26:21-23). The
feminine *metzoraas* is used of Moshe's hand (Shemos 4:6) and of Miriam
(Bamidbar 12:10). For the four accounts of the alternation, see above. On the
sins that bring *tzaraas*, Chazal list idolatry, immorality, murder, chillul
Hashem, blasphemy, theft, misusing public property, haughtiness, *lashon hara*,
stinginess, false testimony, a judge who perverts the law, swearing in vain,
entering another's domain, evil thoughts, and causing strife between brothers
(*Vayikra Rabbah* 17:3, *Erachin* 15b, *Zohar Balak* 206a, *Tanchuma Metzora* 4).

## Rendering guidance

- *Tzaraas* is best left transliterated in a Torah context. "Leprosy" imports a
  modern diagnosis the sources do not make, and on Rav Hirsch's reading it names
  precisely the thing a *nega* is not.
- *Nega*: "spot" where the surface mark is meant, "affliction" where the blow is
  meant. Plural *negaim*; the masechta is *Negaim*.
- Trap: English "affliction" will absorb both *tzaraas* and *nega*, and the
  Arizal's whole distinction dies with it. In the doubled phrase *nega tzaraas*,
  translate two words, "a spot of *tzaraas*," never one.
- Second trap: "leper" absorbs *tzarua* and *metzora* alike. Where a passage
  turns on the man's stage (afflicted, healed, purified), mark the difference,
  for instance "one bearing the affliction" against "one under the laws of the
  *metzora*."
- *Tzirah* keeps "hornet" in a plain rendering of Shemos 23:28, Devarim 7:20,
  Yehoshua 24:12, with a note where Ibn Ezra's "malady" reading is being used.
