# Priest: kohen / komer

## Bottom line

*Kohen* is the general word: a **priest** of any cult, and in Tanach it is used
of Poti Phera, Yisro, and the priest of Michah's idol no less than of Aharon's
sons. *Komer* is specific: an **idolatrous priest** (Radak, Ibn Kaspi). So in a
halachic or Mikdash context render *kohen* as **Kohen** and keep it a proper
term; where Tanach uses it of a gentile functionary, "priest" is right and
carries no slur. Never render *komer* as plain "priest," and never render
*kohen* as "idolatrous priest" on your own; the Targum and Rashi decide that
case by case.

## Distinctions

### kohen (כהן)
Used of idolatrous and foreign functionaries throughout: Poti Phera *kohen On*
(Bereishis 41:50), Yisro *kohen Midyan* (Shemos 2:16, 3:1), the Egyptian
priests exempt from Yosef's levy (Bereishis 47:22), and the man appointed over
Michah's idol (Shoftim 18:4). But Targum Onkelos does not treat these alike: at
Poti Phera and Yisro he renders *kohen* as *rabbah*, master, which is political
standing rather than ritual office, while at the Egyptian priests and Michah's
priest he renders *komer*. Rashi likewise calls the Egyptian *kohanim*
*komrim*, and gives the rule: *kohen* means a religious functionary, except
where it is attached to a place name (*kohen Midyan*), where it means a
position of leadership in that place.

On the root, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim gives the biliteral כ-ן, base or basis,
which also yields *ken* (so, yes) and *nachon* (correct), both of which affirm
a thing by acknowledging its basis, and *tochnis* (plan) and *hachanah*
(preparation), the bases of any serious undertaking. A *kohen*, then, is the
man charged with seeing that the preparations for the worship at a temple, of
whatever kind, are in order. Rav Hirsch ties *kohen* to *hachanah* as well: the
Kohen models how Hashem is to be served and so prepares others for that
service. Rabbi Pappenheim adds secondary senses: one who wears distinctive
garments, as a *chasan* does (Yeshayahu 61:10), and one who holds official
standing, as a prince does (Shmuel II 8:18). Rabbi Aharon Marcus proposes the
root *koh*, "thus," the prophets' word when they relay Hashem's message, which
suits the fact that kohanim taught Torah as well as served in the Mikdash (see
Malachi 2:7).

### komer (כומר)
Radak (Tzefaniah 1:4, Melachim II 23:5, and *Sefer HaShorashim*): *komer* means
blackened (compare Eichah 5:10), so a *komer* is a priest in black vestments,
against the Kohen, whose garments in the Mikdash were white. Rabbi Eliyahu
HaBachur (*Sefer HaTishbi*) reads it as closed, clustered, cloistered, citing
the Targum to Tehillim 77:10 and the Talmudic *komer shel anavim*, a clustered
mass of grapes (Bechoros 31a); on his reading a *komer* is specifically a
secluded, cloistered priest, a monk, while *kohen* is the general word.

Rav Hirsch reads *komer* from *nichmeru rachamav*, of Yosef's mercy welling up
when he saw Binyamin (Bereishis 43:30), together with *michmar*, a net
(Yeshayahu 19:8, 51:20). The idolatrous priest catches his followers in the net
of his cult by working on their imagination and their feelings, where the Torah
addresses a man's understanding; so the *komer* feeds on his followers'
emotions while the Kohen feeds them Torah.

### the disputed pesukim
Yoshiyahu's purge: he removed the *komrim* from their idolatrous posts and
barred those *kohanim*, Aharon's descendants, who had served idolatry from
serving in Yerushalayim (Melachim II 23:5-9, with Radak).

Tzefaniah 1:4, where both words stand together, has three readings. Targum and
Rashi: *komrim* are any who worship idols and *kohanim* are the idolatrous
priests, which reverses the usual division. Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi: *komrim* are
the priests of Baal, *kohanim* are Hashem's kohanim, some of whom had gone
corrupt. Radak, in three places: both words mean idolatrous priests. Ibn Kaspi
and Radak both leave the general rule standing, that *kohen* covers a priest of
any sort while *komer* is the idolater's.

## Rendering guidance

- Aharon's descendants: **Kohen**, capitalized and untranslated, in any frum
  context. "Priest" for them reads Christian and flattens the halachic term.
- *Kohen* of a gentile: **priest**, or "chieftain" where Onkelos reads *rabbah*
  (Yisro, Poti Phera). Say which you followed if the passage is being darshened.
- *Komer*: **idolatrous priest**, or "priest of the idol." Where Rabbi Eliyahu
  HaBachur's reading is at issue, "cloistered priest" or "monk."
- Trap: English "priest" absorbs both words. In Tzefaniah 1:4, where both stand
  in one pasuk, one English word for both destroys the pasuk; pick a reading,
  render the two differently, and footnote the other two views.
- Trap: do not import "idolatrous" into *kohen* where the Hebrew has not said
  it. Rashi and the Targum supply that in specific places only, and where the
  word is bound to a place name it may not mean a priest at all.
