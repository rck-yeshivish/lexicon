# Priest: kohen / komer

## Bottom line

*Kohen* is the general word: a **priest** of any cult, and in Tanach it is used
of Poti Phera, Yisro, and the priest of Michah's idol no less than of Aharon's
sons. *Komer* is specific: an **idolatrous priest** (Radak, Ibn Kaspi). So in a
halachic or Mikdash context render *kohen* as **Kohen** and keep it a proper
term; where Tanach uses it of a gentile functionary, "priest" is right and
carries no slur. As a rule do not render *komer* as plain "priest," and never
render *kohen* as "idolatrous priest" on your own; the Targum and Rashi decide
that case by case. The rule does not settle Tzefaniah 1:4, where the article
leaves four readings standing, including Targum and Rashi reversing the usual
division and Dr. Chaim Tawil making the two words synonyms from two languages.

## Distinctions

### kohen (כהן)
Used of idolatrous and foreign functionaries throughout: Poti Phera *kohen On*
(Bereishis 41:50), Yisro *kohen Midyan* (Shemos 2:16, 3:1), the Egyptian
priests exempt from Yosef's levy (Bereishis 47:22), and the man appointed over
Michah's idol (Shoftim 18:4), so that in all these places it seems the word is
being used of a religious functionary of an idolatrous cult. But Targum Onkelos
does not treat these alike: at Poti Phera and Yisro he renders *kohen* as
*rabbah*, master, which implies political leadership rather than ritual office,
while at the Egyptian priests and Michah's priest he renders *komer*. Rashi
likewise calls the Egyptian *kohanim* *komrim*, and gives the rule: *kohen*
means any religious functionary, except where it is attached to a place name
(*kohen Midyan*), where it means a position of leadership in that place. So
sometimes the word names a priest, of legitimate worship or otherwise, and
sometimes a political leader.

On the root, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740-1814) gives the biliteral כ-ן, base
or basis,
which also yields *ken* (so, yes) and *nachon* (correct), both of which affirm
a thing by acknowledging its basis, and *tochnis* (plan) and *hachanah*
(preparation), the bases of any serious undertaking. A *kohen*, then, is the
man charged with seeing that the preparations for the worship at a temple, of
whatever kind, are in order. Rav Hirsch (1808-1888) ties *kohen* to *hachanah*
as well: the Kohen sets an example of how Hashem is to be served and so
"prepares" others to be initiated into that service. Rabbi Pappenheim adds
secondary senses: one who wears any sort of special clothes, as a *chasan* does
(Yeshayahu 61:10), and anyone priest-like in functioning in an official
capacity, as a prince does (Shmuel II 8:18). Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916)
notes that the Arabic *kahin*, soothsayer or diviner, is probably a cognate of
*kohen*, and on that basis suggests that the root of *kohen* is *koh*, "thus,"
the prophets' word when they relay Hashem's message. The column adds that this
makes sense, since kohanim not only served in the Mikdash but were instrumental
in teaching Torah, which is likewise a relaying of Hashem's word (see Malachi
2:7).

### komer (כומר)
Radak (Tzefaniah 1:4, Melachim II 23:5, and *Sefer HaShorashim*): *komer* means
blackened (compare Eichah 5:10), so a *komer* is a priest in black vestments,
which a Kohen is not, the legitimate Kohanim in the Mikdash having worn white.
Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549, *Sefer HaTishbi*) argues that *komer*
literally means closed, clustered, cloistered, citing the Targum to Tehillim
77:10 and finding his evidence in the Talmudic *komer shel anavim*, a mass of
grapes all clustered together (Bechoros 31a); on his reading a *komer* is
specifically an idolatrous priest secluded and clustered in a monastery, that
is, a monk, while *kohen* is the more general word.

The column also notes a letter from the Elephantine Papyri that distinguishes
Jewish priests, called *kohanim*, from Egyptian priests, called *komrim*.

Rav Hirsch reads *komer* from *nichmeru rachamav*, of Yosef's mercy welling up
when he saw Binyamin (Bereishis 43:30), together with *michmar*, a net
(Yeshayahu 19:8, 51:20). The idolatrous priest catches his followers in the net
of his cult by working on their imagination and their feelings, where the Torah
addresses a man's understanding; so the *komer* feeds on his followers'
emotions while the Kohen feeds them Torah.

### the disputed pesukim
Yoshiyahu's purge: he removed the *komrim* from their idolatrous posts and
helped them do teshuvah, and barred those *kohanim*, Aharon's descendants, who
had previously been *komrim* for idolatry from serving in the Mikdash in
Yerushalayim (Melachim II 23:5-9, with Radak).

Tzefaniah 1:4, where both words stand together, has four readings. Targum and
Rashi seem to understand that *komrim* are any who take part in idol worship
and *kohanim* are the idolatrous priests, which reverses the usual division.
Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi (1279-1345): *komrim* are the idolatrous priests of Baal,
*kohanim* are Hashem's kohanim, some of whom had been acting corruptly. Radak,
in three places: both words mean idolatrous priests. Dr. Chaim Tawil notes that
*kumru* means priest in Akkadian and in other Western Semitic languages, so that
Tzefaniah's *komrim* was likely an oblique reference to cultic functionaries
from the places where those languages were spoken; on that reading it could be
that *komrim* and *kohanim* both mean idolatrous priests and are perfect
synonyms drawn from two different languages. Ibn Kaspi and Radak both leave the
general rule standing, that *kohen* covers a priest of any sort while *komer* is
the idolater's.

## Rendering guidance

- Aharon's descendants: **Kohen**, capitalized and untranslated, in any frum
  context. "Priest" for them reads Christian and flattens the halachic term.
- *Kohen* of a gentile: **priest**, or "chieftain" where Onkelos reads *rabbah*
  (Yisro, Poti Phera). Say which you followed if the passage is being darshened.
- *Komer*: **idolatrous priest**, or "priest of the idol." Where Rabbi Eliyahu
  HaBachur's reading is at issue, "cloistered priest" or "monk."
- Trap: English "priest" absorbs both words. In Tzefaniah 1:4, where both stand
  in one pasuk, one English word for both destroys the pasuk; pick a reading,
  render the two differently, and footnote the other views. Note that on Dr.
  Tawil's reading the two words really are synonyms, so that a translation
  distinguishing them takes a side.
- Trap: do not import "idolatrous" into *kohen* where the Hebrew has not said
  it. Rashi and the Targum supply that in specific places only, and where the
  word is bound to a place name it may not mean a priest at all.
