# Priest: kohen / komer

## Bottom line

*Kohen* is the general word: a **priest** of any cult, and in Tanach it is used
of Poti Phera, Yisro and the priest of Michah's idol no less than of Aharon's
sons. *Komer* is specific: an **idolatrous priest** (Radak, Ibn Kaspi). *Kohen*
does not by itself mean an idolatrous priest; the Targum and Rashi decide case
by case whether it does. Where it is bound to a place name, as with Poti Phera
and Yisro, Onkelos renders it *rabbah*, master, and Rashi takes it as a position
of leadership. The general rule does not settle Tzefaniah 1:4, where both words
stand in one pasuk and four readings are given. Targum and Rashi seem to reverse
the usual division there, and on Dr. Chaim Tawil's Akkadian evidence the two
words may be synonyms drawn from two languages.

## Distinctions

### kohen (כהן)
Used of idolatrous and foreign functionaries throughout: Poti Phera *kohen On*
(Bereishis 41:50), Yisro *kohen Midyan* (Shemos 2:16, 3:1), the Egyptian
priests exempt from Yosef's levy (Bereishis 47:22), and the man appointed over
Michah's idol (Shoftim 18:4), so that in all these places it seems the word is
being used of a religious functionary of an idolatrous cult. But Targum Onkelos
does not treat these alike: at Poti Phera and Yisro he renders *kohen* as
*rabbah*, master, which implies political leadership more than ritual office,
while at the Egyptian priests and Michah's priest he renders *komer*. Rashi
likewise calls the Egyptian *kohanim* *komrim*, and gives the rule: *kohen*
means any religious functionary, except where it is attached to a place name
(*kohen Midyan*), where it means a position of leadership in that place. So
sometimes the word names a priest, of legitimate worship or otherwise, and
sometimes a political leader.

- On the root, Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (1740-1814) gives the biliteral כ-ן, base
  or basis, which also yields *ken* (so, yes) and *nachon* (correct), both of
  which affirm a thing by acknowledging its basis, and *tochnis* (plan) and
  *hachanah* (preparation), the bases of any serious undertaking. A *kohen*,
  then, is the man charged with seeing that the preparations for the worship at
  a temple, of whatever kind, are in order.
- Rav Hirsch (1808-1888) ties *kohen* to *hachanah* as well: the Kohen sets an
  example of how Hashem is to be served and so "prepares" others to be initiated
  into that service.
- Rabbi Pappenheim adds secondary senses: one who wears any sort of special
  clothes, as a *chasan* does (Yeshayahu 61:10), and anyone priest-like in
  functioning in an official capacity, as a prince does (Shmuel II 8:18).
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843-1916) notes that the Arabic *kahin*, soothsayer or
  diviner, is probably a cognate of *kohen*, and on that basis suggests that the
  root of *kohen* is *koh*, "thus," the prophets' word when they relay Hashem's
  message. Indeed, kohanim served in the Mikdash and were also instrumental in teaching Torah, so it makes sense that they would be associated with
  prophecy, which likewise relays Hashem's word (see Malachi 2:7).

### komer (כומר)
- Radak (Tzefaniah 1:4, Melachim II 23:5, and *Sefer HaShorashim*): *komer*
  means blackened (compare Eichah 5:10), so a *komer* is a priest in black
  vestments, which a Kohen is not, the legitimate Kohanim in the Mikdash having
  worn white.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549, *Sefer HaTishbi*) argues that *komer*
  literally means closed, clustered, cloistered, citing the Targum to Tehillim
  77:10 and finding his evidence in the Talmudic *komer shel anavim*, a mass of
  grapes all clustered together (Bechoros 31b); on his reading a *komer* is
  specifically an idolatrous priest secluded and clustered in a monastery, that
  is, a monk, while *kohen* is the more general word.

A letter from the Elephantine Papyri distinguishes
Jewish priests, called *kohanim*, from Egyptian priests, called *komrim*.

Rav Hirsch reads *komer* from *nichmeru rachamav*, of Yosef's mercy welling up
when he saw Binyamin (Bereishis 43:30), together with *michmar*, a net
(Yeshayahu 19:8, 51:20). The idolatrous priest catches his followers in the net
of his cult by working on their imagination and their feelings, where the Torah
addresses a man's understanding; so the *komer* feeds on his followers'
emotions while the Kohen feeds them Torah.

### the disputed pesukim
Yoshiyahu's purge: he removed the *komrim* from their idolatrous posts and
helped them do teshuvah, and barred those *kohanim*, Aharon's descendants, who
had previously been *komrim* for idolatry from serving in the Mikdash in
Yerushalayim (Melachim II 23:5-9, with Radak).

Tzefaniah 1:4, where both words stand together, has four readings.
- Targum and Rashi seem to understand that *komrim* are any who take part in
  idol worship and *kohanim* are the idolatrous priests, which reverses the
  usual division.
- Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi (1279-1345): *komrim* are the idolatrous priests of
  Baal, *kohanim* are Hashem's kohanim, some of whom had been acting corruptly.
- Radak, in three places: both words mean idolatrous priests.
- Dr. Chaim Tawil notes that *kumru* means priest in Akkadian and in other
  Western Semitic languages, so that Tzefaniah's *komrim* was likely an oblique
  reference to cultic functionaries from the places where those languages were
  spoken; on that reading it could be that *komrim* and *kohanim* both mean
  idolatrous priests and are perfect synonyms drawn from two different
  languages.

Ibn Kaspi and Radak both leave the general rule standing, that *kohen* covers a
priest of any sort while *komer* is the idolater's.

## Rendering guidance

- Aharon's descendants: **Kohen**, capitalized and untranslated. "Priest" for them reads Christian and flattens the halachic term.
- *Kohen* of a gentile: **priest**, or "chieftain" where Onkelos reads *rabbah*
  (Yisro, Poti Phera). Say which you followed if the passage is being darshened.
- *Komer*: **idolatrous priest**, or "priest of the idol." Where Rabbi Eliyahu
  HaBachur's reading is at issue, "cloistered priest" or "monk."
- Trap: English "priest" absorbs both words. In Tzefaniah 1:4, where both stand
  in one pasuk, one English word for both destroys the pasuk; pick a reading,
  render the two differently, and footnote the other views. Note that on Dr.
  Tawil's reading the two words really are synonyms, so that a translation
  distinguishing them takes a side.
- Trap: do not import "idolatrous" into *kohen* where the Hebrew has not said
  it. Rashi and the Targum supply that in specific places only, and where the
  word is bound to a place name it may not mean a priest at all.
