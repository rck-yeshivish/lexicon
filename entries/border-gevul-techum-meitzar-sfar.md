# Border: gevul / techum / meitzar / sfar

## Bottom line

*Gevul* is the Biblical word and, on Rav Hirsch's reading, names the whole
**territory** inside the line, not only the line: "throughout your borders"
means the whole country. *Techum* is the Targum's rendering of *gevul* and the
Mishnaic word for **the bounded area** (*techum Shabbos*). *Meitzar* (plural
*mitzarim*) is the Rabbinic word for a **boundary**, as between adjoining
fields, distinct, per Ibn Kuraish, from the Biblical *meitzar* meaning
**straits**. *Sfar* typically names not the line itself but what sits near it,
the **frontier** or border region exposed to what lies outside. Rashi reads
*bein hametzarim* two ways at once: the Three Weeks, taking
*mitzarim* from *tzarah*, "sorrow"; and, in the plain sense, "between the
boundaries."

## Distinctions

### gevul (גבול)
Root ג-ב-ל. The Torah uses *gevul* and its inflections fourteen times in the
twelve pesukim laying out the borders of Eretz Yisrael (Bamidbar 34:1-12). The
root also means to limit: Hashem tells Moshe to bound the people off
(*v'higbalta*) around Har Sinai (Shemos 19:12; *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* there).

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 7:27) makes the key point: *gevul* at its core is the
area within the borders, not just the edge. Hashem strikes Pharaoh's whole *gevul*
with frogs (Shemos 7:27) and brings locusts into his *gevul* (Shemos 10:4);
chametz is forbidden "in all your borders" (Shemos 13:7). The frogs are not
confined to Egypt's edges but fill the whole country, and the last two cases
likewise take in a whole country, not just the border region (see *Yerios
Shlomo* p. 836 and *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* to Shemos 13:7). Rav Hirsch links
*gevul* to *yevul*, produce, what one brings inside one's bounds, on the
gimmel/yod interchange.

Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) adds *givlim*, "stonecutters" (Melachim I 5:32),
for giving stones a bound by cutting them to size, or a gentilic for men of
Geval (Yechezkel 27:9); Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg proposes a metathesis of
*galav*, "barber," both trades being cutting. Two further etymologies: Rabbi
David Golumb reads ג-ב-ל as a metathesis of ב-ל-ג, "strengthening" (Amos 5:9,
Tehillim 39:14, Iyov 9:27, 10:20), since what sits on a border needs
fortifying. Rabbi Avraham Rivlin raises the opposite problem: in Rabbinic
Hebrew the same root means to knead and mix (*Ohalos* 2:2, *Shabbos* 24:3,
*Bava Metzia* 10:5, *Parah* 9:5), which unifies rather than divides. He answers
that the border separating one land from another is what unites that land as
one; *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* (to Shemos 19:12) answers that a *gevul* is the
point of convergence, where one domain ends the other begins, so that it both
separates and merges. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds a third possibility of his own: Hebrew
roots not uncommonly carry a sense and its opposite, *shoresh* (root) beside
*l'sharesh* (to uproot), since a word stands in for the abstract concept it
represents, and opposites are conceptually related, inversely.

Outside the parshanut, philologists such as Wilhelm
Gesenius have long taken *gevul* as cognate with the Arabic *jabal*,
"mountain," mountains serving as natural borders between two polities or even
between two plains. The Yemenite Jewish surname
Jibli/Gibli, from that Arabic word, is the equivalent of the Hebrew surname
Harari and the Yiddish/German Berg, all of them meaning "mountain."

### techum (תחום)
The standard Targumic rendering of *gevul* (Targum to Bereishis 10:19, 23:17,
Shemos 19:12, 19:23, Devarim 19:4), and in Mishnaic Hebrew the word for what
falls inside a bound: *techum Shabbos*, the two thousand amos outside the city
within which one may walk on Shabbos (*Eruvin* 5:5, *Beitzah* 4:2, *Sotah* 5:3).
- Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his etymological dictionary of Hebrew, takes the
  Rabbinic Hebrew *techum* as a loanword from that Aramaic word, which he in
  turn sees as borrowed from the Akkadian *tahumu*.
- Rabbi Benzion Chaim Lubetzki (*Midrash Safah Echas*) reads it as a metathesis
  of *chasum*, "sealed," the space inside being sealed off by what rings it.
- Rabbi Golumb sets the tav aside and takes the core root as ח-ו-ם, "brown/red,"
  again from the fortifying a frontier needs; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein records
  the derivation without being able to reconstruct how Rabbi Golumb gets from
  the color to the border.

### meitzar (מיצר) and mitzarim (מצרים)
Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Kuraish (*Risala*) identifies *mitzarim* in Eichah 1:3 as the
plural of the Rabbinic *meitzar*, "boundary," as in *dina d'bar meitzra*, the
neighbor's right of first refusal on an adjoining field (Kesubos 44a). He notes
that this is not the Biblical *meitzar* of "from the straits I called out to
Hashem" (Tehillim 118:5), which belongs to *tzar*, narrowness, and denotes
something slightly different. Some commentators, among them Ibn Ezra (to Eichah
1:3) and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*), do connect the two. Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein suggests the connection can be accounted for thematically: a border
serves to narrow down the contours of a given plot.

Rashi to Eichah 1:3, following *Eichah Rabbah* 1:29, reads *bein hametzarim* as
the three weeks bookended by Shivah Asar B'Tammuz and Tishah B'Av, taking
*mitzarim* as a cognate of *tzarah*, "sorrow," "suffering"; he then gives the
contextual, plain-sense reading, that the Jews were caught where they could not
escape, penned between two fences, and glosses with *gevul*, while Targum and
Mahari Kara gloss with *techum*. Rabbi Avraham Yitzchok Shain (*Birkas Ish*)
theorizes that the name Mitzrayim (Bereishis 10:6, Divrei HaYamim I 1:8) comes
from this sense of boundary: Cham, set on sparing his sons the in-fighting he
had known with his own brothers, and that Kayin and Hevel had known, took pains
to mark out Kush's territory clearly once his second son was born.

### sfar (ספר)
The word typically refers to something located near the border: an *ir
hanidachas* at the *sfar* is not destroyed, lest the gap invite the neighboring
nations in (*Sanhedrin* 1:5), and *eglah arufah* is not done for a corpse found
near the *sfar* (*Sotah* 9:2). Targum uses it likewise (Bereishis 49:13,
Devarim 1:7, Tzefaniah 2:5-6). In the sense of "border" the word seems to
belong only to later Rabbinic Hebrew; there is one possible Biblical
appearance, *sefarah* in Bereishis 10:30, where the land of Shem's descendants
stretches from Meisha (Mecca, per Rabbi Saadia Gaon) to its *sfar* at Har
HaKedem, and Targum Onkelos renders with *sfar*. On that basis Rabbi
Mecklenburg (*HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* to Vayikra 23:16; *Iyun Tefillah* to
Tehillim 147:5) calls it the only instance of *sfar* in Biblical Hebrew, though
most other commentators appear to take *sefarah* there as a place name. The
etymology is not immediately clear; Rabbi Golumb conjectures that *sfar* comes from
*perazi*, used of a city-wall fortification, by samech/zayin interchange and
metathesis, border cities having been especially fortified. Elsewhere (*Targumna*
to Shemos 9:29 and Vayikra 14:9) Rabbi Golumb takes the core meaning of the
string ס-פ-ר as "circle" or "round," related to the Greek *sphere*: a city near
the border is said to be on the *sfar* because such cities are typically
surrounded all around by a city wall that protects them from invasion. Rabbi
Moshe Shapiro holds that all words from ס-פ-ר are interrelated: as a *sippur*
joins many details into one holistic unit, a *sfar*, whether the border of a
country or the city limits, confines everything within it and makes it one
unit. Ramak (*Pardes Rimonim*, shaar 8 ch. 2) uses *sfar* to explain
*sefirah*: as a border marks off the limits of an area, a *sefirah* is Hashem's
influx as it reaches man in finite, bounded form, kin to *mispar* (number) and
*sippur* (what can be told, as Hashem Himself cannot).

## Rendering guidance

- Translate *gevul* by the sense the pasuk needs. "Border" for Bamidbar 34,
  where the line is the subject; "territory" or "throughout your domain" for
  Shemos 10:4 and 13:7, where "border" would give a false picture of frogs or
  chametz confined to the edge.
- Trap: English "boundary" will absorb *gevul*, *techum*, and *meitzar*. Where
  Rashi glosses with *gevul* and Targum with *techum* on one phrase, that is
  one gloss in two dialects, not two opinions; render both "boundary" and say
  so.
- *Bein hametzarim* as the calendar period stays transliterated, with "the
  Three Weeks" as the gloss. Where Rashi's second reading is in play, render
  "between the boundaries" and keep "straits" for the Tehillim 118:5 sense.
- *Techum Shabbos* stays transliterated, glossed once as "the Shabbos limit."
- *Sfar* is "frontier" or "border region," never plain "border," since the
  point in both mishnayos is exposure to the outside.
