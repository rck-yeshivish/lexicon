# Border: gevul / techum / meitzar / sfar

## Bottom line

*Gevul* is the Biblical word and, on Rav Hirsch's reading, names the whole
**territory** inside the line, not only the line: "throughout your borders"
means the whole country. *Techum* is the Targum's rendering of *gevul* and the
Mishnaic word for **the bounded area** (*techum Shabbos*). *Meitzar* (plural
*mitzarim*) is the Rabbinic word for a **boundary** between fields, distinct,
per Ibn Kuraish, from the Biblical *meitzar* meaning **straits**. *Sfar* is the
**frontier**, the borderland exposed to what lies outside. The trap is *bein
hametzarim*, which Rashi reads two ways at once: "between the straits" (from
*tzarah*) and "between the boundaries."

## Distinctions

### gevul (גבול)
Root ג-ב-ל. The Torah uses *gevul* and its inflections fourteen times in the
twelve pesukim laying out the borders of Eretz Yisrael (Bamidbar 34:1-12). The
root also means to limit: Hashem tells Moshe to bound the people off
(*v'higbalta*) around Har Sinai (Shemos 19:12; *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* there).

Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 7:27) makes the key point: *gevul* at its core is the
area within the borders, not the edge. Hashem strikes Pharaoh's whole *gevul*
with frogs (Shemos 7:27) and brings locusts into his *gevul* (Shemos 10:4);
chametz is forbidden "in all your borders" (Shemos 13:7). None of these mean a
strip along the edge (see also *Yerios Shlomo* p. 836 and *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah*
to Shemos 13:7). Rav Hirsch links *gevul* to *yevul*, produce, what one brings
inside one's bounds, on the gimmel/yod interchange.

Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) adds *givlim*, "stonecutters" (Melachim I 5:32),
for giving stones a bound by cutting them to size, or a gentilic for men of
Geval (Yechezkel 27:9); Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg proposes a metathesis of
*galav*, "barber," both trades being cutting. Two further etymologies: Rabbi
David Golumb reads ג-ב-ל as a metathesis of ב-ל-ג, "strengthening" (Amos 5:9,
Tehillim 39:14, Iyov 9:27, 10:20), since what sits on a border needs
fortifying. Rabbi Avraham Rivlin raises the opposite problem: in Rabbinic
Hebrew the same root means to knead and mix (*Ohalos* 2:2, *Shabbos* 24:3,
*Bava Metzia* 10:5, *Parah* 9:5), which unifies rather than divides. He answers
that the border separating one land from another is what unites that land as
one; *HaKtav VeHaKabbalah* (to Shemos 19:12) answers that a *gevul* is the
point of convergence, where one domain ends the other begins.

### techum (תחום)
The standard Targumic rendering of *gevul* (Onkelos to Bereishis 10:19, 23:17,
Shemos 19:12, 19:23, Devarim 19:4), and in Mishnaic Hebrew the word for what
falls inside a bound: *techum Shabbos*, the two thousand amos outside the city
within which one may walk (*Eruvin* 5:5, *Beitzah* 4:2, *Sotah* 5:3). Rabbi
Benzion Chaim Lubetzki (*Midrash Safah Echas*) reads it as a metathesis of
*chasum*, "sealed," the space inside being sealed off by what rings it. Rabbi
Golumb treats the tav as a radical and the root as ח-ו-ם, again from the
fortifying a frontier needs.

### meitzar (מיצר) and mitzarim (מצרים)
Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Kuraish (*Risala*) identifies *mitzarim* in Eichah 1:3 as the
plural of the Rabbinic *meitzar*, "boundary," as in *dina d'bar meitzra*, the
neighbor's right of first refusal on an adjoining field (Kesubos 44a). He
insists this is not the Biblical *meitzar* of "from the straits I called out to
Hashem" (Tehillim 118:5), which belongs to *tzar*, narrowness. Ibn Ezra (to
Eichah 1:3) and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) do connect them, and
the connection is easy enough: a border narrows down the contours of a plot.

Rashi to Eichah 1:3, following *Eichah Rabbah* 1:29, reads *bein hametzarim* as
the three weeks from Shivah Asar B'Tammuz to Tishah B'Av, taking *mitzarim*
from *tzarah*; he then gives the plain-sense reading, that the Jews were caught
where they could not escape, penned between two fences, and glosses with
*gevul*, while Targum and Mahari Kara gloss with *techum*. Rabbi Avraham
Yitzchok Shain (*Birkas Ish*) reads the name Mitzrayim (Bereishis 10:6, Divrei
HaYamim I 1:8) from this sense of boundary: Cham, set on sparing his sons what
he had known with his brothers, marked out Kush's territory sharply once his
second son was born.

### sfar (ספר)
The frontier: an *ir hanidachas* on the *sfar* is not destroyed, lest the gap
invite the neighboring nations in (*Sanhedrin* 1:5), and *eglah arufah* is not
done for a corpse found near the *sfar* (*Sotah* 9:2). Targum uses it likewise
(Bereishis 49:13, Devarim 1:7, Tzefaniah 2:5-6). Rabbi Mecklenburg (*HaKtav
VeHaKabbalah* to Vayikra 23:16; *Iyun Tefillah* to Tehillim 147:5) notes one
Biblical instance, *sefarah* in Bereishis 10:30, where Onkelos renders *sfar*;
most commentators take *sefarah* there as a place name. Rabbi Golumb derives
*sfar* from *perazi*, city fortification, by samech/zayin interchange and
metathesis. Ramak (*Pardes Rimonim*, shaar 8 ch. 2) uses *sfar* to explain
*sefirah*: as a border marks off the limits of an area, a *sefirah* is Hashem's
influx as it reaches man in finite, bounded form, kin to *mispar* (number) and
*sippur* (what can be told, as Hashem Himself cannot).

## Rendering guidance

- Translate *gevul* by the sense the pasuk needs. "Border" for Bamidbar 34,
  where the line is the subject; "territory" or "throughout your domain" for
  Shemos 10:4 and 13:7, where "border" would give a false picture of frogs or
  chametz confined to the edge.
- Trap: English "boundary" will absorb *gevul*, *techum*, and *meitzar*. Where
  Rashi glosses with *gevul* and Targum with *techum* on one phrase, that is
  one gloss in two dialects, not two opinions; render both "boundary" and say
  so.
- *Bein hametzarim* as the calendar period stays transliterated, with "the
  Three Weeks" as the gloss. Where Rashi's second reading is in play, render
  "between the boundaries" and keep "straits" for the Tehillim 118:5 sense.
- *Techum Shabbos* stays transliterated, glossed once as "the Shabbos limit."
- *Sfar* is "frontier" or "border region," never plain "border," since the
  point in both mishnayos is exposure to the outside.
