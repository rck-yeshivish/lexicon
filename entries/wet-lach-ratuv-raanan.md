# Wetness: lach / ratuv / ra'anan / rutfash / tofeach / mefulam

## Bottom line

*Lach* is **moist**: a little wet, and on Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim's reading the
inner moisture of a living thing. *Ratuv* is **wet**. Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Radak
simply define it as *lach*, and the column suggests that perhaps the two are
not technically synonyms, *lach* being native Hebrew and *ratuv* borrowed from
Aramaic; Pappenheim instead makes *ratuv* enough liquid to show on the
surface and to wet whatever touches it, moisture that comes from outside, and
Rabbi Avraham Bedersi likewise makes it moisture from external water.
*Ra'anan* is **fresh, verdant**, for Bedersi the inner moisture that keeps a
thing growing. *Rutfash* is **revivified**, flesh restored to youthful
moisture. *Tofeach* and *mefulam* are Rabbinic: *tofeach* is **wet**, familiar
in the phrase for wet enough to make something else wet; *mefulam* is **wet**
for Rashi but **hearty, plump** for the *Aruch*, and the column suggests Rashi
may mean **sodden**, so saturated as to be bloated. The trap is English "wet,"
which absorbs *lach* and *ratuv* and loses Pappenheim's difference between a
little moisture and enough to wet something else, a difference that can carry
halachic weight.

## Distinctions

### lach (לח)
Three times in the Torah and four more in Tanach: Yaakov's still-*lach* branches
(Bereishis 30:37), the *nazir*'s grapes, whether *lachim* or dried (Bamidbar
6:3), and Moshe Rabbeinu at 120, "his eye had not dimmed and his *leicho* had
not fled" (Devarim 34:7); also of flora that has not dried out (Shoftim 16:7-8;
Yechezkel 17:24, 21:3). It appears numerous times in the Mishnah (*Demai* 2:3;
*Nedarim* 7:1; *Eduyos* 5:4; *Menachos* 9:2; *Bechoros* 6:3; *Mikvaos* 9:2;
*Niddah* 4:3, 7:2).

The word is a case study in root theory. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*, 920 to 970), the consummate biliteralist, gives the root as
lamed-ches, which makes sense for a two-letter word. The triliteralists are at
a loss: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) offers lamed-vav-ches or
lamed-yud-ches; Radak, more definitively, and Ibn Parchon (*Machberes
HeAruch*) settle on lamed-vav-ches. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi (1821 to
1898) suggests *lach* is related to *rach*, soft, through the interchange of
REISH with LAMED and KAF with CHES. Pappenheim, like Ibn Saruk, takes
lamed-ches as the ultimate root, meaning moisture itself, and hangs a family on
it: *lechi*, the cheek, a concentration of inner moisture, and from the cheek's
likeness to a flat board *luach*, a board (in Rabbinic Hebrew *lechi* is also
the upright post of an *eruv*); *lechem*, bread or meat, which sustains and
restores inner moisture; and *melach*, salt, which draws liquid out, as in
salting meat. The name of the Lechi paramilitary group is an acronym of
*lochamei cheirus Yisrael* and has nothing to do with the word.

### ratuv (רטוב)
All the classical lexicographers agree on the root reish-tes-beis. It occurs
twice in Tanach, both in Iyov: a well-watered plant, *ratov* before the sun
(8:16), and the man robbed of his clothing, soaked by the mountain runoff,
*yirtavu* (24:8). It is the Targumim's standard rendering of *lach*, and Rashi
(Iyov 8:16, 24:8; Sukkah 10b), Ibn Ezra (Iyov 8:16), and Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) define *ratuv* as *lach*. Since the Targumim typically render
*lach* by *ratuv*, as Rashi (Iyov 24:8) and Ibn Ezra (Iyov 8:16) note, the
column suggests that perhaps the two are not technically synonyms, because
*lach* is a native Hebrew word while *ratuv* is borrowed from Aramaic (or
another Semitic language).

Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) takes another approach and distinguishes them
twice over. Quantitatively: *lach* is only slightly wet, certainly not enough
to wet what touches it, often something that has begun to dry, like Yaakov's
branches or harvested grapes still moist inside as against raisins; *ratuv* is
very wet, with the wetness visible on the surface and certainly enough to wet
what touches it. Qualitatively: *lach* is the inner moisture of every living
creature and plant, without which the body withers, while *ratuv* is moisture
arriving from outside and seen on the surface.

Mishnaic Hebrew keeps *ratuv* for wet (*Tevul Yom* 3:6; see also *Uktzin* 2:2)
but more commonly uses the cognate *rotev*, juices, fluids or sauce (*Pesachim*
7:2; *Nedarim* 7:6; *Zevachim* 3:4; *Chullin* 7:5, 9:1; *Taharos* 1:4, 3:1).
Pappenheim reads *rotev* off *ratuv*, liquid too abundant to be absorbed so that
it stays on the surface, and sees it as synonymous with biblical *marak*
(Shoftim 6:19-20), a link already drawn by pseudo-Rashi to Nedarim 52a. Modern
Hebrew has made *marak* "soup," close to its original meaning.

### ra'anan (רענן)
Twenty occurrences in Tanach, only one in the Torah (Devarim 12:2). Ibn Ezra
there says it means the same as *ratuv*; the Targumim do not generally render
it so, but in at least one place they do (Tehillim 92:15, see also 92:11).
Since the word always ends in a double nun, Radak gives the root as
reish-ayin-nun and Ibn Saruk as a four-letter reish-ayin-nun-nun. Rabbi Avraham
Bedersi (thirteenth century; *Chosam Tochnis*, the first lexicon of Hebrew
synonyms) defines *ratuv* as moisture from external water and sets it against
its ostensible synonym *ra'anan*, the internal moist freshness that lets a thing
keep growing rather than dry and wither. Rabbi Tedeschi parses *ra'anan* as a
portmanteau of *re'ah*, friend, and *anan*, cloud: a plant still moist keeps
growing until it is so tall it is "a friend of the clouds."

### rutfash (רטפש)
A *hapax*, like *ratuv* found only in Iyov: Elihu says that when a sick man's
merits intercede, Hashem heals him so that "his flesh is revivified (*rutfash*)
more than in youth" (Iyov 33:25). Its apparent root is the four-letter
reish-tes-peh-shin, but four-letter roots are rare in Biblical Hebrew and occur
more often in foreign loanwords, and the commentators instead relate it to
*ratuv* in four ways. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 25:6): the final shin is
paragogic, leaving reish-tes-peh, equal to *ratuv*'s reish-tes-beis by the
peh-beis interchange. A portmanteau of *ratuv* and *pash*, expansion: those
who implicitly or explicitly follow this include Rabbi Yosef Kara, Rabbi Yosef
Kimchi, Sforno, *Metzudas Zion* (Iyov 33:25), Radak (Tehillim 103:5), and
pseudo-Rashi (Nedarim 41a). Malbim (Iyov 33:25), similarly, a portmanteau of
*ratuv* and *tafash*, smeared. Rabbi Yosef Kimchi (Iyov 33:25) and Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) read it as a cognate of *ratuv*, its first two letters matching
*ratuv*'s and its last two matching *ratuv*'s last two in *at-bash*.

### tofeach (טופח)
Rabbinic only (*Eduyos* 4:6; *Keilim* 8:3-4, 10:8; *Taharos* 1:9, 3:1, 8:8-9;
*Machshirin* 3:5), and familiar from *tofeach al menas lehatfiach* (Berachos
25b; Yoma 78a), so wet that it can make other things wet. Its root
tes-peh-ches means something else in Tanach, the source of *tefach*, a
handbreadth (the column renders it "cubit"), and of words for measuring length
and extension, a sense that carries into Rabbinic Hebrew. How that fits the
"wet" sense is unclear; the column suggests, as its own tentative proposal,
that perhaps an item dripping wet expands in its dimensions.

### mefulam (מפולם)
Three appearances in the Bavli, with no known relatives for the ostensible root
peh-lamed-mem in Biblical or Mishnaic Hebrew, with one possible exception
(below): *mefulamos* fish accepted from a gentile on Yom Tov (Beitzah 24b);
*bohu* as the *mefulamos* stones sunk in the deep from which water flowed
(Chagigah 12a); and *mefulamos* stones, quarried from the sea bed and so
untouched by any tool, fit for the mizbeach (Zevachim 54a; see Rabbi Chaim
Kanievsky's *Derech Chochmah* to the Rambam, *Hilchos Beis HaBechirah* 1:92).
Rashi in all three places: wet. Rabbi Nosson of Rome (1035 to 1106) in *Sefer
HeAruch*, against Rashi, defines it hearty, plump, or fat.

On the etymology, Dr. Marcus Jastrow (1829 to 1903) takes it as a cognate of
Arabic *tafailam*, whose meaning is comparable with the *Aruch*'s definition.
Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842 to 1894), in *HeAruch HaShaleim*, suggests the root
derives either from the Persian for a clump of dirt or from Greek *piloma*,
*piloga*, *pilos* ("clay, mud, pile"), treated as though Hebrew. The Greek
*piloma* in fact appears in some versions of Mishnah *Shabbos* 22:6 (the
Kaufmann manuscript and the Alfasi), a muddy river one may not go into on
Shabbos. To square all this with Rashi's "wet," the column suggests Rashi might
have read the word on the model of *tofeach*: so saturated with liquid as to be
bloated, distended, and plump.

Ralbag (Bereishis 1:2) takes another road, reading the *mefulamos* that define
*bohu* as a cognate of *palmoni* (Daniel 8:13), itself from *ploni* and
*almoni*, terms for the anonymous and unknown, so that *bohu* means mysterious
stones. The Rema (1520 to 1572) cites his reading in *Mechir Yayin* (to Esther
2:1) and *Toras HaOlah* (3:73); see also *Ben Yehoyada* to Chagigah 12a.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "wet" will absorb *lach* and *ratuv*. Use "moist" for *lach*, "wet" or
  "soaked" for *ratuv*, and keep the pair apart wherever halachah is at stake.
  The column notes that the kind of difference Pappenheim draws can have
  practical ramifications: how saturated a fabric must be before light
  squeezing on Shabbos is *sechitah*, and when a wet food counts as a liquid
  for *bishul*. *Lach* against *yavesh* is "moist" against "dry" (Bamidbar
  6:3).
- *Leicho* at Devarim 34:7 is Moshe Rabbeinu's inner vitality, not surface
  wetness; "his freshness had not fled" reads correctly, "he was not wet" does
  not.
- *Ra'anan* of a tree is "leafy" or "verdant," of a person "fresh"; do not
  render it "wet," which is *ratuv*'s word even though Ibn Ezra equates them.
- *Tofeach al menas lehatfiach* is a fixed halachic phrase. Leave it
  transliterated and gloss it once: damp enough to wet something else.
- *Mefulam*: "wet" where the passage follows Rashi, "plump" where it follows
  the *Aruch*.
