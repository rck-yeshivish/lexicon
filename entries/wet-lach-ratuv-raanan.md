# Wetness: lach / ratuv / ra'anan / rutfash / tofeach / mefulam

## Bottom line

*Lach* is **moist**: a little wet, and on Pappenheim's reading the inner
moisture of a living thing. *Ratuv* is **wet** proper: enough liquid to show on
the surface and to wet whatever touches it, and it comes from outside. *Ra'anan*
is **fresh, verdant**, the moisture that keeps a plant growing. *Rutfash* is
**revivified**, flesh restored to youthful moisture. *Tofeach* and *mefulam* are
Rabbinic: **damp enough to transfer** and **sodden**. The trap is English "wet,"
which absorbs *lach* and *ratuv* and loses the halachically live difference
between a little moisture and enough to wet something else.

## Distinctions

### lach (לח)
Three times in the Torah and four more in Tanach: Yaakov's still-*lach* branches
(Bereishis 30:37), the *nazir*'s grapes, whether *lachim* or dried (Bamidbar
6:3), and Moshe Rabbeinu at 120, "his eye had not dimmed and his *leicho* had
not fled" (Devarim 34:7); also of flora that has not dried out (Shoftim 16:7-8;
Yechezkel 17:24, 21:3). It is common in Mishnaic Hebrew (*Demai* 2:3; *Eduyos*
5:4; *Menachos* 9:2; *Bechoros* 6:3; *Mikvaos* 9:2; *Niddah* 4:3, 7:2).

The word is a test case in root theory. Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*), the biliteralist, gives the root as lamed-ches; Ibn Janach (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) offers lamed-vav-ches or lamed-yud-ches; Radak and Ibn Parchon
(*Machberes HeAruch*) settle on lamed-vav-ches. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi
relates *lach* to *rach*, soft. Pappenheim, with Ibn Saruk, takes lamed-ches as
an element meaning moisture itself, and hangs a family on it: *lechi*, the
cheek, a concentration of inner moisture, and from the cheek's flatness *luach*,
a board; *lechem*, which restores inner moisture; and *melach*, salt, which
draws liquid out.

### ratuv (רטוב)
All the classical lexicographers agree on the root reish-tes-beis. It occurs
twice in Tanach, both in Iyov: a well-watered plant, *ratov* before the sun
(8:16), and the man robbed of his clothing, soaked by the mountain runoff,
*yirtavu* (24:8). It is the Targumim's standing rendering of *lach*, and Rashi
(Iyov 8:16, 24:8; Sukkah 10b), Ibn Ezra (Iyov 8:16), and Radak (*Sefer
HaShorashim*) simply define *ratuv* as *lach*. That the Targum renders *lach* by
*ratuv*, as Rashi and Ibn Ezra note, suggests the two are not strictly synonyms
at all: *lach* is native Hebrew, *ratuv* Aramaic.

Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*) distinguishes them twice over. Quantitatively:
*lach* is only slightly wet, not enough to wet what touches it, often something
already partly dried, like Yaakov's branches or harvested grapes still moist
inside as against raisins; *ratuv* has enough liquid to show on the surface and
certainly to wet what touches it. Qualitatively: *lach* is the inner moisture of
every living thing, without which it withers, while *ratuv* is moisture arriving
from outside and sitting on the surface.

Mishnaic Hebrew keeps *ratuv* for wet (*Tevul Yom* 3:6; *Uktzin* 2:2) but far
more often uses *rotev*, juices or sauce (*Pesachim* 7:2; *Nedarim* 7:6;
*Zevachim* 3:4; *Chullin* 7:5, 9:1; *Taharos* 1:4, 3:1). Pappenheim reads
*rotev* off *ratuv*, liquid too abundant to be absorbed, and equates it with
biblical *marak* (Shoftim 6:19-20), a link already drawn by pseudo-Rashi to
Nedarim 52a.

### ra'anan (רענן)
Twenty occurrences in Tanach, only one in the Torah (Devarim 12:2). Ibn Ezra
there equates it with *ratuv*, and in at least one place the Targum renders it
so (Tehillim 92:15). Radak gives the root as reish-ayin-nun, Ibn Saruk as a
four-letter reish-ayin-nun-nun. Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*, the
first Hebrew synonym lexicon) makes the distinction directly: *ratuv* is
moisture from external water, *ra'anan* the internal moist freshness that lets a
thing keep growing rather than dry and wither.

### rutfash (רטפש)
A *hapax*: Elihu says that when a sick man's merits intercede, Hashem heals him
so that "his flesh is revivified (*rutfash*) beyond his youth" (Iyov 33:25).
Four-letter roots are rare in Tanach, so the commentators tie it back to
*ratuv*. Rav Hirsch (Bereishis 25:6): the final shin is paragogic, leaving
reish-tes-peh, equal to *ratuv*'s reish-tes-beis by the peh-beis interchange.
Rabbi Yosef Kara, Rabbi Yosef Kimchi, Sforno, *Metzudas Zion* (Iyov 33:25),
Radak (Tehillim 103:5), and pseudo-Rashi (Nedarim 41a): a portmanteau of *ratuv*
and *pash*, expansion, and Malbim similarly from *ratuv* and *tafash*, smeared.
Rabbi Yosef Kimchi and Radak (*Sefer HaShorashim*) read it as a cognate of
*ratuv*, its first two letters matching *ratuv*'s and its last two matching
*ratuv*'s last two in *at-bash*.

### tofeach (טופח)
Rabbinic only (*Eduyos* 4:6; *Keilim* 8:3-4, 10:8; *Taharos* 1:9, 3:1, 8:8-9;
*Machshirin* 3:5), and familiar from *tofeach al menas lehatfiach* (Berachos
25b; Yoma 78a), wet enough to make something else wet. Its root tes-peh-ches
means something else in Tanach, the source of *tefach*, a handbreadth (the
article calls *tefach* a cubit), and of words for length and extension; the
column suggests, as its own proposal, that a thing soaked through swells in its
dimensions.

### mefulam (מפולם)
Three appearances in the Gemara, with no evident biblical or Mishnaic relatives
for the root peh-lamed-mem: *mefulamos* fish accepted from a gentile on Yom Tov
(Beitzah 24b); *bohu* as the *mefulamos* stones sunk in the deep from which
water flowed (Chagigah 12a); and *mefulamos* stones, quarried from the sea bed
and so untouched by any tool, fit for the mizbeach (Zevachim 54a; see *Derech
Chochmah* to the Rambam, *Hilchos Beis HaBechirah* 1). Rashi in all three
places: wet. Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) instead defines it hearty,
plump, or fat, and the column reconciles the two on the model of *tofeach*, so
saturated as to be bloated. Ralbag (Bereishis 1:2) takes another road, reading
*mefulamos* from *palmoni* (Daniel 8:13), itself from *ploni* and *almoni*, so
that *bohu* means mysterious stones; so too the Rema (*Mechir Yayin* to Esther
2:1; *Toras HaOlah* 3:73).

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "wet" will absorb *lach* and *ratuv*. Use "moist" for *lach*, "wet" or
  "soaked" for *ratuv*, and keep the pair apart wherever halachah is at stake:
  how saturated a fabric must be before light squeezing on Shabbos is
  *sechitah*, and when a wet food counts as a liquid for *bishul*, both turn on
  it. *Lach* against *yavesh* is "moist" against "dry" (Bamidbar 6:3).
- *Leicho* at Devarim 34:7 is Moshe Rabbeinu's inner vitality, not surface
  wetness; "his freshness had not fled" reads correctly, "he was not wet" does
  not.
- *Ra'anan* of a tree is "leafy" or "verdant," of a person "fresh"; do not
  render it "wet," which is *ratuv*'s word even though Ibn Ezra equates them.
- *Tofeach al menas lehatfiach* is a fixed halachic phrase. Leave it
  transliterated and gloss it once: damp enough to wet something else.
