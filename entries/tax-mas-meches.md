# Tax: mas / meches (and the Aramaic terms)

## Bottom line

*Mas* is the word typically translated **tax**, and in Modern Hebrew it is the
generic word. Some scholars argue, however, that a more accurate translation is
**corvée work**, compulsory unpaid labor imposed by a sovereign, and the Bible
bears that meaning out in several places: the *sarei missim* over the Jews in
Mitzrayim, and Shlomo HaMelech's conscription. *Meches* is the other Biblical
Hebrew word that sometimes means tax. It appears six times in that sense, all in
Bamidbar 31, for the consecrated share of the Midianite booty given to Hashem.
Its origin is disputed three ways. Dr. Chaim Tawil takes it as a loanword from
an Akkadian word for dues or tributes. Menachem Ibn Saruk, Chayyuj, Ibn Janach
and Radak find a Hebrew root meaning number. For Rabbi Pappenheim that root
means covering, as in *kis*, a pocket, so *meches* is the tax that goes into the
king's own purse. The Aramaic terms are technical, and the Gemara equates
several of them (*belo* is *gulgalta*, which is *karga*; *halach* is *arnona*).

## Distinctions

### mas (מס)
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740 to 1814) offers three possible
etymologies.

- In *Yerios Shlomo*, from the single letter *samech*, rejection and
  disassociation (whence *nas*, fleeing, and *ma'us*, disgusting): paying a tax
  is disassociating oneself from that money and handing it over to the
  authorities.
- In *Cheshek Shlomo*, from מ-ס, melting or disintegrating: taxes melt a man's
  assets away.
- Also in *Cheshek Shlomo*, from ש-א, carrying or lifting: a tax is a burden one
  must carry.

Rabbi Klein adds that the *Oxford English Dictionary* gives the English noun
*tax* two definitions, "a compulsory contribution to state revenue" and "a
strain or heavy demand"; if Rabbi Pappenheim is correct, the second meaning is
actually the basis for the first.

Although *mas* is typically translated "tax," some scholars argue that a more
accurate translation would be "corvée work," a system of compulsory, unpaid
labor or civil service imposed by a sovereign, and that meaning is borne out by
the Bible in several places. The Egyptian taskmasters set over the enslaved Jews
are *sarei missim* (Shemos 1:11), and Shlomo HaMelech's mandatory conscription
of civilians is called a *mas* (Melachim I 5:27).

Shadal (to Shemos 1:11): *mas* is from נ-ש-א, a burden the taxpayer carries, or
because those charged with the tax are lifted up from the rest of the people. He
cites his son Filosseno that Italian *leva*, related to English *levy*, also
means to lift and is used of taxes.

### meches (מכס)
Six appearances in Tanach in the sense of tax, all in one chapter (Bamidbar
31:28-41), of the consecrated booty from the war against Midian, to be given to
Hashem.

Dr. Chaim Tawil of Yeshiva University argues that the Biblical *meches* is a
loanword from the Akkadian *miksu* ("dues" or "tributes"), from the Akkadian
verb *makasu* ("to collect a share from a rented field, to collect taxes,
duty"). Others, however, have found a Hebrew basis for the word.

- Menachem Ibn Saruk (920 to 970) gives the root as כ-ס.
- Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Chayyuj, Rabbi Yonah Ibn Janach (990 to 1050) and Radak (1160
  to 1235) give כ-ס-ס.

Either way these roots mean number. This explains how *meches* was borrowed to
mean a tax, since a rate is usually a function of numbers. The grammarians point
to *michsas* (Shemos 12:4), the number of people collectively offering a single
korban Pesach, as evidence that the root means number.

Rabbi Pappenheim agrees that the root is כ-ס but argues that its core meaning is
not number but covering. *Kis*, a pocket, is a covered space into which one puts
small items such as coins so as not to lose them; so *meches* is specifically
the tax that ends up in the king's pocket or purse, as against other taxes used
for public works. For the Jewish People that King is Hashem, to whom the
*meches* is given. *Michsas* he calls merely a borrowed usage as well: it refers
to several people pooling their money into a single pot or "pocket" to buy the
korban.

### the Aramaic terms in Tanach
The exemption in Ezra 7:24 releases its beneficiaries from *mindah*, *belo* and
*halach*, all Aramaic words.

- Rabbi Baruch Avraham Toledano (1825 to 1917): *mindah* is related to *middah*,
  measure, the fee imposed as a function of the measurement of one's property;
  *belo* is an expression of wearing out and refers to food, which is digested
  and "worn out"; *halach* is related to *holech*, walking or travelling, taxes
  for the upkeep of roads, bridges and the like.
- Bava Basra 8a and Nedarim 62b give a different set: *mindah* is *menas
  hamelech*, the king's portion; *belo* is *gulgalta* money; *halach* is
  *arnona*.

Rabbi Klein remarks that the Gemara's *mindah* seems to be the Aramaic
equivalent of *meches* as Rabbi Pappenheim explains it, a tax for the king's
personal profit.

### the Aramaic terms in Chazal
*Karga*, from the Persian *karaka* and/or the Arabic *harag*, is a poll tax or
head tax, a fixed sum each individual was obligated to pay (see Rashi to Bava
Metzia 73b). As the Gemara's ensuing discussion shows, *gulgalta* means the same
thing, and *gulgalta* is an Aramaicization of the Hebrew *gulgoles*, skull,
since the tax fell on each head.

- *Arnona* is a crop tax levied on farmers: Rashi (Pesachim 6a, Bava Basra 8a)
  has it as a tenth of one's animals and grain every year.
- Rabbi Klein adds that historians record a tax in the Roman Empire called
  *annona*, used to supply grain and other foodstuffs to the city of Rome, and
  named from the Latin *annus*, year (whence English *annual*), because it was
  calculated from the sum total of the year's harvests.
- Alternatively, Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) explains *arnona* as the
  meal each city had to supply to the king or army travelling through it (Rabbi
  Klein glosses the travelling as *halach*, going). Rabbeinu Nissim (Nedarim
  62b) offers two ways of fitting that explanation to the word: *arnona* is
  Greek for a meal; or it means partnership, as the Arnon river was the border
  between Moav and the Emori (Bamidbar 21:13), in effect joining the two
  territories. Maharam Chalavah and Meiri (to Pesachim 6a) also cite these two
  explanations.

*Taska* is a property tax (see Rashi to Gittin 58b, Bava Metzia 73b and 108a).
Elsewhere the word means a sack (Rashi to Megillah 7b) or a basket (Tosafos to
Avodah Zarah 14b), so it seems that the tax sense is simply a borrowed meaning.

In Modern Hebrew the words have shifted: *mas* is the generic word for tax,
*meches* means customs duty, and *arnona* has been redefined as property tax in
place of *taska*.

## Rendering guidance

- Shemos 1:11, *sarei missim*: "taskmasters" or "officers over the forced
  labor," not "tax officers." Melachim I 5:27 likewise, "levy" in the sense of
  conscription.
- Bamidbar 31:28, *vaharemosa meches laHashem*: "you shall raise up a **levy**
  for Hashem" or "a tribute." Rabbi Klein counts these as *meches* in the sense
  of tax.
- Trap: English "tax" will absorb *mas*, *meches* and every Aramaic term at once,
  and it will hide the fact that *mas*, on some scholars' reading, is frequently
  labor. Decide first whether the passage means money or men.
- Trap: "levy" is a good rendering for both *mas* (a levy of men) and *meches*
  (a levy for Hashem). If both words appear in one discussion, keep "levy" for
  one only and choose "tribute" or "conscription" for the other.
- The Aramaic terms of Ezra 7:24 and the sugya in Bava Basra 8a: transliterate
  and gloss on first use, and name the reading you are following, since the
  Gemara and Rabbi Toledano do not divide them the same way.
