# Stoning: sekilah / regimah (margeimah, regem)

## Bottom line

Both mean **stoning**, and Targum collapses them (it renders *sekilah* as *regimah*
every time), so the difference has to be carried by the English. *Sekilah* names the act
of throwing stones and does not by itself mean the man died, which is why the Torah adds
*mos yumas* alongside it; render it **stoning** or **pelting with stones**. *Regimah*
names the outcome, a man buried and subdued under a heap thrown by the whole community;
render it **stoning to death**. The same root ס-ק-ל also means clearing a field of
stones, the exact opposite use of stones, so context alone decides.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| סקילה | sekilah | stoning, pelting | the act of throwing; not necessarily fatal, hence the added "he shall be put to death" (Yashar); could in theory be done with one stone by one man (Shapira-Frankfurter) |
| סקל | sakel | clear of stones | the same root used in reverse, removing stones from a field or road (Yeshayahu 5:2, 62:10) |
| רגימה | regimah | stoning to death | the result: subdued and immobilized (Pappenheim); a heap thrown by "the whole congregation" (Shapira-Frankfurter) |
| מרגמה | margeimah | sling, catapult | the launching device (Mishlei 26:8) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which word is the act and which the result.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: *sekilah* is the
throwing, *regimah* the state of the one stoned, subdued and immobile. Yashar (Rabbi
Yitzchak Shmuel Reggio, cited in *HaKsav VeHaKabbalah* to Vayikra 24:16) says the same in
other terms: *sekilah* is stoning with no implication of death, *regimah* is stoning to
death. *Sefer HaChachmah* (ascribed to Rabbi Elazar Rokeach of Worms) reverses them:
*regimah* is the throwing, *sekilah* the resulting pile of stones. Rabbi Yehuda Leib
Shapira-Frankfurter locates the difference in scale: *sekilah* is a means to killing and
could be done with a single stone, while *regimah* means heaping stone on stone even on a
man already dead, which is why the sources that use it say the whole nation did it. Rabbi
Moshe Tedeschi Ashkenazi (*Otzar Nirdafim*) attempts a distinction and concedes he cannot
draw a line that holds in usage. The Bottom line follows Pappenheim and Yashar, whose
reading is confirmed by the Torah's habit of adding "he shall be put to death" to
*sekilah* and not to *regimah*.

**Why Yehoshua 7:25 uses both on Achan.** Rashi (with Targum and Radak there): Achan took
*regimah*, his animals *sekilah*. Yashar: both fell on Achan, first *regimah*, which
killed him, then *sekilah* on his remains, to disgrace him.

**Whether the two senses of ס-ק-ל are one root.** Ibn Saruk (*Machberes Menachem*) lists
stoning and clearing as separate entries. Ibn Janach and Radak (each in *Sefer
HaShorashim*) treat them as one root carrying opposite senses, using stones and getting
rid of them. Pappenheim bridges them: both are throwing stones, at a man or away from a
field. Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg takes the clearing sense as a metathesis of Mishnaic
ס-ל-ק, to remove. Tedeschi finds the common core in amassing a quantity of stones.

## Distinctions

### sekilah (סקילה)
Twenty occurrences in the sense of stoning: for approaching Har Sinai (Shemos 19:13),
the goring ox (Shemos 21:28-32), the idolater (Devarim 17:5) and the *meisis* (Devarim
13:11), the betrothed *naarah* and her paramour (Devarim 22:21, 22:24). It is also the
word where nobody dies: Dovid HaMelech feared being stoned (Shmuel I 30:6) and was in
fact stoned by Shimi ben Geira and survived (Shmuel II 16:6, 16:13). Fatal instances:
Achan (Yehoshua 7:25) and Navos (Melachim I 21:10-15; the article prints 23:10-15).
Twice the root means the reverse, clearing stones away: Yeshayahu 62:10 and the vineyard
mashal of Yeshayahu 5:2, a usage that carries into Mishnah Shevi'is 2:3 and 3:7.

### regimah (רגימה)
Sixteen occurrences, all fatal stoning: the *megadef* of Vayikra
24:10-23 (four times), the *mekoshesh* (Bamidbar 15:35-36), one who gives his child to
Molech (Vayikra 20:2), one who turns to Ov and Yidoni (Vayikra 20:27), the *ben sorer
umoreh* (Devarim 21:21). What the people wanted to do to Yehoshua and Kalev (Bamidbar
14:10); what they did to Rechavam's tax collector (Melachim I 12:18) and, in Yoash's
day, to Zecharia ben Yehoyada (Divrei HaYamim II 24:21); and what Yechezkel foretells
for a faithless nation (16:40, 23:47). In Mishnah: the crowd pelting the Tzedoki Kohen
with their esrogim at *nisuch hamayim* (Sukkah 4:9), and Sanhedrin 6:4, where a man who
survives the push and the boulder is stoned by all Israel. Pappenheim treats the mem as
non-essential and traces it to the biliteral ר-ג, subduing, with *harigah* and *arigah*
in the same family. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 26:1) links ר-ג-מ to ר-ק-מ, embroidery, both
putting something on top of what is there.

### margeimah (מרגמה) and the wider root
Mishlei 26:8: "like binding a stone in a sling, so is one who gives honor to a fool," and
Chullin 133a hears the same root in throwing a stone to Merculis. Two Rabbinic words are
traced to ר-ג-מ: *argaman*, purple cloth, which Rabbi Yosef Kimchi derives from the
"officer" sense, since only high officials wore it, a view Meiri adopts and Radak cites;
and *targum*, translation (Ezra 4:7), explained variously as transferring a text from place
to place, as speech woven like *rikmah*, or as a metathesis of Aramaic ג-מ-ר, to learn.

## Rendering guidance

- Default: *sekilah* **stoning**, *regimah* **stoning to death**. Where both appear in one
  pasuk (Yehoshua 7:25) they must read differently in English, or the mefarshim there
  become unintelligible.
- The trap is "stoning," which absorbs both; and the opposite trap is ס-ק-ל in a field,
  where Yeshayahu 62:10 means **clear it of stones** and "stone" inverts the pasuk.
- Where *sekilah* did not kill (Shmuel II 16:6), write **pelted him with stones**, not
  "stoned him," which in English implies death.
- *Margeimah* (Mishlei 26:8) is a **sling**, an instrument, not an act. And in a Targum
  *regimah* is simply the Aramaic for *sekilah* (*Meturgaman*), so do not read into the
  Aramaic a distinction that the Hebrew carries.
