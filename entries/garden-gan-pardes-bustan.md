# Garden: gan / pardes / bustan

## Bottom line

*Gan* is the general **garden**, an enclosed and guarded plot; in Tanach it is
the only common one of the three, and in the Mishnah it appears as *ginah*.
*Pardes* is the narrower and grander word, usually **orchard**, and Koheles names
both in one pasuk (Koheles 2:5), which is the proof they are not synonyms.
*Bustan* is Talmudic Aramaic for a **garden**, a Persian word, and Rashi defines
it by *pardes* while Rashbam defines it by *gan*. The one thing not to get wrong:
never render *pardes* as "paradise" in a pasuk, an English word that reached us
through the Septuagint and reads a later theology back into the text.

## Distinctions

### gan (גן)
Over forty times in Tanach: *Gan Eden* (Bereishis 2:15), the garden of Hashem
(Bereishis 13:10; Yeshayahu 51:3; Yechezkel 28:13, 31:8-9), the garden of the
king (Melachim II 25:4; Yirmiyahu 39:4, 52:7; Nechemiah 3:15). It is absent from
the Mishnah, which uses *ginah* (Kilayim 1:2; Shabbos 9:2; Bava Basra 6:1, and
many more). On the root:

- **Guarding.** Menachem Ibn Saruk traces *gan* to the biliteral ג-נ, Ibn Janach
  and Radak to ג-נ-נ, and both to the same root as *haganah*, protection: a
  garden is what a man fences and guards so that what grows in it is not
  trampled. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) makes it explicit, with
  *yagen* (Yeshayahu 31:5), *migen* (Bereishis 14:20), and *magen*, a shield, on
  the same root; he adds *agan*, not any vessel but the one used for watering a
  garden (Shemos 24:6; Shir HaShirim 7:3), and *niggun*, whose variety of sounds
  answers to a garden's variety of growing things. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 2:8)
  notes the same pairing in English, *garden* and *guard*. Rabbi Aharon Marcus
  adds *ganav*, a thief, who hides himself as a garden is hidden from those shut
  out of it; that derivation is open to him, since he lets a biliteral root take
  any letter, and closed to Rabbi Pappenheim, whose system allows only certain
  ones.
- **Softness.** In *Yerios Shlomo*, Rabbi Pappenheim instead traces *gan* to the
  single letter *gimmel*, whose core he gives as softening or melting (with
  precedent in *Machberes Menachem* and in Rashi to Tzefaniah 3:18 and Eichah
  1:4). On that root he places *hismogeg*, *yagon*, *niggun* again (a tune melts
  sorrow away), *gai*, *gas*, *gigis*, *magen*, *gei'eh*, and *gag*; and *gan*,
  the plot whose soil is soft enough for things to grow in it. Rabbi Yehudah
  Aryeh of Carpentras (*Ohalei Yehuda*) offers both cores at once: a *gan* is a
  stand of trees whose shade protects from the sun, or else the place whose ease
  and sweet air cure a man whose heart was *hismogeg*.
- **Related words without a gimmel.** Radak (to Tehillim 80:16) reads *kanah*
  with a *kaf* as a form of *gan*, though in *Sefer HaShorashim* he joins it to
  כ-ו-ן. Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 6:14) reads *ken*, a nest or dwelling, as a
  stronger *gan*: a *gan* is closed off by barriers, a *ken* more enclosed still.

### pardes (פרדס)
Three times in Tanach: Shir HaShirim 4:13, Koheles 2:5, Nechemiah 2:8. Koheles
plants both *ginos* and *pardeisim*, so the two are not one word:

- Rabbi Yosef Kara and *Metzudas Tzion* (to Koheles 2:5): a *gan* or *ginah* is
  for vegetables and flowers, a *pardes* for fruit trees, that is, an orchard.
- Ibn Ezra (to Koheles 2:5 and Shir HaShirim 4:13): both are orchards, but a
  *gan* holds several species and a *pardes* only one. Rabbi Shlomo Aharon
  Wertheimer supports him from the Gemara, where an unqualified *pardes* is a
  pomegranate orchard (Bava Metzia 104a; Bava Basra 7a), as in *pardes rimonim*
  itself.
- Rabbi Avraham Bedersi (*Chosam Tochnis*), on his rule that a pasuk lists near
  synonyms in ascending order, infers from Koheles naming *gan* first that a
  *pardes* is the more esteemed: a *gan* grows all sorts, often for profit, a
  *pardes* is an exclusive plot of one species where royalty takes its pleasure.
  His son Rabbi Yedaiah HaPenini (introduction to *Sefer HaPardes*) makes a
  *pardes* smaller than a *gan*, and Rabbi Wertheimer supports him from the
  Gemara's diminutive *ginunyisa* for a small garden (Bava Basra 68a).
- Rabbi David Luria, against Ibn Ezra, holds a *pardes* is exactly the orchard of
  many species, and on that basis derives it from Hebrew פ-ר-ד, separation, the
  species standing in their separate rows. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras
  likewise looks inward, reading *pardes* as *pri* joined to one of *hidus* (Bava
  Kamma 2:1), *rar*, *dar*, *hadar*, *dash*, or *datz*.
- Against all of these stands the four-letter root itself. Ibn Ezra and Rabbi
  Eliyahu HaBachur both hold that a Hebrew word built on four letters is usually
  a loanword, and Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*) already noted an Arabic
  cognate; Rabbi Ernest Klein gives the original as Old Persian *pairidaeza*, an
  enclosure, the name of the boundary passing to what grew inside it.
- As a place rather than a plot: Chagigah 14b, the four who entered the *Pardes*,
  which Rashi explains as Heaven. The Arizal reads *PaRDeS* as *pshat*, *remez*,
  *drush*, *sod*; the Ramak puts *rayah* for *remez*, the Rema *seser* for *sod*;
  for the Rambam see Yesodei HaTorah 4:13 and Talmud Torah 1:12. Chazal also read
  Avraham's *eshel* (Bereishis 21:33) as a whole *pardes* rather than one tree
  (Sotah 10a; Targum Yonasan and Targum Yerushalmi there).

### bustan (בוסתן)
The ordinary word for a garden in the Aramaic of the Gemara: David HaMelech's
gardens (Shabbos 30b), Achashverosh's (Megillah 16a), the Reish Galusa's (Eruvin
25b), and in *Targum Sheini* to Esther 3:8. Rashi (to Eruvin 25b and Bava Metzia
22a) defines it with *pardes*; Rashbam (to Bava Basra 61b) defines it with *gan*,
so the word itself does not decide between them. It is a Persian word, which
Rabbi Ernest Klein parses as "place of fragrance," and it served as a personal
name too: Bustanai, the Reish Galusa of seventh-century Bavel, named for the
garden in the Persian king's dream (*Seder Olam Zuta*; *Seder HaDoros*).

## Rendering guidance

- *Gan* in Tanach and *ginah* in the Mishnah: "garden" for both; the shift is one
  of stratum, not sense. *Pardes*: "orchard," and "park" only where the passage
  is about royal pleasure grounds and Bedersi's reading is in play.
- Koheles 2:5, where both appear: "gardens and orchards." This is the one pasuk
  where a single English word for both destroys the verse. *Bustan*: "garden,"
  noting that Rashi glosses it *pardes* and Rashbam *gan*.
- Trap: English "garden" absorbs all three, and "paradise" absorbs *pardes* and
  *Gan Eden* together. In Chagigah 14b keep "the *Pardes*" untranslated; the
  Gemara is naming a place, and "paradise" hands the reader a foreign picture.
- Second trap: a source building on *gan* and *haganah*, or *gan* and *ken*,
  cannot survive in English; keep the Hebrew and put the link in a note.
