# Blue: sapir / techeiles / kachol

## Bottom line

The Torah has no word for the color blue. *Sapir* is a **stone**, *techeiles* a
**dyed wool**, and *kachol* comes from the name of a cosmetic powder; its range runs to dark
and black.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| ספיר | sapir | sapphire | a gemstone, the color implied by the object (Shemos 24:10) |
| תכלת | techeiles | techeiles | wool dyed from the chilazon; its shade Rashi calls *yarok* |
| כחול | kachol | dark, kohl-colored | named from the kohl powder, shades running blue to black |
| כרתי | karti | indigo dye | the dye called *Hindi* (Rabbeinu Yonah; Tosafos); told apart from *techeiles* (Berachos 9b) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**What shade techeiles is.**

- Rashi (to Bamidbar 15:38 and Shemos 25:4) defines *techeiles* as wool dyed
  with the blood of the chilazon, and calls its color *yarok*, which in his
  usage is a catch-all covering the band from orange to violet, with yellow,
  green, blue and indigo in between, so his definition does not settle the
  shade.
- The Midrash fixes it by comparison instead: *techeiles* resembles the color of
  the *sapir* stone, which is why looking at the tzitzis recalls the whole
  Torah, the Luchos having been hewn from *sapir*. The Midrash's comparison is
  to an object and not to a name for a hue.

On the breadth of *yarok*, see the entry on *yerek*/*yarok*.

**Whether kachol is blue.**

- Rashi (Bava Basra 4a and Sukkah 51b) explains *avnei kochla*, among the
  materials with which, on one opinion in the Gemara, Herod adorned the Beis
  HaMikdash, as marble that is *kachol*, apparently blue; compare the Ramban to
  Bereishis 49:12.
- Against a simple identification with blue, Rabbi Klein holds that there are
  places where *kachol* clearly means black and not blue, and gives as his
  example the Midrash (Bamidbar Rabbah) on the tribal flags, where Yissachar's
  is *kachol* and Dan's is *sapir*. Besides that, the word itself comes from
  kohl, the antimony powder applied around the eye for healing or adornment,
  whose hues ran widely through the dark colors from blue to black, so that it
  certainly does not mean "blue" alone. The Gemara's *kimachol bishfoferes*,
  "like kohl in its tube," refers to the applicator.

Two further arguments come from outside the traditional sources.

- Frankie Snyder, an expert in the tiles found at the Temple's site, wrote to
  Rabbi Klein that archaeologists have found several colors of marble from
  Herod's Temple but no blue marble; Rabbi Klein takes this to suggest that
  *kachol* does not really mean blue.
- And Professor Aharon Demsky of Bar Ilan University points to an ancient wine
  decanter found near Chevron inscribed "[Belonging] to Yachzeyahu, wine
  *kachol*"; Rabbi Klein, who has never heard of blue wine, takes the
  inscription most likely to mean dark wine, and sees in it support for his view
  that *kachol* originally meant black or dark rather than blue.

## Distinctions

### sapir (ספיר)
The second stone of the second row of the Choshen HaMishpat (Shemos 28:18,
39:11). Under the Kisei HaKavod at Sinai was what looked like *livnas hasapir*,
resembling the clear sky (Shemos 24:10), and Yechezkel's vision of the throne
has the likeness of a *sapir* stone (Yechezkel 1:26, 10:1); *sapir* is also
among the seven precious stones of Gan Eden in the rebuke of the king of Tzor
(Yechezkel 28:13). Targum renders *even sapir* simply as *even tava*, a good
stone, without naming a hue. Which gemstone it is remains open:
gemologists tend to identify *sapir* with lapis lazuli or with the
sapphire, both blue jewels. Rabbeinu Bachya: the *sapir* on the Choshen
corresponds to Yissachar, who toiled in Torah, since the Luchos of *sapir* were
Torah made physical; tradition holds that Moshe's staff was *sapir* as well. In
Rabbinic literature the word is largely displaced by Aramaic variants,
*sampiryon*, *sanpiryon*, *sampirinon*, *sanpirinon*, which seem to be Aramaic
variants of the Hebrew. Etymologists admit that the ultimate
source of the word is obscure: some claim it is a Sanskrit loanword, others
prefer to see it as a conjugation of the Aramaic *shappir*, "beautiful" or
"good," applied to a gem, adapted into Greek and reborrowed from there.

### techeiles (תכלת)
The Torah commands that the tzitzis carry *techeiles* so that one will remember
all the mitzvos (Bamidbar 15:38). Rashi, as above: wool dyed with the blood of
the chilazon. What the chilazon is remains unsettled: some claim it might
be the *murex trunculus*, a marine snail found off the coast of Eretz Yisrael, or
the *sepia officinalis*, the common cuttlefish, though traditional sources assert
that it is something hidden from us, so that we do not know exactly what it is.

Beside *techeiles* the Gemara sets *karti*, which one must be able to tell apart
from it (Berachos 9b). Rabbeinu Yonah (to Berachos) and Tosafos and Tosafos
HaRosh (to Chullin 47b) identify *karti* as the dye called *Hindi*, in Tosafos's Old French *indis*, indigo.

### kachol (כחול)
Not a Biblical color word. Its origins are in Arabic, where it was pronounced
*kohl*: the silverish antimony powder applied around the eye for medicinal or
cosmetic purposes, mentioned many times in the Gemara and generally applied with a
special tube. Its use as the ordinary Hebrew word for the color blue is modern.

## Rendering guidance

- ספיר: "sapphire," and where the passage is describing appearance rather than
  material, add what the pasuk itself adds ("like the clear sky," Shemos 24:10)
  rather than supplying a hue.
- תכלת: keep it transliterated, *techeiles*, glossed once as the dye of the
  chilazon commanded for tzitzis. "Blue" is a translation of a guess.
- כחול: "dark," or "kohl-colored" where the cosmetic is in view; "blue" only
  following Rashi on the marble.
- Trap: English "blue" will absorb all three, and a reader will conclude the
  Torah names a color. Where two of them appear together, the pairing is the
  point: the Midrash comparing *techeiles* to *sapir* is comparing a dye to a
  stone, and the flags of Yissachar and Dan are *kachol* against *sapir*.
  Different English for each, every time.
- In a passage where Rashi glosses *techeiles* as *yarok*, do not render *yarok*
  "green." His *yarok* is a wide band, and "green" makes his gloss look like a
  contradiction of the Midrash.
