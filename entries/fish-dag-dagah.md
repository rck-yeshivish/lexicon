# Fish: dag / dagah

## Bottom line

*Dag* is **a fish**, the singular; *dagah* is the loaded form, and what it adds
is disputed: **a large fish or a small one** (Chazal), **a female fish**
(Rashi and others), **a dead fish** (Zohar), or **fish as a collective**, the
species rather than the individual (Ibn Ezra). Most of the sources that take up
the difference focus on the switch from *dag* to *dagah* in Yonah 2:1 and 2:2.
Rashi glosses several Aramaic words in the Gemara (*avruma*, *munini*, *gildna*,
*tzachanta*) as **small fish**.

## Distinctions

### dag (דג)
The plain singular. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to the biliteral ד-ג,
fecundity and increase in number, fish being famously prolific; from the same
root he takes *dayag* (Yeshayahu 19:8) and *davag* (Yirmiyahu 16:16),
fisherman, and *dugah* (Amos 4:2), a fishing boat. Ibn Chayyuj, Ibn Janach, and
Radak treat the vav in *davag* as root-letter. In Nechemiah 13:16 the word is
written דאג though read *dag*: Ibn Ezra (there and in *Sefer Tzachus*) takes the
root as triliteral ד-י-ג or ד-ו-ג with an aleph standing in for the middle
letter; the Rasag commentary there reads the aleph as a derashah, that trading on
Shabbos brought extra *da'agah*, worry, into the world.

### dagah (דגה)
- Chazal (Nedarim 51b): *dag* is a big fish; *dagah* covers big and small alike.
  Hence Yonah's two fish, the second smaller than the first, and hence *dagah*
  for the fish of the Nile that died in Makas Dam (Shemos 7:18), where big and
  small perished together. On this reading the *dagah* the people remembered
  eating in Mitzrayim (Bamidbar 11:5) could be either.
- Other sources, among them Rashi (to Yonah 2:1) and *Yalkut Shimoni* 550: *dag*
  is a male fish, *dagah* a female. The first fish's belly was roomy and Yonah was too much at ease, so
  Hashem had it spit him out into a pregnant female whose cavity was cramped,
  and only then did he daven. The difficulty, which Rabbi Klein raises, is why
  only female fish would be eaten in Mitzrayim or killed in the plague. (It may
  suit the reading in Yoma 75a that "eating *dagah*" in Bamidbar 11:5 is not
  about food at all but about the illicit intimacy the people were free to
  engage in there.)
- Zohar (Beshalach 47b-48a), followed by Rabbeinu Bachaya (to Bamidbar 11:5 and
  in *Kad HaKemach*; cf. his comments to Bamidbar 22:33) and Rabbi Avraham bar
  Chiya HaNasi (*Higayon HaNefesh*): *dag* is a living fish, *dagah* a dead one.
  Hashem killed the fish around Yonah to make him uncomfortable enough to
  daven; and the Zohar further adduces the reading from Shemos 7:18, which uses
  *dagah* for the dead fish of the plague. On this reading it seems that the
  people in the midbar recalled eating *dagah* because they ate dead fish, one
  may not eat a live fish (see Rema, Yoreh Deah 13:1).
- Ibn Ezra (to Shemos 7:18), and see Rashi to Yirmiyahu 6:6 and Malbim to Yonah
  2:2: *dagah* is the collective, fish as a species rather than an individual
  fish. This explains Shemos and Bamidbar (fish of every kind) but not the
  switch in Yonah.
- Rabbi Mordechai Gimpel Yaffe takes the opposite line: *dagah* is one
  particular Egyptian fish, which is why both Bamidbar 11:5 and Shemos 7:18
  prefix it with the definite hei. He too leaves Yonah unexplained.

### the verb (ויִדגו)
Yaakov blesses Yosef's sons *v'yidgu*, may they multiply like fish (Bereishis
48:16). Rav Hirsch notes that this is the only verbal use of ד-ג in all of
Chumash; everywhere else the cognates are nouns.
- Among the earlier grammarians, Menachem alone derives the verb from the same
  root as *dag*.
- Ibn Janach and Radak assign it a triliteral ד-ג-ה, though Radak seems the more
  open to connecting it to *dag*.
- Rabbi Yehuda Ibn Balaam lists ד-ג-ה among verbs formed from nouns.
- Rabbi Aharon Marcus reverses the direction, making fecundity primary and
  "fish" the borrowed sense, and uses this to account for Chumash never saying
  *dag/dagim*, only *dagah*, *digas*, and *v'yidgu*. (Rabbi Klein himself points
  out the counterexample: *digei* in Bereishis 9:2, apparently the construct of
  *dag*.)

### the wider family
Rabbi Pappenheim also attaches two further words to ד-ג that the other
commentators do not necessarily see as related. *Da'agah*, worry: an
apprehension resting on slight grounds, like the fisherman who picks his spot on
faint signs with no real proof that fish are there. *Dagan*, grain: an especially
fecund plant, one seed begetting many, as one fish fathers many. Menachem,
Ibn Janach, and Radak each give these their own triliteral roots, ד-א-ג and
ד-ג-נ. *Dugmah*, specimen (Shabbos 10:1, Eduyos 5:6, and often in the Gemara),
is said to be a loanword from the Greek *deigma*; but given Rabbi Pappenheim's
biliteral, Rabbi Klein conjectures that it may derive from ד-ג too, an example
being a mere sampling of a quantitatively larger pool, with the mem extraneous
to the root.

As an aside, Menachem does agree that the name of the Philistine god Dagon
derives from the same root as *dag*, the idol that represented that deity having
been fish-shaped; Rabbi Klein treats the deity at length in the encyclopedic
section of his *God versus Gods: Judaism in the Idolatry* (Mosaica
Press, 2018).

### the Aramaic small fish
Rashi glosses several Aramaic words as "small fish": *avruma* (Succah 18a;
*avdumah* in *Sefer HaAruch*); *munini* (Sanhedrin 49a, Avodah Zarah 29a,
Kesubos 60b), which also names fish brine (Rashi to Shabbos 105b, Gittin 69b)
and by extension brine as such (Shabbos 110b, "*munini* of grasshoppers");
*gildna*, elsewhere a particular fish (Rashi to Sanhedrin 100b, Rabbeinu
Gershom and Rashbam to Bava Basra 73b) and elsewhere small fish (Rashi to
Horayos 12a, Berachos 44b, Kesubos 105b); and *tzachanta* (Rashi to Succah 18a,
Sanhedrin 49a), which Yerushalmi Nedarim 6:3 gives as the Bavel name for the
*tarit*. On *munini*: Rabbi David Golomb parses it as a diminutive mem plus the
Aramaic *nun*, fish; Rabbi Moshe Batzri as a contraction of *mei nuna*, fish
water. Rashi identifies *tarit* as tuna (Megillah 6a, Berachos 44a), and likewise
*sultanit* and the Spanish *kulyis* (Shabbos 39a, Chullin 66a). On the species: ichthyologists, mentioned by Dr. Moshe Raanan, identify *gildna*
with the flathead grey mullet (*Mugil cephalus*) or with gilt-head bream
(*Sparus aurata*), and modern scholars, also cited by Dr. Raanan, identify
*tzachanta* with either sardines or anchovies.

## Rendering guidance

- *Dag*: "fish" (singular, a fish). *Dagah*: "fish" as well in plain narrative,
  but where the passage or a commentary is trading on the form, say what it
  means: "the fish, big and small," "a female fish," "a dead fish," "the fish"
  as a collective.
- Trap: English "fish" is singular and plural at once, so it will absorb *dag*,
  *dagah*, and *dagim* without a trace. In Yonah 2:1-2 that is fatal; the entire
  midrashic reading hangs on the one-letter change. Render "a fish" then "the
  fish" and carry the distinction in a note, or spell it out in the body.
- Trap: *da'agah* (worry) and *dagah* (fish) look alike and, for Rabbi
  Pappenheim, are related. Do not let a wordplay on the pair pass as an
  etymological claim in a translation; the mainstream lexicographers give them
  separate roots.
- *V'yidgu* (Bereishis 48:16) is a verb, not a noun: "may they multiply like
  fish," not "may they be fish."
- The Aramaic diminutives all render "small fish." Where a sugya turns on the
  species (kashrus signs, brine), keep the Aramaic transliterated and gloss it
  once, rather than committing to a modern species name in the body.
