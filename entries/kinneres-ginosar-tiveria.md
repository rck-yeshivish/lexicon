# Kinneres: Kinneret / Ginosar / Yamah shel Tiveria

## Bottom line

One lake, three names, each seemingly taken from whichever city on its shore was
most prominent at the time: **Kinneret** in Tanach, **Ginosar** in the Targumim
and in the works of that era, Josephus included, and **Yamah shel Tiveria**, the
Sea of Tiberias, in the Gemara. It seems, Rabbi Klein says, that the lake has no
name of its own at all. "The Sea of Galilee" is a regional label and
not the term any of these sources uses.

| גניסר | Ginosar | Ginosar | the same city renamed (Megillah 6a); the Targumim's rendering of Yam Kinneres |
| טבריה | Tiveria | Tiberias | the Talmudic name, once Tiveria became the leading city on the shore |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which name to write in a get.** A *get* must name the city where it is written
and fix its location by the nearest body of water, so a *get* written in Tiveria
must name this lake. Which of its three names is the correct one was disputed at
length between Rabbi Yosef Karo and Rabbi Moshe ben Yosef of Trani, who sat
together on the beis din of Tzefas.

**Where the name Kinneres comes from.**

- A popular account says the lake is called *Kinneres* because its pear shape
  resembles a *kinnor*, a musical instrument. There is no real source for this.
- The Gemara (*Megillah* 6a), which identifies the Biblical Kinneres with
  Ginosar, derives the name differently and applies it to the city, not the
  lake: its fruits are as sweet as the sound of a *kinnor*.
- Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*) defines *kinnor* either as a kind of
  berry, which Jastrow identifies as a thorn jujube (compare Rashi to *Bava
  Basra* 48b), or as a musical instrument, a harp or lyre.

**Whether Tiveria is a Biblical city.** Two accounts stand against each other.

- Josephus (*Antiquities of the Jews* XVIII:8) has Herod, the Herodian tetrarch
  of the Galilee, found the city and name it in honor of the Roman emperor
  Tiberius.
- The Gemara (*Megillah* 5b-6a), on the other hand, identifies it with one of
  two cities named in Yehoshua 19:35, *Chamas* or *Rakas*: *Chamas*, "hot," for
  the natural hot springs there, and *Rakas*, "empty," because even the empty
  and unlearned among its people were as full of mitzvos as a pomegranate is of
  seeds. For the name Tiveria itself the Gemara gives two accounts: the city
  sits at the *tabur*, the navel of Eretz Yisrael, in importance rather than in
  geography, and the name is *tovah re'iyasah*, "its sight is good," which
  Tosafos explains as its beauty, its gardens and orchards.

**Why the rabbinic sources drop the name Kinneres.** Archaeologists at ancient
Ugarit (Ras Shamra, in modern Syria) found a list of old Canaanite gods carrying
a god named Kinnaru, and *kinnor* appears in Ugaritic for a stringed instrument
in the same sense as the Hebrew; on this some academic scholars have argued that
Kinnaru was the Canaanite god of music and that the city Kinneres was named after
him. The late Dr. Dov Ginzberg of the Geological Survey of Israel argued from
this that perhaps Chazal avoided the Biblical name because of its idolatrous
origins and named the lake instead for one of the Jewish cities nearby, Ginosar
or Tiveria. Rabbi Klein presents all of this as a possibility only, and adds that
if nothing else, perhaps it accounts for the city Kinneres being renamed Ginosar.

## Distinctions

### Kinneres (כנרת)
The name the Torah uses in fixing the eastern border of Eretz Yisrael (Bamidbar
34:11; Devarim 3:17; Yehoshua 12:3, 13:27). Yehoshua 19:35 lists a fortified city
Kinneres in the territory of Naftali, captured in Yehoshua's day, and
this city's name turns up in various ancient inscriptions as well. The
lake is called the Sea of Kinneres because that city was then the leading
settlement on its shore. Topographically it is a lake, not a sea, but *yam*
covers both.

### Ginosar (גניסר)
The Targumim render Kinneres as Ginosar and Yam Kinneres as Yam Ginosar, and
Josephus (*War of the Jews* III:10) likewise calls it the Lake of Genezareth, an
anglicization of the Greek form of Ginosar. The Gemara (*Megillah* 6a) states
that the Biblical city Kinneres is Ginosar. The fruit of Ginosar is praised in
the highest terms (*Berachos* 44a), and the Midrash (*Bereishis Rabbah* 98:17)
expounds the name as *ganei sarim*, gardens of officers, for the fertility of
that land and the fruit it produced. By the Second Temple period the city was
known as Ginosar and still prominent, so the lake took its name, and that is the
term the works of that era use.

### Yamah shel Tiveria (ימה של טבריה)
The Bavli calls the lake *Yamah shel Tiveria* (*Shabbos* 87a-87b, *Moed Katan*
18b, *Bava Kamma* 81a-81b, *Bava Basra* 74, *Bechoros* 55a) and the Yerushalmi
*Yam shel Tiveria* (*Shekalim* 6:2, *Bava Basra* 5:1); both mean the Sea of
Tiberias. The shift in name tracks the rise of Tiveria on the western shore past
Kinneres and Ginosar. The Arabic name for the lake, *Buhairet Tabariyya*, carries
the same sense.

### kinnor (כנור)
Ordinarily a stringed instrument. Its place here is only through the Gemara's
derashah on the city's name and the possibility, from Rabbi Nosson of Rome, that
the word also names a berry.

## Rendering guidance

- Use the name the source uses: "Kinneres" in a pasuk, "Ginosar" in a Targum,
  "the Sea of Tiveria" in a sugya. Substituting one for another silently erases
  the chronology, which is Rabbi Klein's whole point.
- Trap: English "Sea of Galilee" absorbs all three, and it renders none of them.
  Rabbi Klein traces the phrase to the Christian Bible, which occasionally calls
  the lake the Sea of Tiberias but more often the Sea of Galilee, and which is
  why English speakers know it by that name; Galilee was the administrative name
  of the whole northern region of Eretz Yisrael in Hasmonean and Herodian times,
  so this too, he says, is the lake named for its surroundings rather
  than a name of its own. Keep it out of a Torah text; if a reader needs the
  identification, put it in a note.
- Second trap: *kinnor* elsewhere is a harp or lyre and should stay so. Do not
  import "harp" into the lake's name on the strength of the shape story, for
  which no early source is given.
- *Yam* here is a lake. "Sea" is the conventional rendering and is fine, but
  where the geography matters, say so.
- Chamas and Rakas are city names, not adjectives; keep them transliterated and
  let the derashah on "hot" and "empty" be carried in a note.
