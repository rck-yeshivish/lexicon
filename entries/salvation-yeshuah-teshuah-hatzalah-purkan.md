# Salvation: yeshuah / teshuah / hatzalah / purkan

## Bottom line

*Yeshuah* and *teshuah* are the grand words: **salvation**, total release from
oppression, and specifically salvation that answers a cry (*shav'ah*) for help.
*Hatzalah* is the plain one: **rescue**, being pulled out of danger (root sense
"separate, remove"). Whether it is the lesser word is itself disputed: the
Rokeach calls it partial or temporary next to *yeshuah*, while Malbim has it
carrying more, protection from future troubles on top of the saving. *Purkan*
(Aramaic, the Targum's *pidyon*) is **redemption**, being bought back. The one
thing not to get wrong is the English: render *hatzalah* "rescue" or "saving,"
and reserve "salvation" for *yeshuah*/*teshuah*.

## Distinctions

### yeshuah (ישועה) and teshuah (תשועה)
Both are tied to crying out: they name the aspect of salvation that is Hashem's
answer to our prayers and efforts. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos 14:13) suggests that
*shav'ah*, crying out, is the act of yearning for a *teshuah*; unlike other
words for crying out, which may be no more than a verbalizing of one's
suffering, *shav'ah* is an unmistakable cry for salvation, a prayer rather than
a complaint. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: the biliteral ש-ע underlies *yeshuah*,
*teshuah*, and *shav'ah*, and its core is enjoyment and pleasure (so too
*sha'ashua*, playful or delightful; *mishan*, leaning and relying, the
satisfaction of being supported by another; and *sha'ah*, an hour, a unit of
time a person gives to a task he wants to do). The column offers an alternative
of its own, that the core of ש-ע is "smoothness," here the uninterrupted passage
from prayer to salvation.

Rav Hirsch proposes an etymological difference between the two. *Yeshuah*, root
י-ש-ע, is kin to י-ש-ה and to
the biliteral י-ש, "exists": it is the first-order existence Hashem grants, the
salvation from a danger that threatened one's very being. *Teshuah*, root
ש-ו-ע, is kin to ש-ב-ע (seven) and ש-פ-ע (influx): it concerns what is
peripheral to existence, one's property and economic freedom, so it often names
salvation through military victory over outside conquerors.

Rabbi Yechiel Michel Heilpern and Rabbi Chanoch Zundel of Bialystok: *yeshuah*
and *teshuah* are salvation from a grave threat, murder or a crime of a sexual
nature (Devarim 22:27), which is why the Midrash (Shemos Rabbah 1:32) infers
from *vayoshi'an* (Shemos 2:17) that the shepherds meant harm to Yisro's
daughters. Peirush HaRokeach: *yeshuah*/*teshuah* is total and utter salvation
from a state of oppression and subjugation. Malbim argues further that *yeshuah*
is mere salvation from the dire situation itself, without the further protection
*hatzalah* implies.

Bava Kamma 80a mentions a ceremony called *Yeshua HaBen* without saying what it
is. *Sefer HaAruch* and Rashi explain it as the festive meal of a *pidyon haben*
(see also Tosafos there), on the grounds that the Targumim render cognates of
both *yeshuah* and *pidyon* with words of *purkan*.

### hatzalah (הצלה)
Peirush HaRokeach: only a partial or temporary respite from oppression, not a
total release. Malbim (to Yirmiyahu 15:20) adds that *hatzalah* focuses on the
element of salvation that releases a person from mortal danger; the column
suggests that this may be why Hatzolah is the name the volunteer paramedic
organizations took. Elsewhere Malbim argues that *hatzalah*, against *yeshuah*,
implies being saved from the situation plus being protected from future
troubles. Heilpern and Rabbi Chanoch Zundel: *hatzalah* is saving from any
threat, not only the gravest. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim: from the biliteral צ-ל,
"separation for a constructive purpose," so extricating a person from peril;
under the same root he puts *tzel*, a shadow, which appears to separate itself
from a person, *tzli*, roasting, which uses heat to draw the moisture out of raw
food, and *atzal*, bestowal, as when one blesses another. Rav Hirsch (to Shemos
3:22, Devarim 32:2): root נ-צ-ל, kin to נ-ש-ל (remove) and נ-ז-ל (flow away),
so saving someone by removing him from the hazard and separating him from what
endangers him. Radak connects the "saving" and "removing" senses of נ-צ-ל; Ibn
Janach fails to see a thematic connection between them and takes them for two
different meanings carried by one root.

### purkan (פורקן)
The Targum's standard Aramaic for *pidyon*: redemption, Hashem buying us back
from the Greeks after having, as it were, sold us to them by letting them rule
over us. Chazal ordinarily keep the phrasing of the tefillos to Biblical Hebrew,
and used *purkan* here because the root פ-ר-ק already appears in Tanach in the
sense of salvation (Eichah 5:8, Tehillim 136:24); in Biblical Hebrew it also
means to slip something off (*porek*), an apt description of how the Jews of the
Chanukah story shook off the yoke of Syrian-Greek domination and asserted their
own political independence. Given the word's association with financial
transactions, Rabbi Yitzchak Eliyahu Landau of Dubno explains that in *Al
HaNissim* it is deliverance from financial ruin.

## Rendering guidance

- ישועה: "salvation"; in a pasuk, "deliverance" also serves. תשועה: "salvation"
  or "deliverance"; where Rav Hirsch's reading fits (a war, a freedom
  regained), "victory" is defensible with a gloss.
- הצלה: "rescue," "saving," "being saved"; מציל: "rescuer," "one who saves."
  Not "salvation."
- פורקן in a tefillah: "redemption"; leave it beside *teshuah* as two words,
  not one.
- Trap: English "salvation" (and "save") absorbs *yeshuah*, *teshuah*, and
  *hatzalah* alike. In *Al HaNissim* (פורקן ... תשועה) or a derashah on why a
  pasuk says *vayoshi'a* rather than *vayatzel*, keep each Hebrew word its own
  English: purkan "redemption," teshuah "salvation," hatzalah "rescue."
- When the three appear together, keep them apart by aspect, which is how the
  column itself sums up: *purkan* is the redeeming aspect, as though Hashem
  bought us back after proverbially selling us; *teshuah* is salvation as a
  response to our prayers for help; *hatzalah* is salvation as the separating of
  us from a dangerous position.
