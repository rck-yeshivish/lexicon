# Hammer: makeves / patish / kurnas (and halmus)

## Bottom line

*Makeves* is the heavy hammer, **mallet** or **sledgehammer**, the tool Yael used
(Shoftim 4:21); *halmus* is the poetic word for the very same tool (Shoftim
5:26), so render both **mallet**. *Patish* is the plain **hammer**, in the Gemara
the blacksmith's; *kurnas*, Mishnaic only, is the **smith's hammer**. The sources
grade their sizes against each other and contradict each other doing it, so
follow whichever source is being quoted rather than a fixed hierarchy.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מקבת | makeves | mallet, sledgehammer | the heavy one; from נ-ק-ב, what makes a hole (Radak) |
| פטיש | patish | hammer | "a big makeves" (Radak); the blacksmith's (Rashi to Shabbos 21b) |
| קורנס | kurnas | smith's hammer | Mishnaic; big makeves (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur) or small (Sefer HeAruch) |
| הלמות | halmus | mallet | the poetic twin of makeves; from ה-ל-ם, "to break" (Ibn Janach, Radak) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which hammer is bigger.** Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*): *kurnas* is a
big *makeves*. Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*): *kurnas* is a small
*makeves* and a big *patish*. Rashi (to Shabbos 21b): *patish* is a big *kurnas*,
the blacksmith's, though elsewhere (Shabbos 73a) he equates the two. Radak:
*patish* is a big *makeves*. Rabbi Yitzchak Avineri reverses the axis: *makeves*
is a *patish* with one sharp head. Nothing reconciles them, so render each word
by its own character rather than by relative size.

**Where *makeves* comes from.** Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon, and Radak file it under
נ-ק-ב, "to bore," Ibn Parchon explaining that the *dagesh* in the *mem* stands for
a dropped *nun*, as though the word were *menakeves*. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
treats the *mem* as extraneous and reads the biliteral ק-ב, "making a hole," which
also gives *nekev*, *nekeivah*, *kav*, and *yekev*. Menachem Ibn Saruk does not
list the word at all. Either way it is the tool that drives into a hole.

**What *makavan* means** (Bechoros 7:1, a blemish disqualifying a kohen). Bechoros
43b says he is hammer-headed; Rabbeinu Gershom and Rabbi Nosson of Rome, that his
forehead and back-head protrude. Rashi makes it another word for *kardum*.

**Whether Maccabee means "hammer."** With a *kaf* it is an acronym of *mi kamocha
ba'eilim Hashem* (Shemos 15:11): *Peirush Rokeach* there, Rabbi Avraham Saba
(*Tzror HaMor*), Alshich, the Shelah, and *Zohar Chadash* 73b with the *Biur
HaGra*; the Chasam Sofer reads *Matisyahu Kohen ben Yochanan*; the Ramak (*Ohr
Yakar*) and the Maggid of Kozhnitz tie it to *kibui*, extinguishing. With a *kuf*
it is *makeves*, "hammer," already in Rabbi Yochanan Treves. The spelling follows
the derivation; keep whichever the source uses.

## Distinctions

### makeves (מקבת)
Five times in Tanach: Yael's (Shoftim 4:21); the quarry-tool of "the *makeves* of
the pit you were dug from" (Yeshayahu 51:1); the tools not heard at the building
of the Beis HaMikdash (Melachim I 6:7); and twice of making idols (Yeshayahu
44:12; Yirmiyahu 10:4); in the Mishnah, Keilim 29:5-7 and Parah 3:11. Rabbi Moshe
Tedeschi-Ashkenazi: the hammer that wedges a nail into a hole.
### patish (פטיש)
Three times: the artisan flattening metal for an idol (Yeshayahu 41:7); "is My
word not like fire, and like a *patish* that shatters rock" (Yirmiyahu 23:29);
and Bavel as "the *patish* of the whole earth" (Yirmiyahu 50:23). Its root פ-ט-ש
yields nothing else: Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras (*Aholei Yehuda*) reads
the letters as פ-ש-ט, "flattening"; Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi prefers
ט-ו-ש, "to fly" (Iyov 9:26), for the chips that fly when stone is struck.

### kurnas (קורנס)
Absent from Tanach, Hebrew and Aramaic parts alike, but Rashi glosses *makeves* by
it (to Yirmiyahu 10:4), and it is the hammer that beat the Menorah from a single
block of gold (Rashi to Shemos 25:31, Menachos 28a). It is in the Mishnah at
Shabbos 12:1 and Keilim 13:4 and 29:6. Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Mussaf HeAruch*)
holds the word is Greek; others take it as native Aramaic.

### halmus (הלמות)
Once, in Devorah's song: "her hand to the peg, her right hand to the *halmus* of
the toilers, and she struck (*halmah*) Sisera" (Shoftim 5:26). Since the prose
account calls the same tool a *makeves*, the two are one thing, as Ibn Janach and
Radak say outright and as Targum confirms by rendering both *arzafta* (the
Aramaic word, which Rashi glosses *kurnas* at Berachos 34a). The root ה-ל-ם also
gives the verb "to break"; Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim reduces it to the biliteral
ה-ל, "spreading out," as in *yahel*, *yahalom*, *hallel*, and *ohel*, so *halmah*
is smashing that scatters the pieces. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi instead reads
ח-ל-ם, "strong," a hammer that tires even a strong man.

## Rendering guidance

- Shoftim 4:21 and 5:26 are one story told twice. Render מקבת "mallet" and הלמות
  "mallet" too, so the reader sees it is the same tool.
- Trap: English "hammer" absorbs all four. Where a source grades two at once
  (*Sefer HeAruch*; Rashi to Shabbos 21b), keep "hammer" and "sledge" apart.
- קורנס in a Mishnah or in Rashi is "smith's hammer"; the generic "hammer" is
  now פטיש.
- מכבי / מקבי: transliterate "Maccabee"; rendering it "the Hammer" silently
  picks one side of the machlokes.
