# Hammer: makeves / patish / kurnas (and halmus)

## Bottom line

*Makeves* is the heavy hammer, **mallet** or **sledgehammer**, the tool Yael used
(Shoftim 4:21); *halmus* is the poetic word for the very same tool (Shoftim
5:26). *Patish* is the plain **hammer**, for Rashi (to Shabbos 21b)
the blacksmith's; *kurnas*, Mishnaic only, is the **smith's hammer**. The sources
grade their sizes against each other, and HaBachur and the *Aruch* disagree over
whether *kurnas* is a big or a small *makeves*.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| מקבת | makeves | mallet, sledgehammer | the heavy one; from נ-ק-ב, what makes a hole (Radak) |
| פטיש | patish | hammer | "a big makeves" (Radak); the blacksmith's (Rashi to Shabbos 21b) |
| קורנס | kurnas | smith's hammer | Mishnaic; big makeves (Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur) or small (Sefer HeAruch) |
| הלמות | halmus | mallet | the poetic twin of makeves; from ה-ל-ם, "to break" (Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach, Radak) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which hammer is bigger.** Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (*Meturgaman*): *kurnas* is a
big *makeves*. Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HeAruch*): *kurnas* is a small
*makeves* and a big *patish*. Rashi (to Shabbos 21b): *patish* is a big *kurnas*,
the blacksmith's, though elsewhere (Shabbos 73a, Moed Katan 11a) he simply
equates the two. Radak:
*patish* is a big *makeves*. Rabbi Yitzchak Avineri similarly writes that
*makeves* is a type of *patish* with one sharp head.

**Where *makeves* comes from.** Ibn Janach, Ibn Parchon, and Radak file it under
נ-ק-ב, "to bore," Ibn Parchon explaining that the *dagesh* in the *mem* stands for
a dropped *nun*, as though the word were *menakeves*. Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
takes a similar approach but treats the *mem* as extraneous and reads the
biliteral ק-ב, "making a hole," which also gives *nekev* (hole), *nekeivah*
(female), *kav* (a measure taken with a hollowed piece of wood), *yekev* (a
wine-pit), *kubah* (a chamber of hewn stone), *keivah* (the stomach, with a hole
at each end) and *nokev* (stating something explicitly, or cursing, as boring an
opening into what ambiguity had closed). Menachem Ibn Saruk (*Machberes
Menachem*) ignores the word altogether and lists it under no root.

**What *makavan* means** (Bechoros 7:1, a blemish disqualifying a kohen). Bechoros
43b says he is hammer-headed; the Mainz Commentary ascribed to Rabbeinu Gershom
and Rabbi Nosson of Rome (*Sefer HaAruch*), that his forehead and back-head
protrude outwardly so that his head looks like a *makeves*. Rashi takes a
slightly different approach, seeing *makavan* as another word for *kardum*, a
hatchet.

**Whether Maccabee means "hammer."** With a *kaf* it is an acronym of *mi kamocha
ba'eilim Hashem* (Shemos 15:11): *Peirush Rokeach* there, Rabbi Avraham Saba
(*Tzror HaMor*), Alshich, the Shelah, and *Zohar Chadash* 73b with the *Biur
HaGra*; the Chasam Sofer reads *Matisyahu Kohen ben Yochanan*; the Ramak (*Ohr
Yakar*) and the Maggid of Kozhnitz tie it to *kibui*, extinguishing. With a *kuf*
it is *makeves*, "hammer," already in Rabbi Yochanan Treves; on that
understanding Abraham Kahana and Rabbi Eliyahu Shmuel Hartom spelled the word
with a kuf when they translated the apocrypha into Hebrew, explaining that
Yehudah was called Maccabee because he fought like a hammer smashing its
opponents, which Kahana compares to the Frankish king Charles Martel, *martel*
being Old French for hammer. Rabbi Hartom also floats two further readings: that
the nickname was given at birth because Yehudah's head was shaped like a hammer
(compare *makavan* above), and that Maccabee is a shortened *makav-yahu*, from
*nakav* in the sense of marking or designating, "the one designated by Hashem."

## Distinctions

### makeves (מקבת)
Five times in Tanach: Yael's (Shoftim 4:21); the quarry-tool of "the *makeves* of
the pit you were dug from" (Yeshayahu 51:1); the tools not heard at the building
of the Beis HaMikdash (Melachim I 6:7); and twice of making idols (Yeshayahu
44:12; Yirmiyahu 10:4); in the Mishnah, Keilim 29:5 and 29:7 (how much of the
handle counts as part of the tool for *tumah*) and Parah 3:11. Rabbi Moshe
Tedeschi-Ashkenazi: the hammer used primarily to wedge a nail into a hole.

### patish (פטיש)
Three times: the artisan flattening metal for an idol (Yeshayahu 41:7); "is My
word not like fire, and like a *patish* that shatters rock" (Yirmiyahu 23:29);
and Bavel as "the *patish* of the whole earth" (Yirmiyahu 50:23). In the first
two the Targum simply Aramaicizes it as *patisha*. Its root פ-ט-(י)-ש yields
nothing else, so its base is hard to pin down: Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Carpentras
(*Aholei Yehuda*) connects it with פ-ש-ט, spreading or flattening, which a
hammer is adept at; Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi derives it from either ט-ו-ש,
"to fly" (Iyov 9:26), or נ-ט-ש, "to spread" (Bamidbar 11:31), for the chips of
rock that fly and scatter when stone is struck.

### kurnas (קורנס)
Absent from Tanach, Hebrew and Aramaic parts alike, but Rashi glosses *makeves* by
it (to Yirmiyahu 10:4), and it is the hammer that beat the Menorah from a single
block of gold (Rashi to Shemos 25:31, Bamidbar 10:2, Menachos 28a). It is in the
Mishnah at Shabbos 12:1 and 17:2 and Keilim 13:4 and 29:6. (Kohut prefers the
vocalization *kornas*, HaBachur *karnas*.)

Its etymology is disputed. Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Mussaf HeAruch*) already
writes that the word comes from Greek, and Rabbi Ernest Klein, in his
etymological dictionary of Hebrew, derives it from the Greek *koryne*, bat or
baton or club or mace, apparently related to *korys* (helmet), *korymbos*
(zenith, cluster of flowers) and *koryphe* (head, summit). Dr. Alexander Kohut
(*Aruch Completum*), on the other hand, contends that *kurnas* is a native
Aramaic and Syriac word. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein proposes a possible link
between *kurnas* and the name of the Greek deity Cronus, on phonetic similarity
and shared consonants, some scholars having traced Cronus to a Proto-Indo-European
root meaning "to cut"; he concedes he knows of no evidence that Cronus was ever
depicted with a hammer, and says only that the convergence warrants further
exploration.

### halmus (הלמות)
Once, in Devorah's song: "her hand to the peg, her right hand to the *halmus* of
the toilers, and she struck (*halmah*) Sisera" (Shoftim 5:26). Since the prose
account calls the same tool a *makeves*, the two are one thing, as Ibn Janach and
Radak say outright in their *Sifrei HaShorashim* and as Targum confirms by
rendering both *arzafta* (Shoftim 4:21 and 5:26). *Arzafta* or *marzafta* is the
Talmudic Aramaic word (Berachos 34a, Megillah 25a, Gittin 56b), which Rashi
defines as *kurnas* (to Berachos 34a, Gittin 56b, Bava Basra 20a), bringing the
cluster full circle; see also Targum Yonasan to Yeshayahu 41:7, rendering *holem*
as *kurnas*, and Targum to Tehillim 73:10. There is also an Arabic cognate,
*marzaba*, sledgehammer. The root ה-ל-ם (so Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak)
also gives the verb "to break"; Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Yerios Shlomo*,
*Cheshek Shlomo*) reduces it to the biliteral ה-ל, "spreading out," as in *yahel*
(light diffusing), *yahalom*, *mahul* (a thick liquid diluted through a thinner
one), *hallel* and *tehillah*, *halah* and *halom*, and *ohel* (a tent spread
over its pegs), so *halmah* is smashing that scatters the pieces; Pappenheim does
not say so explicitly, but Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds that *halmus* is then
the instrument that brings that about. Rabbi Moshe Tedeschi-Ashkenazi instead reads ח-ל-ם or
א-ל-ם, "healthy, strong, powerful," a hammer so big that even a strong man is
left tired out by a blow with it.

## Rendering guidance

- Shoftim 4:21 and 5:26 are one story told twice. Render מקבת "mallet" and הלמות
  "mallet" too, so the reader sees it is the same tool.
- Trap: English "hammer" absorbs all four. Where a source grades two at once
  (*Sefer HeAruch*; Rashi to Shabbos 21b), keep "hammer" and "sledge" apart.
- קורנס in a Mishnah or in Rashi is "smith's hammer"; the generic "hammer" is
  now פטיש.
- מכבי / מקבי: transliterate "Maccabee"; rendering it "the Hammer" silently
  picks one side of the machlokes.
