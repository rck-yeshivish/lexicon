# Chance: mikreh / keri / te'unah / i'rua

## Bottom line

*Mikreh* is an **occurrence** or **happenstance**; Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim
explains it as the sudden, unanticipated collision of a person with a new set of
circumstances. *Keri*, from the same root, appears in the Torah's rebuke
(Vayikra 26), where many commentators, Rav Hirsch among them, take it as
**offhandedness** or **flippancy** toward Hashem, while Rabbi Pappenheim reads
it as the sinner's provoking posture. On either reading, Vayikra 26 is about
attitude, not chance. In Mishnaic Hebrew *keri* is the euphemism for a seminal
emission. *Te'unah* is a **mishap** or **befalling**; Malbim takes it to refer
to the confluence of an incident's causes, where *mikreh* is the point at which
things meet. *I'rua* is post-biblical, a plain **event** or **incident**.

## Distinctions

### mikreh / keri (מקרה, קרי)
Root ק-ר-ה / ק-ר-א. In several places in Tanach a *mikreh* is a random happening: the Pelishtim
test whether their plague was Hashem's hand or merely a *mikreh* (Shmuel I
6:9); Shlomo HaMelech says one *mikreh* meets man and beast (Koheles 3:19) and
one *mikreh* the righteous and the wicked (Koheles 9:2-3); the upshot, Rabbi
Klein says, is that since all is Divinely pre-ordained it is hopeless to try to
escape one's fate. The verb carries the
same sense of the unprepared-for: *vayikar* of Hashem appearing to Bilaam
(Bamidbar 23:4, 23:16), *karahu* of the accident Yaakov feared for Binyamin
(Bereishis 42:38, 44:29), *karcha* of Amaleik chancing upon Yisrael (Devarim
25:18), *yikareh* of coming upon a bird's nest (Devarim 22:6), and *vayiker
mikreh* of Rus arriving in Boaz's field (Rus 2:3). Professor Stephen B. Chapman's essay
"Miqreh and YHWH" studies the Bible's understanding of the Divine Hand behind
every case of *mikreh*.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim (*Cheshek Shlomo*) takes the biliteral ק-ר to mean
"strong impact," and gathers under it *korah* (beam) and *tikrah* (ceiling),
*kir* (wall), *kiryah* (city), *kurei* (spider web), *krum* (membrane), and
*kor* (cold, the clash of temperatures against the body's warmth). On that
reading a *mikreh* is the sudden collision of a person with a new set of
circumstances. He treats *kara* / *kriyah* (calling) as the same biliteral with
an extraneous aleph: a call gets attention and so brings about a meeting, an
impact; likewise *likras*, moving toward a meeting.

On *keri* in Vayikra 26 (26:21, 26:23, 26:24, 26:27-28, 26:40-41, and nowhere
else in Tanach) there are two readings.

- Many commentators, Rav Hirsch (to Bereishis 42:4 and Vayikra 26:21) among
  them: *keri* is serving Hashem in a non-deliberate, glib way, and the
  measure-for-measure is that Hashem deals back offhandedly.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes a slightly different approach: *keri* is the
  sinner's antagonistic posture, an attempt to anger Hashem through his actions,
  so that the problem is twofold, the sin itself and the attitude behind it; the
  word then carries his "stronger impact" and draws a stronger response.

Rabbi Klein leaves both standing.

In the Mishnah *keri* means a seminal emission (*Berachos* 3:4-6, *Terumos*
1:6, *Avodah Zarah* 3:4, *Avos* 5:5, *Tamid* 1:1, *Middos* 1:9, *Keilim* 1:5,
*Mikvaos* 3:4 and 8:4, *Niddah* 5:1,
*Zavim* 1:1-2 and 2:3), the language avoiding the matter by calling it
simply "an incident." The euphemism has biblical warrant in *mikreh* used of
ritual impurity (Devarim 23:11, Shmuel I 20:26).

### mikreh against metziah (מציאה)
Malbim, as cited in *Sefer HaCarmel*, treats *mikreh* and *metziah* as almost
synonymous and attempts to
separate them by cause: when the cause is unknown or irregular the happening
is a *mikreh*; when the cause is not so out of the ordinary it is a *metziah*.
Hence Yaakov's sons report the *koros* that befell them in Mitzrayim (Bereishis
42:29), all of it unexpected, while Yehoshua's spies report *motza'os*
(Yehoshua 2:23), since they were sent out precisely to find things worth
reporting.

### te'unah (תאונה)
Root א-נ-ה. Tanach uses the verb: "no evil shall befall you" (Tehillim 91:10),
"no misfortune will befall the righteous" (Mishlei 12:21), and *inah l'yado* of
the inadvertent killer whose exile Hashem arranges (Shemos 21:13). Rashi there
describes the arrangement: Hashem brings the deliberate murderer and the
inadvertent killer together so that each receives his due.

- Malbim (*Sefer HaCarmel*): *mikreh* is the meeting-point of things, while
  *te'unah* is the confluence of an incident's **causes**. Rabbi Klein glosses
  the first as perhaps the more physical impact of Rabbi Pappenheim's reading,
  and the second as seemingly a more abstract coming-together of factors. Malbim
  illustrates the distinction from Shemos 21:13: Hashem does not transport the
  killer to the Ir Miklat, He assembles the circumstances that will send him
  there.
- The same root א-נ-ה yields *anah*, "where?", of an unknown location, and Rabbi
  Shaul Goldman accounts for the pairing by saying that *te'unah* looks not so
  much at an event's causes or effects as at the unpredictability of its landing
  place, the arbitrary "where" of it.

Modern Hebrew *te'unas derachim*, literally an occurrence of the roads, is this
word.

### i'rua (אירוע)
Root א-ר-ע. Not in Tanach; the verb is Mishnaic. Five things *ir'u* on the
seventeenth of Tammuz (*Taanis* 4:6); the verb serves for a disqualification
befalling a Korban Pesach (*Pesachim* 8:6), a Parah Adumah (*Parah* 4:4), or
another korban (*Zevachim* 12:4), and for a wedding or a bereavement that
preoccupies a man (*Kilayim* 7:7, *Taharos* 9:2, *Moed Katan* 2:1-2, *Berachos*
4:2). It frequently pairs with rabbinic *keri* (*Yoma* 1:1, *Avos* 5:5, *Tamid*
1:1, *Middos* 1:9 and 2:2). Targum generally renders *mikreh* / *keri* with
cognates of *i'rua* or of ע-ר-ע; at Tehillim 85:11 it renders *nifgashu*, "met,"
with a cognate of *i'rua*.

### the falling metaphor
Rabbi Shaul Goldman proposes that an event or occurrence is often framed in
spatial terms, as something that falls and lands, which conveys a happening with
no discernible pattern or intention behind it: it is construed as cast from
above and striking the ground at random. The *mikreh*/*keri* cluster already
implies a convergence of seemingly unrelated factors, and he takes it further, that the
impact of those converging forces is consistently conceived as a downward fall,
perhaps because gravity is the most universal form of impact there is. He finds
English doing the same: an event "falls out" on a date, and
*incident*, *accident* and *coincidence* all come from the Latin *cadere*,
"falling upon," with *befalling* saying it outright and *landing* naming the end
of a fall; *somewhere*, *wherever* and *whenever* carry the same sense of a
place or time not deliberately fixed.

On that model he reads the Hebrew back. By phonetic shift and the interchange of
reish with lamed and hei with ayin, ק-ר-ה may also relate to ק-ל-ע, to sling or
hurl, the image of a projectile striking a target, which the Talmudic Aramaic
*ikla*, someone who happens to arrive somewhere, seems to corroborate. The same
roots reach the ground itself: *karka*, a doubling of ק-ר, is land, and so is
the Biblical Aramaic *arka*, which appears once (Yirmiyahu 10:11). The standard
Aramaic for land is *ar'a*, cognate to Hebrew *eretz* with the tzadi becoming an
ayin, and its root is the root of *i'ra*/*i'rua*, so that an *i'rua* is, at root, something landing on the ground; other uses of א-ר-ע
in Targumic Aramaic mean ruin or collapse. He adds that *kara*/*kriyah*/*vayikra*,
primarily to call or proclaim, may also suggest deliberately summoning an event,
which gives those words a paradoxical double duty, purposeful invocation
(*mikra kodesh*) beside random occurrence, and a middle ground between accident
and intention; and the Targumic rendering by ע-ר-ע comes back to א-ר-ע through
the interchange of ayin and aleph. He maintains that *arai*, non-permanence, is
properly spelled ע-ר-א and has nothing to do with *i'rua*, whatever the
occasional spelling with an aleph.

## Rendering guidance

- Vayikra 26 *keri*: "with offhandedness," "casually," "as happenstance." Never
  "by accident" or "by chance," which turn the Torah's charge from an attitude
  into a mishap and break the measure-for-measure of the pasuk.
- Trap: English "accident" will absorb *mikreh*, *te'unah* and *i'rua* at once.
  Keep "occurrence/happenstance" for *mikreh*, "mishap/befalling" for *te'unah*,
  "event/incident" for *i'rua*, and hold the assignment across a passage.
- Rus 2:3, *vayiker mikreh*: render the doubling, "an occurrence occurred," or
  "as it happened, she happened upon"; do not smooth it to "she happened to
  come," which drops the pointed redundancy the pasuk builds on.
- Koheles 3:19 and 9:2-3 repeat *mikreh*; keep one English word throughout,
  since the point is that it is the same *mikreh*.
- Mishnaic *keri* stays transliterated with a gloss at first occurrence. Do not
  render it "accident," and do not let it borrow the Vayikra sense. Where
  *mikreh* and *metziah* stand together, "chance occurrence" against "find"
  carries Malbim's distinction without a footnote.
