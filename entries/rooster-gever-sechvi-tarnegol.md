# Rooster: gever / sechvi / ziz sadai / tarnegol

## Bottom line

*Tarnegol* is the ordinary word for a **rooster** in Mishnaic Hebrew and the
safe rendering wherever it appears. *Gever* means **man** or **male** far more
often than rooster; read it as a bird only where the context forces it (the
Mishnah's "call of the *gever*," Targum Yonasan on Etzion Gever). *Sechvi* is
the genuinely open word: Chazal fix it as **rooster** in the morning berachah,
but most mefarshim read it as the **heart**. *Ziz sadai* is a bird of some kind,
which Targum renders **wild fowl**. Never translate *sechvi* as "rooster" in
Iyov 38:36 without noting that Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Rosh, and Tur read it
otherwise.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What sechvi means** (Iyov 38:36, "Who gives understanding to the *sechvi*").
Three positions.

1. **Rooster.** Chazal report that in a certain foreign place people called a
   rooster a *sechvi*; Bavli Rosh Hashanah 26a names Kennesrin in northern
   Syria, Yerushalmi Berachos 9:1 names Rome, and *Vayikra Rabbah* 25:5 names
   Arabia. Berachos 60b builds the morning berachah on this, "Who gave the
   *sechvi* understanding to distinguish day from night."
2. **The heart.** The majority of mefarshim, or at least they hold it means this
   as well: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), Rabbeinu Chananel (to Rosh
   Hashanah 26a), Rashi and Ibn Ezra (to Iyov 38:36), Radak (*Sefer
   HaShorashim* and to Tehillim 73:7), Rosh (Berachos 9:23), and Tur (*Orach
   Chaim* 46). *Siddur HaRokeach* reads it as the neshamah.
3. **The skull.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to ס-ח, covering or barrier,
   the root of *sukkah* (a covered enclosure), *nesech* (a libation covering a
   spot with liquid), and *sichah* (smearing with oil); the *sechvi* is the
   skull that covers and shields the brain.

*Daas Mikra* records a further reading, that the passage in Iyov concerns types
of cloud and *sechvi* is a cloud. The Bottom line follows Chazal for the
berachah, since the halachah is settled there, and flags the heart reading
wherever Iyov 38:36 itself is being translated.

**How sechvi and "seeing" connect.** Rashi ties it to *socheh*, gazing (compare
Targum to Shmuel I 17:42 and Yeshayahu 21:8): the heart is the organ that tries
to see an action's future consequences, or else the rooster, which sees far
(Abudarham). Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg specifies that the seeing is
intellectual rather than physical, and groups *sechvi* with *hasket* (to listen
with the mind, not merely hear) and *maskis* (arresting figures engraved in
stone).

**Where the name Nergal points.** Sanhedrin 63b: the Babylonians brought Sukkos
Benos, a hen-shaped idol, and the men of Kutha made Nergal, shaped like a
rooster (Melachim II 17:30); Rabbeinu Chananel specifies a wild rooster. Rabbi
Meir HaLevi Abulafia offers two accounts of the name: *tarnegol* has the same
letters as *nergal*, or *nergal* is from *ragil*, frequent, since the rooster
copulates most often. Yerushalmi Avodah Zarah 3:2 disagrees outright and makes
Nergal a foot-shaped idol, from *regel*, treating the nun as non-essential.
Rabbi Chaim Futernik goes the other way and derives *sechvi* itself from the
name Sukkos Benos.

## Distinctions

### gever (גבר)
Root ג-ב-ר, cognate with *gevurah*. Rabbeinu Efrayim: a rooster is called
*gever* because its voice is distinct among birds as a man's is from a woman's.
The Mishnah uses "the call of the *gever*" three times for daybreak (Yoma 1:8,
Sukkah 5:4, Tamid 1:2, and see Yoma 20b), and Targum Yonasan (to Bamidbar 33:35)
renders Etzion Gever as the City of the Rooster; the place recurs at Devarim 2:8,
Melachim I 9:26 and 22:49, Divrei HaYamim II 8:17 and 20:36.

Why Bilaam called himself a *gever* (Bamidbar 24:3, 24:15) drew several
readings. Rabbeinu Efrayim: Berachos 7a says the moment of Divine anger each
morning can be identified when the rooster's red crest turns white, and Bilaam
likewise knew the moment a curse would take. *Peirush HaRokeach*: the rooster
copulates constantly (Berachos 22a), and Bilaam was as single-minded. *Siddur
HaRokeach*: the rooster shuts one eye and stands on one foot at that moment, and
Bilaam was blind in one eye and lame in one foot. Rabbeinu Bachya (to Bamidbar
24:3): the rooster crows seven times (*Perek Shirah* ch. 4 assigns it seven
songs), matching Bilaam's seven oracles. *Peirush HaRokeach* also explains
Etzion Gever's name from its inhabitants' gift for counsel and for intuiting the
hour as a rooster does, and Rabbi Menachem Tziyyoni cites the Mekubalim that
some of them commanded an esoteric wisdom called the Knowledge of the Chicken,
hinted at in the city's name.

### ziz sadai (זיז שדי)
Twice in Tehillim (50:11, 80:14), plainly a bird. Targum both times renders
*tarnegol bara*, wild chicken. Rabbi Yehuda Felix notes that *tarnegol bara* also
denotes the *duchifas* (Vayikra 11:19, and Gittin 68b), commonly identified as
the hoopoe, so the Aramaic phrase is not by itself decisive.

### tarnegol (תרנגול)
Not in Tanach; ubiquitous in the Mishnah (Terumos 11:9, Shabbos 5:4 and 18:2,
Pesachim 2:7, Nedarim 5:1, Bava Kama 2:1 and 7:7, Bava Metzia 5:4, Eduyos 6:1,
Chullin 3:5 and 12:1, Keilim 8:5, Taharos 3:8, and many more). Bava Kama 7:7
records the prohibition on raising chickens in Yerushalayim.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "rooster" absorbs *gever*, *sechvi*, and *tarnegol*. In a passage that
  moves between them, keep *gever* as "the *gever*" or "the cock" and reserve
  "rooster" for *tarnegol*.
- *Gever* in Tanach almost always means a man. Bamidbar 24:3, *ne'um hagever*,
  is "the word of the man"; the rooster reading is a derashah on top of that, not
  a replacement for it, and both should show if the derashah is being quoted.
- In the berachah, render *sechvi* as the siddur's own tradition takes it,
  "rooster," and do not import the heart reading into the tefillah.
- In Iyov 38:36 the pasuk pairs *tuchos* (the kidneys, or the inward parts) with
  *sechvi*. If you render *tuchos* internally, "heart" for *sechvi* keeps the
  parallel; if you render "rooster," the parallel breaks, which is exactly the
  reason most mefarshim went the other way.
- *Ziz sadai* is best left as a phrase with a gloss, "the wild creature of the
  field," rather than pinned to a species Tanach does not name.
