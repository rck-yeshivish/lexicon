# Rooster: gever / sechvi / ziz sadai / tarnegol

## Bottom line

*Tarnegol* is the ordinary word for a **rooster** in Mishnaic Hebrew. *Gever*
sometimes means rooster but more often means **man** or **male**; the Mishnah's
"call of the *gever*" is understood as the rooster's, and Targum Yonasan renders
Etzion Gever as the City of the Rooster. The meaning of *sechvi* is genuinely
open. Chazal take it for a **rooster** and build the morning berachah on it, but
most mefarshim (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Rosh and Tur among them) read it as the
**heart**, or at least as also meaning heart, and Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim takes
it as the **skull**. *Ziz sadai* is a bird of some kind, which Targum renders
**wild fowl**.

## Where the authorities disagree

**What sechvi means** (Iyov 38:36, "Who gives understanding to the *sechvi*").
Three positions.

1. **Rooster.** Chazal report that in a certain foreign place people called a
   rooster a *sechvi*; Bavli Rosh Hashanah 26a names Kennesrin in northern
   Syria, Yerushalmi Berachos 9:1 names Rome, and *Vayikra Rabbah* 25:5 names
   Arabia. Berachos 60b builds the morning berachah on this, "Who gave the
   *sechvi* understanding to distinguish day from night."
2. **The heart.** The majority of mefarshim, or at least they hold it means this
   as well: Ibn Janach (*Sefer HaShorashim*), Rabbeinu Chananel (to Rosh
   Hashanah 26a), Rashi and Ibn Ezra (to Iyov 38:36), Radak (*Sefer
   HaShorashim* and to Tehillim 73:7), Rosh (Berachos 9:23), Tur (*Orach
   Chaim* 46), and others. *Siddur HaRokeach* reads it as the neshamah.
3. **The skull.** Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim traces it to ס-ך, covering or barrier,
   the root of *sukkah* (a covered enclosure), *nesech* (a libation covering a
   spot with liquid), and *sichah* (smearing with oil); the *sechvi* is the
   skull that covers and shields the brain.

*Daas Mikra* (to Iyov 38:36) cites other scholars who read the passage in Iyov
as concerning types of cloud, so that *sechvi* means a cloud.

**How sechvi and "seeing" connect.**

- Rashi ties it to *socheh*, gazing (compare Targum to Shmuel I 17:42 and
  Yeshayahu 21:8): the heart is the organ that tries to see an action's future
  consequences, or else the rooster, which sees far (Abudarham).
- Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg specifies that the seeing is intellectual rather
  than physical, and groups *sechvi* with *hasket* (to listen with the mind) and *maskis* (arresting figures engraved in stone).

**Where the name Nergal points.** After the Assyrians conquered the Kingdom of
Yisrael they settled Mesopotamian foreigners in the land, each bringing his own
deity: the men of Bavel brought Sukkos Benos and the men of Kutha made Nergal
(Melachim II 17:30).
- Sanhedrin 63b relates that Sukkos Benos was a hen-shaped idol and Nergal a
  rooster-shaped one; Rabbeinu Chananel specifies a wild rooster.
- Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia offers two accounts of the name: *tarnegol* contains the
  letters of *nergal*, or *nergal* is from *ragil*, frequent, since the
  rooster is understood to copulate most often.
- The Yerushalmi (Avodah Zarah 3:2) understands Nergal differently, as a
  foot-shaped idol, from [*regel*](foot-paam-regel.html#%D7%A8%D7%92%D7%9C), treating the nun as not integral to the core
  meaning.
- On the strength of the Sanhedrin passage Rabbi Chaim Futernik surmises that
  *sechvi* in the sense of rooster is itself related to the name Sukkos Benos.

## Distinctions

### gever (גבר)
Root ג-ב-ר, seemingly a cognate of [*gevurah*](strength-koach-gevurah-ometz-chozek.html#%D7%92%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%94). Rabbeinu Efrayim: a rooster is
called *gever* because its voice is distinct among birds as a man's is from a
woman's. The Mishnah uses "the call of the *gever*" three times for daybreak
(Yoma 1:8, Sukkah 5:4, Tamid 1:2, and see Yoma 20b), and Targum Yonasan (to
Bamidbar 33:35) renders Etzion Gever as the City of the Rooster. The city is one
of the stops of the forty years in the wilderness (Bamidbar 33:35-36, Devarim
2:8); Shlomo HaMelech later stationed his navy there (Melachim I 9:26, Divrei
HaYamim II 8:17), and Yehoshafat's ships broke down there (Melachim I 22:49,
Divrei HaYamim II 20:36).

Abudarham (on Yom Kippur): the custom of using a rooster for *kaparos* on Erev
Yom Kippur is because *gever* means both rooster and man, which makes the
rooster especially fit to stand in for a person.

Why Bilaam called himself a *gever* (Bamidbar 24:3, 24:15) drew several
readings.
- Rabbeinu Efrayim: Berachos 7a says the moment of Divine anger each morning can
  be identified when the rooster's red crest turns white, and Bilaam likewise
  knew the moment a curse would take. (A 2021 paper by Dr. Jessica L. Lamont of
  Yale demonstrates that chickens were particularly associated with curse
  rituals in ancient Greece.)
- *Peirush HaRokeach*: the rooster copulates frequently (Berachos 22a), and
  Bilaam was as single-minded.
- *Siddur HaRokeach*: the rooster shuts one eye and stands on one foot at that
  moment, and Bilaam was blind in one eye and lame in one foot.
- Rabbeinu Bachya (to Bamidbar 24:3): the rooster crows seven times (*Perek
  Shirah* ch. 4 assigns it seven songs), matching Bilaam's seven oracles.

*Peirush HaRokeach* also explains Etzion Gever's name from its inhabitants' gift
for counsel and for intuiting the hour as a rooster does, and Rabbi Menachem
Tziyyoni cites the Mekubalim that some of them commanded an esoteric wisdom
called the Knowledge of the Chicken, hinted at in the city's name.

### ziz sadai (זיז שדי)
Twice in Tehillim (50:11, 80:14), plainly a bird. Targum both times renders
*tarnegol bara*, wild chicken. Rabbi Dr. Yehuda Felix points out that *tarnegol
bara* also denotes the *duchifas* (Vayikra 11:19), commonly identified as the
hoopoe (see Gittin 68b).

### tarnegol (תרנגול)
Not in Tanach; ubiquitous in the Mishnah (Terumos 11:9, Maasros 3:7, Shabbos
5:4, 18:2 and 24:3, Pesachim 2:7 and 4:7, Nedarim 5:1, Bava Kamma 2:1, 7:7 and
10:9, Bava Metzia 5:4, Bava Basra 3:5, Eduyos 6:1, Avodah Zarah 1:5, Chullin 3:5
and 12:1, Meilah 3:5, Keilim 8:5, Parah 5:6, Taharos 3:8). The word also turns
up in the Dead Sea Scrolls (11QT[c]) in connection with the prohibition on
raising chickens in Yerushalayim, a prohibition codified in the Mishnah as well
(Bava Kamma 7:7).

Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein derives *tarnegol* from the Akkadian *tar-lugallu*
("cock"), assuming an interchange of nun and lamed. The Akkadian word is itself
a compound of the Sumerian *tar* ("bird," similar to the
Hebrew *tor*, pigeon) and *lugal* ("king"), perhaps an allusion to the rooster's
crest, which resembles a king's crown. Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein adds a further curiosity of
his own: Bilaam, who called himself a rooster, had as his patron Balak son of
Tzippor, whose father's name means "bird" and who was himself a king.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "rooster" absorbs *gever*, *sechvi*, and *tarnegol*. In a passage that
  moves between them, keep *gever* as "the *gever*" or "the cock" and reserve
  "rooster" for *tarnegol*.
- *Gever* in Tanach almost always means a man. Bamidbar 24:3, *ne'um hagever*,
  is "the word of the man"; the rooster reading is a derashah on top of that, not
  a replacement for it, and both should show if the derashah is being quoted.
- In the berachah, render *sechvi* as the siddur's own tradition takes it,
  "rooster," and do not import the heart reading into the tefillah.
- In Iyov 38:36 the pasuk pairs *tuchos* (the kidneys, or the inward parts) with
  *sechvi*. If you render *tuchos* internally, "heart" for *sechvi* keeps the
  parallel; if you render "rooster," the parallel breaks.
- *Ziz sadai* is best left as a phrase with a gloss, "the wild creature of the
  field," rather than pinned to a species Tanach does not name.
