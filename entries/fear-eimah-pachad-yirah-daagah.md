# Fear: eimah / pachad / yirah (morah) / daagah

## Bottom line

For Ibn Ezra, *eimah* is the felt emotion of being afraid: **dread, terror**.
*Pachad* is the sudden, short-lived start of fear, for Ibn Ezra its outward
manifestation: **fright, fear**. *Yirah*
and its noun *morah* are the sustained fear that has built up over time: **fear**
(the general word). *Daagah* is fear of a loss or of a coming evil: **worry,
anxiety**. Rashi contrasts *pachad* as sudden with *morah* as pent up over time;
for Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer *pachad* is brief and *morah* continual and
therefore the more intense.

## Distinctions

### eimah (אימה)
Root א-י-ם. Ibn Ezra (Shemos 23:27): *eimah* is the emotive feeling of being afraid,
as against *pachad*, the outward manifestation of fear. Rashi (Shemos 15:16): *eimah*
and *pachad* differ as fear of a distant threat differs from fear of one close at
hand; the versions of Rashi and the other commentators disagree on which is which.
Either way, Rabbi
Klein observes, a man reacts differently to hearing news of a threat than to
meeting one, and Hebrew registers those differing fight-or-flight responses in
two otherwise synonymous words.

### pachad (פחד)
Root פ-ח-ד.
- Rashi (Devarim 11:25), distinguishing it from *morah*, repeats the far/near
  distinction there and adds a second one: *pachad* is a sudden fear, *morah* a
  fear pent up for some time.
- Wertheimer: *pachad* is the less intense, sudden but short-lived; and it
  applies only to human beings, who have the intellect to grasp what a danger
  implies, whereas the other words can apply to animals too, whose instinct
  reacts without comprehension.
- Ibn Ezra: the outward manifestation of fear.

### yirah / morah (יראה / מורא)
Root י-ר-א. Rashi (Devarim 11:25): the fear that has remained pent up for some time.
Wertheimer: the more intense type, because it is continual and builds on itself. The
Beis HaLevi (Rabbi Yosef Dov Soloveitchik, parashas Vayigash): *yirah* is fear of
what one anticipates *might* happen, as against *daagah*, worry over what one
foresees *will* happen.

### daagah (דאגה)
Root ד-א-ג.
- Rashi (Yoma 75a): fear of losing something one has; Rashi (Gittin 70a): fear
  of the arrival of a frightening situation, such as famine or invasion. Shlomo
  HaMelech's counsel, דאגה בלב איש ישחנה (Mishlei 12:25): speak it out to
  others, for worry confined to finite words loses its limitlessness; on the
  second reading, distract oneself with other matters.
- Beis HaLevi: worry over what is foreseen as certain.
- Wertheimer: not the worrying itself but the despondency of one steeped in
  anxious fright; Rabbi Klein notes that this echoes Franklin Delano Roosevelt's
  inaugural line that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.
- Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim connects *daagah* to the biliteral ד-ג of *dag*, fish:
  it is a minimally justified apprehension, the worrier seeing only some signs
  of a possible danger, as the fisherman (*dayag*, *davag*) picks his spot on
  vague signs with no solid reason to think there will really be fish there.
  Menachem Ibn Saruk, Ibn Janach and Radak all derive *daagah* from its own
  triliteral root, ד-א-ג.

## Rendering guidance

- אימתה ופחד (Shemos 15:16): "terror and dread" or "dread and fright"; two words,
  two renderings. Since the far/near assignment is disputed, do not build a
  rendering on it; Ibn Ezra's inner/outer split (the feeling, then its
  manifestation) is the safer basis for the pair.
- פחדכם ומוראכם (Devarim 11:25): "the fright of you and the fear of you," keeping
  *pachad* as the sudden start and *morah* as the settled fear.
- Standalone יראה of a threat: "fear." Standalone פחד: "fright" when the suddenness
  matters, "fear" otherwise, but never "terror" as if it were the graver word.
- דאגה: "worry" or "anxiety," never "fear"; Mishlei 12:25 reads "worry in a man's
  heart."
- Trap: English "fear" absorbs all four. When the pasuk pairs or lists them, or a
  derashah turns on the sequence, give each its own word.
- Trap: "dread" is a fair rendering of *eimah* but also of settled *morah*; reserve
  it for *eimah* when both are in play.
- Scope: Rabbi Klein treats fear as the emotion before a threat. *Yiras Shamayim* as
  awe or reverence is a separate usage he does not take up; do not import rulings
  for that from here.
