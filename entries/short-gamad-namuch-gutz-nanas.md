# Shortness of stature: gamad / namuch / gutz / nanas

## Bottom line

*Gomed* is a **measure**, an *amah* or a cut-down *amah*, and *gamadim* in
Yechezkel are read either as sounders of the sea's depth or as **dwarfs** an
*amah* tall. *Namuch* is **low**, the plain Rabbinic word for short in height, of
a person or a fence alike. *Gutz* is **stunted**, from an Aramaic sense of
cutting off, as though the man's height had been cut short. *Nanas* is the
Rabbinic term for a **dwarf**, and equally for short boards or low walls. The
one thing not to get wrong: none of these is exclusively about people, so a
short wall in a sugya is *namuch* or *nanas*, not a metaphor.

## Distinctions

### gomed and gamadim (גומד, גמדים)
The root gimmel-mem-dalet occurs twice in Tanach. Ehud's sword, with which he
struck Eglon, was a *gomed* long (Shoftim 3:16). Rashbam (Bava Basra 100a) and
Radak (Shoftim 3:16) take *gomed* as simply an *amah*; *Sefer HeAruch*, Rashi,
and Mahari Kara there take it as a truncated *amah*, and Rashi uses a form of
*gidem*, amputated, to explain that the sword was less than an *amah*. Rabbi
Yehoshua Steinberg infers from Rashi's wording that he read *gomed* as a
metathesis of *gidem*, dalet and mem trading places.

The other occurrence is Yechezkel 27:11, the *gamadim* who manned the watchtowers
of Tyre. Rashi and Mahari Kara first explain *gamadim* as seamen skilled at
measuring the sea's depth with ropes, then, together with Radak (there and in
*Sefer HaShorashim*), as short people, no more than an *amah* tall. Targum
Yonasan renders *gamadim* as Cappadocians; Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur (*Sefer Tishbi*)
combines the Targum with Rashi's second reading and takes the Cappadocians for a
nation of dwarfs.

In Talmudic Hebrew the root means contracting or shrinking: Eretz Yisrael
expands to hold its inhabitants and is *gamda*, contracted, when left desolate
(Gittin 57a; see also Beitzah 15a, Pesachim 111a, Chullin 43a). Bachur takes
*gomed* to be the native Hebrew word for a short person.

### namuch (נמוך)
Not in Tanach; several times in the Mishnah (*Kilayim* 4:7, *Eruvin* 9:1,
*Zevachim* 1:2), and used of anything low, a short fence as readily as a short
man. Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur (*Sefer Tishbi*), who vocalizes it *namoch*, takes it
as a secondary base built on the *nifal* of biblical *mach*, poor or brought
low: the added nun marks one who has been lowered, and the resulting word is
then handled as though the nun belonged to the root. The comparison is to
Rabbinic *terumah*, built on the biblical reish-(vav)-mem of raising and then
treated as though the tav were root. The root of *mach* itself is given as
mem-vav-kaf (Ibn Janach and Radak), mem-kaf-kaf (Ibn Chayyuj), mem-kaf (Ibn
Saruk), or a bare kaf (Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim); see the separate entry on the
words for poverty.

### gutz (גוץ)
Neither biblical nor Mishnaic, but frequent in the Gemara, masculine *gutz* and
feminine *gutza*. Yevamos 106b treats the halachos of *chalitzah* where the man
or the woman is unusually short. Bava Metzia 59a: if your wife is short, bend
down to hear her. Bava Metzia 27b: that a corpse is tall or short is not enough
to identify it. Megillah 27b describes Rav Huna as a *gutza*. And Berachos 31b
has Chanah asking that her son be neither too tall nor too short, neither too
thin nor too fat, neither too pale nor too ruddy, neither too clever nor too
foolish.

Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (*Musaf HeAruch*) held the word to be Greek. The reading
the column prefers is the Aramaic one: the verb *gutz* in Aramaic means to gnaw,
bite, or cut off, so a short man is one whose stature was cut off short of what
was expected.

### nanas (ננס)
The standing Rabbinic word for a dwarf, and the very word Rashi and Radak used
in glossing *gamadim* at Yechezkel 27:11. Bechoros 45b advises that a very tall
man not marry a very tall woman lest their child be a mast, nor a *nanas* marry
a *nanaseses* lest their child be the size of a finger. Like the others it is
not confined to people: low boards and walls are *nanasin* (Rashi to Yoma 16b,
Pesachim 64a, and to Yechezkel 40:43). Nevuchadnetzar is called "the *nanas* of
Bavel" (*Pesikta DeRav Kahana* 13:5; see *Yalkut Shimoni* to Daniel 1062).
Several Tannaim carry it as a name or nickname: Ben Nanas (*Kesubos* 10:5,
*Gittin* 8:10, *Bava Basra* 7:3 and 10:8, *Shevuos* 7:5) and Rabbi Shimon ben
Nanas (*Bikkurim* 3:9, *Shabbos* 16:5, *Eruvin* 10:15, *Bava Basra* 10:8).
Bachur (*Sefer Tishbi*) equates *nanas* with the Latin *nano*. It has nothing to
do with Modern Hebrew *ananas*, pineapple.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "short" will absorb all four. Where a sugya or a commentary pairs two of
  them, render *namuch* "low," *gutz* "stunted" or "short-statured," and *nanas*
  "dwarf."
- *Gomed* in Shoftim 3:16 is a unit of length, not a description of Ehud. Write
  "a *gomed* in length" and gloss it once with the dispute (an *amah*, or less
  than an *amah*); "a cubit long" quietly decides the machlokes.
- *Gamadim* at Yechezkel 27:11 is genuinely contested. Say which Rashi you are
  following, the sounders or the dwarfs, rather than picking silently.
- In halachic contexts *namuch* and *nanas* often describe objects. "A *nanas*
  board" is a low board; do not personify it.
- The Moed Katan 18a description of Pharaoh as one *amah* tall is a statement
  about stature, and Chazal's word for such a man is *nanas*. Keep the register
  plain; the modern clinical vocabulary does not belong in a rendering of a
  Gemara.
