# Shortness of stature: gamad / namuch / gutz / nanas

## Bottom line

*Gomed* is a **measure**, an *amah* or a cut-down *amah*, and the *gamadim* in
Yechezkel are read either as sounders of the sea's depth or as **dwarfs** an
*amah* tall. *Namuch* is **low**, the plain Rabbinic word for short in height,
of a person or a fence alike. *Gutz* is **stunted**. Of the three derivations
offered for it, Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein finds the Semitic one more plausible:
the verb *gutz* in Aramaic carries the sense of cutting off, as though the man's
height had been cut short. *Nanas* is the Rabbinic term for a **dwarf**, and
equally for short boards or low walls. *Gamad*, *namuch* and *nanas* are not
confined to people.

The Gemara (Moed Katan 18a) says that the Pharaoh of Moshe's day was but one
*amah* tall; as Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein observes, that gives a different spin
to the scene of Moshe and Aharon standing before him.

## Distinctions

### gomed and gamadim (גומד, גמדים)
The root gimmel-mem-dalet occurs twice in Tanach. Ehud's sword, with which he
struck Eglon, was a *gomed* long (Shoftim 3:16).

- Rashbam (Bava Basra 100a) and Radak (Shoftim 3:16) take *gomed* as the same
  unit of measure as an *amah*.
- *Sefer HeAruch*, Rashi, and Mahari Kara there take it as a truncated *amah*,
  and Rashi uses a cognate of *gidem*, amputee or cut off, to explain that the
  sword was less than an *amah*. Rabbi Yehoshua Steinberg of the Veromemanu
  Foundation infers from this that Rashi read *gomed* as a metathesized form of
  *gidem*, dalet and mem trading places.

The other occurrence is Yechezkel 27:11, the *gamadim* who manned the
watchtowers of Tyre.
- Rashi and Mahari Kara there first explain *gamadim* as seamen adept at
  measuring the depths of the sea with ropes; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein says this
  fits Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein's connection of Hebrew *gamad* to the Ethiopic
  *gwend*, "stick," probably a sort of yardstick used to measure a *gomed*.
- Subsequently Rashi, and Radak there and in *Sefer HaShorashim*, explain
  *gamadim* as short people, no more than an *amah* tall.
- Targum Yonasan there renders *gamadim* as Cappadocians.
- Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (1469-1549) in *Sefer HaTishbi* merges the Targum with
  Rashi's second explanation to posit that the Cappadocians were a nation of
  dwarfs, which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein likens in an aside to the Lilliputians
  of *Gulliver's Travels*.

In Talmudic Hebrew the root means contracting or shrinking: Eretz Yisrael has the
supernatural property of expanding to hold all its inhabitants when settled, and
is *gamda*, contracted or shrunk, when left uninhabited (Gittin 57a; further
examples at Beitzah 15a, Pesachim 111a, Chullin 43a). Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein,
no relation to Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein, connects this to the Arabic *jamada*,
"stiffening, congealing, hardening." As Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein reads him, his
point seems to be that hardening and shrinking are often two sides of one coin,
since in hardening matter typically grows more compact and smaller in its
dimensions. Gamda also appears in the Gemara as a name or nickname, in Rav Gamda, Rav
Chiyya bar Gamda, Rabbi Yehudah ben Gamda, and the Gamda River; and in Modern
Hebrew *gamad* is a garden gnome. Bachur takes *gomed* to be the native Hebrew
word for a short person.

### namuch (נמוך)
Not in Tanach; several times in the Mishnah (*Kilayim* 4:7, *Eruvin* 9:1,
*Zevachim* 1:2), and like *gamad* used of anything low, a short fence as readily
as a short man. Rabbi Dr. Ernest Klein, echoing Bachur's *Sefer HaTishbi*, explains
*namuch* as a secondary base form of the *nifal* of biblical [*mach*](poor-ani-evyon-dal-rash.html#%D7%9E%D7%9B), poor or
lowered: the grammatical nun prefixed to *mach* denotes one who has become
lowered, and the resulting word, which Bachur vocalizes *namoch*, is then handled
as though the nun belonged to the root. The comparison is to the Rabbinic
[*terumah*](waving-terumah-tenufah.html#%D7%AA%D7%A8%D7%95%D7%9E%D7%94), derived from the biblical reish-(vav)-mem of raising and lifting and
then treated as though the initial tav were root. The root of *mach* itself is
given as mem-vav-kaf (Ibn Janach and Radak), mem-kaf-kaf (Ibn Chayyuj), mem-kaf
(Ibn Saruk), or a bare kaf (Rabbi Pappenheim). For more on *mach*, see Rabbi
Reuven Chaim Klein's "The Poor and Unfortunate" (May 2019) and the separate
entry here on the words for poverty.

### gutz (גוץ)
Neither biblical nor Mishnaic, but used several times in the Gemara, masculine
*gutz* and feminine *gutza*. Yevamos 106b discusses the halachic differences in
[*chalitzah*](slipping-off-shemittah-chalitzah-shalaf.html#%D7%97%D7%9C%D7%99%D7%A6%D7%94) where the man or the woman is of especially short stature. Bava
Metzia 59a cites a popular adage, that if your wife is short you should crouch
down to listen to her. Bava Metzia 27b rules that a corpse being merely tall or
short is not enough of a clue to confirm the identity of the deceased. Megillah
27b describes Rav Huna as a *gutza*. And Berachos 31b has Chanah asking that her
son be neither too tall nor too short, too skinny nor too fat, too white nor too
reddish, too intelligent nor too senseless.

Three etymologies are on the table.
- Rabbi Binyamin Mussafia (1606-1675) in *Mussaf HeAruch* writes that *gutz*
  actually comes from Greek.
- Dr. Alexander Kohut (1842-1894) in *Aruch HaShalem* disagrees with that
  supposition, comparing the Hebrew and Aramaic *gutz* to the French *court* /
  *curt* and the Hungarian *kisci*, "small," which Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein
  suggests may be due to the interchange of the gimmel and kuf sounds together
  with some shuffling of consonants; Kohut's etymology broadly follows his
  general observation that many languages have similar sounding terms for one
  phenomenon. Kohut further notes that French *curt* derives from the Latin
  *curtus*, which would relate it to English "short" and to the German and
  Yiddish *kurtz*, and modern linguists trace all of these to the
  Proto-Indo-European root *sker-*, "cutting," the ultimate etymon of scissors,
  shear, scabbard, skirmish, scar, shard, sharp, scrape, shirt, skirt and curt.
- Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein calls a third etymology more plausible, an
  essentially Semitic one: the verb *gutz* in Aramaic generally means gnawing,
  biting or cutting off, so a short man is one whose height seems cut off from
  what one might otherwise have expected. Professor Michael Sokoloff, in *A
  Dictionary of Jewish Babylonian Aramaic of the Talmudic and Geonic Periods*,
  writes that the Aramaic root gimmel-vav-tzadi from which *gutz* derives is a
  later form of the biblical Hebrew root kuf-tzadi-tzadi, which also means "cut
  off."

### nanas (ננס)
The standing Rabbinic word for a dwarf, and the very word Rashi and Radak used
in glossing *gamadim* at Yechezkel 27:11. Bechoros 45b humorously suggests that
an especially tall man not marry an especially tall woman, lest their offspring
be as tall as a ship's mast, and that an especially short man (*nanas*) not marry
an especially short woman (*nanaset*), lest their offspring be as short as a
finger. Like some of the others it is not confined to people: short boards and walls are
*nanasin* (Rashi to Yoma 16b, Pesachim 64a, and to Yechezkel 40:43).
Nevuchadnetzar, a mighty conqueror who was nonetheless short in height, is
described as "the *nanas* of Bavel" (*Pesikta DeRav Kahana* §13:5; see also
*Yalkut Shimoni* to Daniel §1062). Several Tannaim carry *nanas* as part of a
name, or perhaps a nickname: Ben Nanas (*Kesubos* 10:5, *Gittin* 8:10, *Bava
Basra* 7:3 and 10:8, *Shevuos* 7:5) and Rabbi Shimon ben Nanas (*Bikkurim* 3:9,
*Shabbos* 16:5, *Eruvin* 10:15, *Bava Basra* 10:8).

Bachur in *Sefer HaTishbi*, having said that the native Hebrew term for a short
person is *gomed*, equates the Rabbinic *nanas* with the Latin *nano* and with
*ninio*, "baby" in Spanish; those Latinate words derive in turn from the Greek
*nanos* (so *Aruch HaShalem*), which English speakers will recognize as the
prefix in nanosecond, nanogram and nanotechnology. *Nanas*
is entirely unrelated to the Modern Hebrew *ananas*, pineapple, which derives
from the Peruvian *nanas* by way of Spanish.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: "short" will absorb all four. Where a sugya or a commentary pairs two of
  them, render *namuch* "low," *gutz* "stunted" or "short-statured," and *nanas*
  "dwarf."
- *Gomed* in Shoftim 3:16 is a unit of length, not a description of Ehud. Write
  "a *gomed* in length" and gloss it once with the dispute (an *amah*, or less
  than an *amah*); "a cubit long" quietly decides the machlokes.
- *Gamadim* at Yechezkel 27:11 is genuinely contested. Say which Rashi you are
  following, the sounders or the dwarfs, rather than picking silently.
- In halachic contexts *namuch* and *nanas* often describe objects. "A *nanas*
  board" is a low board; do not personify it.
- The Moed Katan 18a description of Pharaoh as one *amah* tall is a statement
  about stature, and Chazal's word for such a man is *nanas*. Keep the register
  plain; Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein himself notes in passing that the English word "midget" is no
  longer socially acceptable, and modern clinical vocabulary does not belong in a
  rendering of a Gemara either.
