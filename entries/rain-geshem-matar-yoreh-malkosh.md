# Rain: geshem / matar / yoreh / malkosh

## Bottom line

*Geshem* and *matar* both mean **rain**, and the authorities split on which is
the general word and which the specific one. *Yoreh* and *malkosh* are rains bound to
their times: *yoreh* is **the first rain**, at the start of the rainy season,
and *malkosh* **the last rain**, in early spring before the harvest.

## At a glance

| Hebrew | translit | English | what sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| גשם | geshem | rain | the commonest word; general rain, or specifically the heavy or blessed rain (the views differ) |
| מטר | matar | rain | rain sent at its time; on one reading heavy rain, hence Sedom's fire and brimstone (Bereishis 19:24) |
| יורה | yoreh | the first rain | opens the season; "teaches" people to secure roofs and store produce (Taanis 6a) |
| מלקוש | malkosh | the last rain | falls near Pesach, before the reaping, from *mekoshesh* (Taanis 6a) |

## Where the authorities disagree

**Which of *geshem* and *matar* is the general term.** There are three
positions, none attributed to a named holder.

1. *Geshem* is the general term for rain of every sort, and *matar* the specific
   one, the "rains of blessing"; a variant of this reads *matar* as heavy rain,
   which fits its use for the fire and brimstone on Sedom (Bereishis 19:24) and
   for the hail in Mitzrayim (Shemos 9:18).
2. The reverse: *matar* is the general term, and *geshem* the specific one, hard
   rain or rain of blessing.
3. The two differ in origin. *Geshem* is rain by the natural
   order, vapors held in the clouds until the winds release them, bound to no
   fixed time; this is why later sources borrowed the word for physical matter,
   *gashmiyus*. *Matar* is rain that Hashem brings directly at set times, and the
   times are the *yoreh* and the *malkosh* of Devarim 11:14.

Rabbi Klein does not choose among them.

## Distinctions

Why does Hebrew have so many words for rain? One answer, Rabbi Klein
says, may simply be that rainfall mattered enormously to an agrarian society; but
since the words of Hebrew bear intrinsic meanings and the Torah is not a
thesaurus, there must be a deeper implication to the multiplicity.

### geshem (גשם) and matar (מטר)
*Geshem* is the more frequent word, and
*matar* is what the pasuk uses in Devarim 11:14 for rain given "in its time,"
with *yoreh* and *malkosh* named as those times. The derivative *gashmiyus*,
materiality, belongs to *geshem* alone, and grows out of the third view above,
that *geshem* is rain by the ordinary running of the world.

Building on Rashi's account of *mabul* (to Bereishis 6:17), Rabbi Moshe Shapiro
explains that the very same waters that fall from the Heavens can serve either
as a destructive *mabul* or as a constructive *geshem*, the difference lying in
whether people deserve that Hashem's rain function as an influx of blessing or
the opposite (see Rashi to Bereishis 7:12). He further explains *geshem* as an
expression of connection between Heaven and earth: superficially, since rain
falls from Heaven to earth, and metaphysically, since Heaven represents
*tzurah* and earth *chomer*, while *geshem* is a physical thing in which
*chomer* and *tzurah* are married together.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro: another word for rain in Tanach and Chazal is *revia*,
which also means union, itself a form of connection.

*Bereishis Rabbah* (47:2) uses *matarin* for the womb, from *matar*; Rabbi Klein
suggests it may also be related to Latin *mater*.

### yoreh (יורה) and malkosh (מלקוש)
Both are seasonal markers, and Taanis 6a explains each from its own root. *Yoreh*
(or *moreh*) carries the senses of shooting and of teaching; it is the season's
first rain, and it teaches people to reinforce their roofs and bring the produce
indoors before the later rains come. *Malkosh* is tied to *mekoshesh*, one who
reaps; it falls at the onset of spring, around Pesach, just before the harvest.

- Ibn Janach and Radak derive three meanings from the one root י-ר-ה: throwing
  (*yarah bayam*, Shemos 15:4), teaching (*yoreh deah*, Yeshayahu 28:9), and
  rain (*yoreh*, *moreh*, Devarim 11:14, Yoel 2:23).
- Menachem Ibn Saruk traces them to the single letter ר and adds a fourth,
  spittle (Vayikra 15:3, Shmuel I 21:14, Iyov 6:6).

None of them suggests a thematic link, and Rabbi Klein proposes that the common
theme is unidirectional movement: a thing thrown goes away from oneself, rain
and spittle flow only downward, and teaching passes from teacher to student and
not the reverse.

### Rabbi Klein's own answer
Rabbi Klein answers that the different words for rain refer to different ways in which Hashem provides for the world, beyond reflecting rain's agricultural importance. Some elements of Creation
need a strong, torrential downpour to be quenched, while others need only a
drizzle and would drown in a deluge. Vegetation grows at different times of the
year, so rain must fall at those different periods. Hashem accommodates all these
considerations, and the Hebrew language reflects them.

## Rendering guidance

- Trap: English "rain" swallows *geshem* and *matar* whole. Where a passage sets
  them against each other, mark the contrast in the prose ("the ordinary rains,
  and the rain He gives in its season") rather than hunting for two English
  nouns.
- Devarim 11:14: "the rain of your land in its time, the first rain and the last
  rain." "Early rain and latter rain" is the older English and is defensible, but
  a note should say that these are the opening and closing rains of one season.
- *Matar* in Bereishis 19:24 and Shemos 9:18 is "rained down," a verb of sending;
  do not soften it to weather.
- *Gashmiyus* is "materiality" or "the physical"; it is a
  derivative of *geshem*, not a word about rain, and should not be glossed as
  though it were.
