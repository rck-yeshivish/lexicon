# Lion: aryeh / kfir / lavi / layish (shachal, shachatz, gur)

## Bottom line

All of these are words for a **lion**, and Chazal count six or seven of them.
The sources divide on what the set is.
The sources of the first group seem to take all seven as the same animal, named
for seven different attributes of it or human reactions to it (Sanhedrin 95a;
*Yalkut Shimoni*). Others take them as stages of a lion's life, and differ among
themselves: Radak's progression runs *gur* the nursing cub, then *kfir*, then
*aryeh*, then *lavi*, then *layish*, each stage stronger than the last, and he
does not say where *shachal* fits; the Vilna Gaon has three stages only, *kfir*,
*aryeh*, *shachal*; Abarbanel flattens the set to two, *gur* and *kfir* young,
*aryeh*, *layish* and *lavi* grown. A minority reads some of these words as other
animals altogether.

## Distinctions

### the seven, and why each is so called
Rabbi Yochanan (Sanhedrin 95a): Tanach has six words for a lion, *ari*, *kfir*,
*lavi*, *layish*, *shachal*, *shachatz*. Rabbi Levi (*Yalkut Shimoni* to Mishlei
20) adds a seventh, *gur aryeh*, and explains each; a like tradition is in *Avos
d'Rabbi Nasan*, version 2, ch. 43.

- *Ari*, because everyone fears it, and *yirah* has the same letters. *Tikkunei
  Zohar* 122a: *aryeh* rearranged is *re'iyah*, sight, the sense lions correspond
  to.
- *Kfir*, because whoever sees a lion denies (*kofer*) his own life. Others read
  it from כ-פ-ר, to cover, as in the *kapores* over the Aron, for the mane that
  covers it.
- *Lavi*, because a lion's roar seizes men's hearts (*levavos*). Rabbi Menachem
  Azariah of Fano: because it eats the heart (*lev*) of its prey first.
- *Layish*, because its teeth knead (*lash*) a man's skin like dough.
- *Shachal*, because men fall sick (*choleh*) from fear before it.
- *Shachatz*, because its teeth are sharp as arrows (*cheitz*).
- *Gur*, which Rabbi Chaim Palagi also reads as an expression of fear (Devarim
  1:17; Iyov 19:29).

### the words as stages of life
- Rashi (to Yechezkel 19:5; Iyov 4:10): *kfir* a young but strong lion, *shachal*
  a middling one, *ari* a large one.
- The Vilna Gaon disagrees. Three stages, each frightening for a different
  reason: young, its teeth frighten, and it is a *kfir*; older, its roar
  frightens, and it is an *aryeh*; older still, even its ordinary voice
  frightens, and it is a *shachal*. So for him *kfir* is the cub, *ari* the
  middle-aged lion, and *shachal* the old one.
- The Baal HaTurim (to Bamidbar 24:9): a *lavi* is younger than an *ari*, without
  saying where it stands against *shachal* and *kfir*.
- Radak (to Shoftim 14:5): the full progression is *gur* (the cub still nursing,
  Eichah 4:3), then *kfir*, then *aryeh*, then *lavi*, then *layish*, and the
  older it grows the stronger it gets. He too leaves *shachal* unplaced.
- Abarbanel (to Shoftim 14:5) rejects so many stages: *gur* and *kfir* are
  synonyms for a young lion, and *aryeh*, *layish* and *lavi* synonyms for an
  older one.
- The Vilna Gaon on the last pair, against Radak: a *layish* is a lioness that
  has not yet borne young, and a *lavi* one that has.

### gur (גור)
Abarbanel (*Mashmia Yeshua*) cites Rabbi Yosef Ibn Kaspi: *gur* is allied to
*ger*, a sojourner, because the cub's agility keeps it always on the move.
Rashbam (to Bereishis 49:9): Yaakov compared Yehudah to a *gur aryeh* because a
cub is faster and stronger than a grown lion.

### kfir (כפיר)
*Avos d'Rabbi Nasan*: an adolescent lion, so called because he denies (*kofer*)
his parents. Rabbi Yechiel Michel Stern: a *kfir* is less fierce than an *aryeh*,
which is why Michah 5:7 has the *kfir* falling on sheep, domesticated and
defenseless, while the *aryeh* takes the beasts of the forest; and Tehillim
104:21 has the *kfir* roaring to ask for its food, meaning it is still too young
to hunt. *Zohar* (Terumah 143a): a *kfir* is a small, weak lion.

### the minority: not lions at all
The Italian scholar Rabbi Yishaya of Trani (to Hoshea 13:8): *lavi* and *laviyah*
are a different species from lions altogether, though he himself admits that
*shachal* and *shachatz* are words for lion. Rabbi Pinchas Eliyahu Horowitz of
Vilna, in his scientific almanac *Sefer HaBris*: *shachal* is a panther and
*layish* a leopard, and he understood these not to be alternate words for "lion."
Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim similarly: *namer*, *lavi* and *shachal* are all words
for a spotted leopard, or perhaps a tiger.

## Rendering guidance

- גור / גור אריה: "lion cub," or "a young lion" where the cub sense would read
  oddly. כפיר: "young lion." אריה / ארי: "lion." לביא: "lion," or "lioness" if
  following the Vilna Gaon. ליש: "old lion," or "lioness" on the same view. שחל
  and שחץ: "lion," with the reading you follow noted.
- Trap: English "lion" covers all seven at once, and both of the famous pesukim
  put two of them in one clause. Bereishis 49:9 has *gur aryeh*, then *aryeh*,
  then *lavi*; Bamidbar 24:9 has *ari* and *lavi*. Render them with two distinct
  English words ("he crouches like a lion, like a great lion, who shall rouse
  him") or the parallel is lost.
- Second trap: the two frameworks are not compatible. Chazal's derashos read all
  seven as one animal under seven names; Radak reads them as an aging sequence.
  Do not mix a derashah from the first into a translation built on the second.
- Where a source is counting the names (Sanhedrin 95a, *Yalkut Shimoni*), the
  names must stay transliterated in the list, or the count cannot be seen.
