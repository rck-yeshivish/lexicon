שתי לשונות דברה תורה (ספרי נשא כג) = זביחה/שחיטה,

When Yosef commanded his chief of staff to prepapre a banquet in honor of his brothers, he said: “Bring the men into the house, and slaughter a slaughtering (*u’tevoach tevach*) and prepare [it], for these men will eat with me in the noon” (Gen. 43:16). We are more familiar with the word *shechitah* which means “slaughtering”, yet here the Torah uses the somewhat obscure word *tevichah*. In this essay, we will discuss how the words *shechitah* and *tevichah* differ from each other, and how they differ from *zevichah*—a third synonym for slaughtering.

There are two more places in the Pentateuch where a cognate of *tevichah* is used. When a thief steals something and is caught, the Torah levies on him a penalty of paying back to the rightful owner twice the value of that which he stole (Ex. 22:3). But, if he stole livestock and then slaughtered or sold it, then he must pay back four (for ovacaprines) or five (for bovines) times the value of the animal (Ex. 21:37). In this context, the word used for “slaughtering” is a cognate of *tevichah*. The next place where such a cognate appears is in Deuteronomy 28 which tells of a series of curses to befall those who fail to keep the Torah and, *inter alia*, reads: “Your ox will be slaughtered (*tavuach*) before your eyes, and you will not eat from it” (Deut. 28:31). Basically all other instances of *tevichah*-related words in the Bible appear outside of the Pentateuch.

Returning to the synonyms at hand, the Malbim explains how they differ from one another. The term *shechitah* refers specifically to the Halachic concept of ritual slaughtering, which requires cutting an animal’s neck through the esophagus and trachea. In contrast, *zevichah* implies slaughtering an animal for a specific purpose, whether as a ritual sacrifice or as food for a festive gathering. Finally, Malbim argues that the word *tevichah* refers to killing an animal for food in a general way—even outside of the context of ritual slaughtering.

While not offering full definitions for the words we are discussing, Gersonides (to Deut. 12:21) notes that the word *zevichah* does not, *per force*, refer to the same act as *shechitah.* Rather, he explains that *zevichah* can also include *nechirah* (“stabbing”), which is a Halachicly not-acceptable way of killing (see Targum pseudo-Jonathan to Num. 22:40). In another context, Gersonides (to Lev. 1:1) writes that *shechitah* and *zevichah* are, in fact, synonyms.

Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740–1814), cited by Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (), offers a slightly different approach. He explains that *shechitah* is a general term for any action which causes the death of a living being. What is colloquially called *shechitah* (i.e. Halachic slaughtering) is included in this term, but is not its full definition. He adduces this view from the fact that the Talmud (*Chullin* 17a) says that for Noahides, “their *nechirah* (stabbing) [of an animal] is their ‘*shechitah*’”. Moreover, when asking of G-d’s forgiveness for the Ten Spies debacle and begging for clemency that He not destroy the Jewish People, Moshe said that if He wipes them out, then the gentiles will say: “Because Hashem lacked the ability to bring this people into the Land… He slaughtered (*vayishchatem*) them in the desert” (Num. 14:16). This does not mean that Hashem was going to literally “slaughter” the Jews, but that He was going to somehow bring about their death. These two usages show that the word *shechitah* includes other forms of killing, besides the specific way of slaughtering prescribed by Halacha.

On the other hand, Rabbi Pappenheim explains that the words *zevichah* and *tevichah* refer to types of *shechitah*. Those two words focus on the *reason* at to why someone would want to kill a living animal: *zevichah* refers to killing an animal for the purposes of ritual sacrifices, while *tevichah* refers to killing an animal for regular human consumption.

Interestingly, there might be an etymological link between *tevichah* and *zevichah*. Both words only differ in the first letter of their root, but share the letters BET-CHET at the end of their roots. The Hebrew word *zevichah* is rendered in Aramaic as *devichah*, with the Hebrew ZAYIN morphing into an Aramaic DALET. The letter DALET, as you may have realized, it sometimes interchangeable with the letter TET, turning *devichah* into *tevichah*. It is therefore understandable that when the Mishnah (*Eduyot* 8:4) declares that liquids in the Temple are ritually pure, there is a point of contention in the Talmud (*Pesachim* 17a) whether this applies to *beit mitbachaya* (the Temple’s “slaughter-house”) or *beit midbachaya* (the Temple’s “sacrifice house”).

Based on the notion that *tevichah* refers to slaughtering an animal for human consumption, Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866–1935) explains why the Torah specifically used that word when referring to the theif’s penalty. If the Torah did not use the word *tevicha*, one might have otherwise though that the penalty only applies to a thief who slaughters the stolen animal for no good reason, or to anger its rightful owner. But if he slaughtered the animal to eat its meat, the thief could excuse his deplorable actions by claiming because that he slaughtered the stolen animal for the purposes of eating it (not to make a profit), and should be exempt for any penalties. For this reason, the Torah uses the word *tevichah* when telling of the theif’s penalty; that is, to stress that even though he slaughtered the animal in order to eat from its meat, he is still held responsible and the penalties mentioned above are still levied upon him.

Until this point, we have assumed that *tevichah* means “slaughtering”. However, Rabbi Wertheimer points to another understanding of that word. He cites R. Yonah ibn Janach (990–1050), who contends that *tevichah* actually means “cooking” (as does the verb *tovech* in Arabic). Accordingly, Ibn Janach understood that Joseph did not tell his chief of staff to “slaughter” meat for his banquet, but to “cook” meat. Nonetheless, Rabbi Wertheimer points out that in many rabbinic sources, *tevichah* certainly refers to “slaughtering”, not “cooking”. In later times, a person described as a *tabach* was a “butcher” (who cuts up meat that has alreay been slaughtered), or a “cook”. The Modern Hebrew cognate, *mitbach,* means “kitchen”.

The occupations of two people in the Bible are described with *tevichah*-related words: Nebuzaradan, the Babylonian general who destroyed the First Temple in Jerusalem, is described (in II Kgs. and Jer.) as *rav ha-tabachim* (“chief of *tabachim*”); and Potiphar, the Egyptian courtier to whom Yosef was sold, as *sar ha-tabachim* (“minister of *tabachim*”). Rashi (to Gen. 37:36) explains that Potiphar was in charge of all those who slaughtered the king’s animals. However, Ibn Ezra and Nachmanides (there) follow Targum Onkelos in explaining that *sar ha-tabachim* refers to Potiphar’s role as Chief Executioner (or at least the Chief Warden of those on death row). This also seems to be the meaning of Nebuzaradan’s title. The connection between *tabach* as an executioner and *tabach* as a slaughterer is obvious: an executioner slays people in the same way that a slaughterer slays animals. [Truth be told, Ibn Ezra contradicts himself because elsewhere (to Dan. 2:14), he writes that Potiphar was the Chief Cook at Pharoah’s court, not his Chief Executioner.]
