# My Husband, My Man

*Much of contemporary discourse is shaped by ideas made popular by feminism that brought the notion of gender power imbalances to the forefront of discussion. Feminism has also contributed to our recognition of issues like patriarchal domination, phallic adoration, monogamous exclusivity, marital rape, and bodily autonomy. The development and awareness of such ideas may reflect the fulfillment of Hosea’s prophecy about the future, in which these sorts of issues will not only influence the relationship between the sexes, but will also affect the Jewish People’s relationship with G-d.*

In the *Haftarah* to *Parashat Bamidbar*, the prophet Hosea foretells of a time when the Jewish People will change the terminology they use to refer to G-d as their figurative “husband.” As Hosea puts it, “It will be on that day — this is the word of G-d — that you will call Me ‘my husband’ (*ishi*) and you will not call Me ‘my husband’ (*baali*) any longer” (Hos. 2:18). In other words, the Jewish People will no longer call G-d “my *baal,*” but will rather call Him “my *ish*.” Both *baal* and *ish* roughly mean “husband,” yet the thrust of Hosea’s prophecy is that in the future, there will be a paradigm shift in the way the Jewish People relate to G-d. How is that paradigm shift reflected in switching out the term *baal* for *ish*?

The word *baal* not only means “husband,” but it is also a proper noun that serves as the name of the chief deity of the Canaanite pantheon. Throughout the Bible, the Jews dallied with Baal-worship and many of the prophets endeavored to break the Jews of that idolatrous habit.  Thus, according to Radak, Abarbanel, and Maharsha, Hosea predicts that after the exile, the Jews will repent and return to G-d so completely that they will no longer be able to use the term *baali* in reference to Him, because they will be so motivated to distance themselves from the homonymous idol Baal. Instead, they will use a synonym of *baali* by referring to G-d as their “husband” with the word *ishi*.

Interestingly, Jerusalemic Talmud (*Avodah Zarah* 3:6) states the Baal idol was shaped like the male member, and Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch (to Num. 23:13) that the very word *baal* is etymologically related to the Greek word *phallus* (via the interchangeability of BET and FEH). Thus, there is even a thematic connection between *baal* in the sense of “husband” and Baal the Canaanite deity. According to this, since the Jews will want to avoid anything that is even remotely connected to idolatrous practice, they will not even use the word *baal* in the sense of “husband” to refer to G-d and will replace the term with *ish*. This implies that Hosea was speaking of a mere lexical shift between the use of different synonyms.

However, other commentators do not view *baali* and *ishi* as perfect synonyms. Instead, they view the shift in terminology as reflective of a shift in the Jewish People’s relationship with G-d. In the rest of this essay, we will explore the various ways in which we can understand how the terms *baali* and *ishi* (which both seem to mean “my husband”) differ from one another. Many of the explanations which we will cite point in the same direction, but there are slight differences and nuances between them.

In explicating the above-mentioned passage from Hosea, the Babylonian Talmud (*Ketubot* 71b) cites Rabbi Yochanan as explaining that Hosea means that the Jewish Nation will relate to G-d “like a bride in her father-in-law’s home, not like a bride in her father’s home.” This cryptic formulation can be understood by juxtaposing two passages in Rashi. In his commentary to the Talmud (there), Rashi notes that the term *ishi* implies “conjugality and marriage,” but he does not explain the connotation of *baali*. In his commentary to Hosea (there), Rashi notes that the term *baali* implies “lording and awe,” but he does not explain the connotation of *ishi*. Maharsha suggests putting these two pieces of the puzzle together, and it is evident from Rashi’s First Edition (quoted in the *Shittah Mekubetzet* to *Ketubot* 71b) that this is indeed Rashi’s intention.

What emerges from this juxtaposition is that the term *baali* is an expression of mere “ownership” — like one who lords over his possessions in the way that the owner of an ox is called the *Baal HaShor* (Ex. 21:28) — that does not truly reflect the intimate partnership between man and wife. As a result, when the Jewish People related to G-d as *baali*, this can be likened to a betrothed bride who still lives in her father’s house, but has technically already been “acquired” by her husband-to-be. In that case, the “owner” technically lords over his subject, but there is no reciprocal relationship.

Hosea’s prophecy thus foretells of a future time when the Jewish People no longer relate to G-d as *baali*, but as *ishi*, a term which connotes intimate familiarity, with both partners enjoying a mutual relationship with one another. This resembles a bride who has already moved into the home of her husband’s family and is already enjoying married life with him. In that case, there is a two-way relationship, with neither partner lording over the other (see also Malbim to Hos. 2:18, who offers a similar understanding).

In a similar vein, Rabbi Yosef Dov Soloveitchik (1820–1892) also understands that *ishi* and *baali* do not mean the exact same thing, but he interprets the verse in question slightly differently. He posits that the word *ishi* means “my husband” and denotes a formal union of man and wife that expects fidelity on the part of the parties involved. The word *baali*, on the other hand, simply refers to a pair of partners who engage in an informal relationship without the covenantal expectation of monogamy. As the idolatrous Jews of Hosea’s times looked at it, the lack of a formal matrimonial pact of devotion leaves them free to worship other gods alongside G-d. Accordingly, Rabbi Soloveitchik explains that Hosea means that in the future, the Jews will no longer consider G-d their *baal*, which would allow them to engage in idolatrous trysts with other deities. Rather, they will consider Him their *ish*, i.e., their eternal partner to Whom they are forever attached—to the exclusion of anyone else.

The way Rabbi Yitzchak Isaac Chaver (1789–1853) explains it, even the term *baali* implies the expectation of monogamy and loyalty. He explains that the difference between *baali* and *ishi*, is that while the former simply implies that the groom “acquires” the bride in the sense that she can now no longer be wed to somebody else, the term *ishi* implies an even more complete relationship between the bride and groom, whereby they have joined to become of one flesh. He cites Adam’s linguistic observation in naming woman *ishah*, wherein Adam states, “This [i.e., Eve] shall be called *ishah* [‘woman’], for this was taken from *ish* [‘man’]” (Gen. 2:23). In this short declaration, Adam recognizes that the Hebrew words *ish* (“man”) and *ishah* (“woman”) share a common root; they are of one flesh.

The Maharal of Prague (in *Netzach Yisrael*, ch. 47) similarly writes that the difference between the word *ish* as “husband” and the word *baal* as “husband” is that the former implies a united connection between the two spouses, while the latter simply connotes a husband as the active party in the act of intimacy. The Maharal explains that the word *ish* recalls the notion that the sacred bond of matrimony is sealed with the name of G-d, as the difference between the word *ish* (spelled with a YOD, but without a HEY) and the word *ishah* (spelled with a HEY, but without a YOD) yields the first two letters of G-d’s Four-Letter name. This demonstrates that *ish* implies a stronger connection of man and wife (who are joined together as equal partners). By contrast, explains the Maharal, the term *baal* is derived from the verb for the act of copulation (*bo’el*), wherein the man is the active partner, and the woman remains passive. This latter term represents a sort of imbalance in the relationship between man and wife that Hosea’s vision anticipates will be corrected in the future.

In line with this, the Maharal explains that Hosea’s vision relays the idea that while the Jewish People originally viewed G-d as He who simply acts upon them (*baal*) — in the sense that their will does not always line up with His — in the future, the Jewish People will recognize themselves as totally bonded with Him (*ish*) and will align their own wants and desires with His.

The Vilna Gaon (to Deut. 21:13) explains that G-d showers the Jewish People with His generous abundance for two different reasons. The first reason is that G-d is the benevolent Master of the Universe and He wishes to bestow His goodness and kindness unto all creatures that He had created. The second is that G-d loves the Jewish People with a sort of love that is analogous to the love between a man and wife. Accordingly, Hosea explains that initially the Jewish People looked at themselves vis-à-vis their relationship to G-d in terms of His role as the Master of the Universe (*baal*), so they worshipped him simply because He bestows his influx of weal upon them. This is conceptually similar to the idolatrous conception of god(s) as the mechanical source of various benefits with whom practitioners are not necessarily expected to forge any relationship. However, Hosea foresees of a time when the Jewish People will not simply relate to G-d as the One who provides them with goods, but as the One who loves them like a man loves his wife (*ish*).

Moreover, the Vilna Gaon (to Hos. 2:18) explains that because the term *baal* derives from the act of intimacy (*be’ilah*), it implies a way of approaching marriage that focuses on the sensual pleasures that it brings. This parallels those who approach G-d as simply the entity that provides them with all sorts of goodies, but has not real connection to them. That conception again reflects the idolatrous view typified by Baal worshippers who viewed worshipping god(s) as simply a means of receiving certain rewards, but without having any bond or responsibility to those gods. By contrast, Hosea foretells that the Jewish People will see G-d through the lens of a marriage-like covenant, whereby they have a certain responsibility towards Him and do not view Him as just as a means by which they can receive all sorts of worldly pleasures. [See also Vilna Gaon (to Gen. 2:23, Song of Songs 1:17, and to *Tikkunei Zohar, Tikkun* #30).]

Based on the interchangeability of the letters BET and PEH, Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch (to Gen. 20:3, Ps. 28:4) notes that the word *baal* is related to the word *pa’al* (“action”). An “action” is a person’s way of controlling a situation and doing what he can towards attaining certain objective. In a similar way, *baal* connotes the act of “lording” or “domineering” over a subdued and subjugated vassal. Just like *pa’al, baal* refers to he who *initiates* an activity, as opposed to the passive, subdued partner who does not initiate but only receives. Rabbi Yehoshua (Jeremy) Steinberg of the Veromemanu Foundation suggests that this idea is also hinted to in the word *bala* (“swallowing”) — whose root BET-LAMMED-AYIN is a metathesis of *baal* (BET-AYIN-LAMMED). This connection highlights the aggressive, and sometimes egotistical, side of human nature that often seeks to dominate and acquire possessions and human beings alike by “swallowing them up” and “lording” over them.

In an explanation that echoes many of the ideas that we have already seen above, Rabbi Hirsch adds that the term *baal* as “husband” implies a power dynamic whereby the male spouse lords over his wife as though she were his subordinate. Based on this, Rabbi Hirsch interprets Hosea’s revelation as referring to a time when the Jewish People viewed themselves as totally humbled, and perhaps even downtrodden, in the way they approached G-d. This attitude is reflected by them calling Him *baali*. But Hosea’s prophecy states that afterwards, the Jews will regain their self-confidence and instead view themselves as equal partners with G-d; they will proudly walk in His path without feeling dominated or subjugated. That outlook can be best characterized by the term *ishi*.

Rabbi Meir Emden (1717–1798), a son of Rabbi Yaakov Emden (1698–1776), offers an alternate take on the difference between *ishi* and *baali* that jibes quite nicely with elements of what he have cited above. He does not view Hosea’s vision as relating to something in the future, but as foretelling of a paradigm shift that has already occurred. The Talmud (*Shabbat* 88a) relates that when the Jews stood at Mount Sinai to receive the Torah, G-d took the mountain like a barrel and held it over the nation, threatening that if they do not accept the Torah, they will be buried there on the spot. The Talmud reports that while the Jews ultimately accepted the Torah, their acceptance cannot be said to be free of duress. However, generations later with the impending genocide decreed by Haman looming on the horizon, the Jewish People effectuated a mass repentance movement, and re-accepted the Torah out of their own volition. As the Book of Esther reports this episode: “The Jews fulfilled and accepted upon themselves and their offspring and all those who accompany them..." (Est. 9:27)

Based on this, Rabbi Meir Emden writes that the Jews originally related to G-d as *baali*, because they felt compelled to accept the Torah and viewed G-d as somewhat of a “coercive owner” who lorded over them and forced them into accepting the Torah. However, as Hosea foretold, the Jews in the time of Ahasuerus later changed their tune and accepted the Torah of their own free will. From then on, they no longer viewed G-d as their coercive *baal*, but as their loving *ish*.[^241]
