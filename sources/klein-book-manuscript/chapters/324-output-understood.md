# Output Understood

<!-- Devarim / Ki Savo -->

בענין גלל ואפשה ראה החלקאות בארץ ישראל (יהודה פליקס) עמ' 88

*Trigger Warning: As citizens of the real world, we are all cognizant of the fact that not everything is always clean and dandy like shaving cream. We sometimes have to get our hands dirty to deal with the complexities and realities of life, even when it’s not all rosy and may even offend our Victorian sensibilities. With that caveat in mind, this article discusses an otherwise “filthy” and “dirty” topic in a mature and serious way. Rabbi Akiva said about learning the Halachot for conducting oneself in the bathroom, “It is Torah and I must learn it” (Brachot 62a), and the same is true of our topic. The rabbis of yore were not prudes, and neither should we be; but reader discretion is advised and one might not want to read this article at the Shabbat table*. *To put it bluntly, this essay discusses various Hebrew words for “excrement,” traces them back to their etymological roots, and shows the nuanced differences between these synonyms.*

When we think of the Hebrew word *zevel,* we generally think of “trash,” “garbage,” or “rubbish.” However, this is a Modern Hebrew neologism; if you look at the original Hebrew sources, a different picture emerges. The word *zevel* never appears in the entire Bible, but it does appear in many places in the Mishnah, where it typically means “animal manure.” Rabbi Tanchum the Jerusalemite (a 13th century grammarian) writes that the noun *zevel* is derived from the verb act of fertilizing a field. Thus, *zevel* seems to focus specifically on “animal excrement.”

The Hebrew word *peresh* in the sense of “excreta” appears seven times in the Bible, always in the context of sacrificial meats (Ex. 29:14, Lev. 4:11, 8:17, 16:27, Num. 19:5, and Mal. 2:3). Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740–1814) and Rabbi Shmuel Strashun of Vilna (1794–1872) explain that *peresh* refers specifically to fecal matter found within a slaughtered animal’s intestines that was never excreted. Rabbi Strashun contrasts this term with *zevel*, which refers to that which had already been excreted.

Early Hebrew lexicographers (like Menachem Ibn Saruk, Yonah Ibn Janach, and Radak) trace the word *peresh* to the triliteral root PEH-REISH-SHIN, which also means “spreading/separating/elucidating.” In that sense, *peresh* refers to “animal droppings” as something strewn about by animals, even if it technically refers to such waste *before* it was actually expelled from the body. Rabbi Pappenheim explains the term *peresh* as referring to the byproducts of digestion that are “separated” from food’s nutrients and not absorbed by the body.

Another Hebrew word for “excrement” is *roey*/*ray’ee/re’ee* (Job 33:21, Nah. 3:6, Nah. 3:6, Zep. 3:1). Although spelled with a REISH-ALEPH, Rabbi Pappenheim traces this word to the biliteral root REISH-VAV, which means “quenching/satisfying.” He explains that this term denotes excrement that results from excess eating such that the body cannot properly absorb all the nutrients consumed, so it passes through and is defecated. This word appears four times in the Mishnah, once spelled REISH-ALEPH-YOD (*Machshirin* 6:7) and thrice spelled REISH-AYIN-YOD (*Shabbat* 20:4, *Makkot* 3:14, *Keilim* 17:2), as the Biblical Hebrew ALEPH morphed into an AYIN in Mishnaic Hebrew (see Mizrachi to Lev. 1:16 and Rabbi Avraham Bakrat's *Sefer Zikaron* there). Rashi (to Lev. 1:16) connects the word *moor’ah* (“crop,” i.e., the internal organ in which a bird stores its partially digested food) to *re’ee*. [HaBachur vowelizes the Mishnaic form of this word as *ray’ee*, but the Kaufmann MS of the Mishnah usually vowelizes it as *re’ee*, except in *Makkot*, where it vowelizes the word as *ree’ee*.]

The Hebrew word *galal* in the sense of “dung” appears five times in the Bible (I Kgs. 14:10, Job 20:7, Zeph. 1:17, Ezek. 4:12, 4:15). Menachem Ibn Saruk (920–970) traces *galal* to the biliteral root GIMMEL-LAMMED, whose core meaning Rabbi Pappenheim explains is “roundness.” Other words derived from this root include *galgal* (“wheel”), *gulgolet* (“skull”), *gal* (a “heap” of stones in a circular formation), and *megillah* (a “scroll,” which is rolled up). In that spirit, the term *galal* focuses on the *round, cylindrical form* of dung pellets. As a later borrowed usage, *galal* also came to refer to any item left outside that can be public nuisance, like “trash” (see Rashi to *Bava Kama* 29b and *Bava Batra* 28a) or “stones” (see Rashi to *Bava Kamma* 92b and *Sanhedrin* 109b). Moreover, Rashi (to Deut. 29:16) explains that the Biblical term *gilulim* (“idols”) is related to *galal* because idols are putrid and disgusting like excrement.

Another word for “excrement” is *tzoeh*. When the Torah describes the need to establish a bathroom outside the Jewish Army’s encampment, it talks about “covering your *tzoeh*” (Deut. 23:14). This is the most common word for “excrement” in the Mishnah (*Brachot* 3:5, *Shabbat* 16:7, *Avodah Zarah* 4:5, *Keilim* 10:2, *Mikvaot* 9:2, 9:4, *Machshirin* 5:6). Both in the Bible and in the Mishnah, *tzoeh* refers exclusively to human feces, never to animal droppings.

The Hebrew grammarians unanimously understand the word *tzoeh* as a cognate of the word *yetziah* (“exit”), with Radak tracing both terms to the triliteral root YOD-TZADI-ALEPH, Menachem tracing them to the biliteral TZADI-ALEPH, and Rabbi Pappenheim tracing them to the monoliteral TZADI. Rabbi Pappenheim clarifies that since *tzoeh* refers to any waste product that exits the body, it is also used in reference to “vomit” (Isa. 28:8). Interestingly, the English word *excrement* itself is semantically similar to the etymologies of the Hebrew terms for excrement, as *ex-* means “[goes] out” (like *tzoeh*), and *crement* derives from the Latin *cernere,* which means “to sift/separate” (like *peresh*).

The Hebrew word *chara* (and Arabic *khara*) for “excrement” is considered especially vulgar and is therefore uncommonly used. However, this word actually appears in the Bible—sort of. The rabbis (*Megillah* 25b) list several instances in which the traditional Masoretic *reading* of a word in the Bible differs from the traditional way of *writing* that word. In such cases, the written words were considered so offensive and distasteful, that tradition maintains they should not be uttered verbally, but instead replaced with different, cleaner words when reading the Bible.

Here are four examples: the Bible curses those who fail to keep the Torah with “untreatable hemorrhoids,” using the word *afolim* in the written text and *techorim* in the oral text (Deut. 28:27). Secondly, the Bible reports that a famine in Samaria was so severe that “guano” (manure made from dove droppings) became very expensive, using the word *charyonim* (“the *chara* of doves”) in the written text and *divyonim* (“that which flows from doves”) in the oral text (II Kgs. 6:25). Thirdly, the Bible reports that the Assyrian operative Rabshakeh talked trash about the people of Judah saying that they “sit on the city wall to eat their excrement,” using the word *chara* for “excrement” in the written text, but *tzoeh* in the oral text (II Kgs. 18:27, Isa. 36:12). Finally, the Bible reports that Jehu destroyed the Temple of Baal and converted it to a “lavatory,” using a cognate of *chara* in the written text and a cognate of *tzoeh* in the oral text (II Kgs. 10:27). In the last three cases, forms of the vulgar word *chara* are instead read as cognates of the cleaner term *tzoeh*.

Menachem and Rabbi Pappenheim trace the word *chara* to the biliteral root CHET-REISH, with Menachem explaining that one of the meanings of that root is “hole” (like in *chor*), thus alluding to the anus from which excrement exits the body. Rabbi Pappenheim has a more sophisticated way of explaining the biliteral root CHET-REISH, finding its core meaning to be “other than the one in front of us.” Accordingly, he explains the word *acher* (“other/another”) as derived from this root, as well as *machar* (“tomorrow,” i.e., a day other than today), *achar* (“after,” i.e., a time other than now), and *achor* (“back,” i.e., the side other than the one facing us). Rabbi Pappenheim then explains CHET-REISH in the sense of “hole” as a derivative of *achor*, as all human beings have a “hole” on their “backside.” *Chara* is thus the waste that exits the body through the hole in the posterior—a very crude way of referring to “excrement.”

We mentioned earlier that when the word *afolim* (“hemorrhoids”) is written in the Bible, it is read as the cleaner term *techorim.* The early lexicographers (i.e., Menachem, Ibn Janach, and Radak) trace the word *techorim* to the triliteral root TET-CHET-REISH, which just means “hemorrhoids.” However, Rabbi Yitzchak of Zeldin writes in *Shoresh Yesha* that *techorim* is actually a portmanteau of the biliteral roots TET-CHET (“trajectory path”) and CHET-REISH (“hole”). Similarly, Rabbi Aharon Marcus (1843–1916) relates the word *techorim* to *achor* (“behind”), alluding to the location of the body parts afflicted by that malady. This leads us to an interesting point, as it seems that somehow *techorim* is a cleaner word than *afolim*, even if *techorim* seems to be derived from the same root as *chara*, which is considered so offensive that it is always read as *tzoeh*!

Perhaps because of this difficulty, Rabbi Yaakov Yehuda Zilberberg (1914–2003) offers an alternate etymology of the word *techorim*. He relates *techorim* to the triliteral root TET-REISH-CHET (*tircha*, “burden, bother, hassle”) by way of metathesis — that is, a reordering of the consonants — to explain that “hemorrhoids” and the like are quite *bothersome* to a person.

Moreover, Rabbi Shabsai Donnolo (913-982) uses the word *techor* to refer to the “rectum” that connects to the small intestine. I have not seen any earlier sources that similarly use this word in an anatomical, neutral way as referring to a body part, but if *techor* primarily has such a meaning, then I can see how *techor*/*techorim* is less offensive that *afolim*.

Interestingly, Rabbi Shet bar Yefet in *Chemat Hachemdah* (to Num. 14:44) explains that the root of *afolim*, AYIN-PEH-LAMMED, is related to the root ALEPH-PEH-LAMMED (“darkness”), by way of the interchangeability of the letters AYIN and ALEPH. As a result, he explains that *techorim* are called *afolim* because they are lesions that occur in the “dark” part of one’s body (i.e., where the sun don’t shine).[^323]
