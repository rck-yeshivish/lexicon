The Book of Ruth tells the story of how Boaz took the responsibility for redeeming the fields of his impoverished cousin Naomi and ended up marrying Naomi’s daughter-in-law, Ruth. Encouraged by her former mother-in-law, Ruth came to Boaz’s grainery in the dead of night in order to offer Boaz the chance to marry her. On principle, Boaz refused to do so straight away because he argued that the right to do so belonged to a closer relative—Naomi’s uncle—and Boaz did not wish to aboragte the latter’s right. The next day, that relative rejected his opportunity to marry Ruth and finally, Boaz sealed the deal and took her “under his wing”.

When the Book of Ruth reports on Boaz’s midnight encounter with Ruth, it says, “In the middle of the night, the man [Boaz] was startled, and felt about—and behold, there was a woman [Ruth] lying at his feet” (Ruth 3:8). The Midrash *Ruth Rabbah* and Rashi explain that initially when Boaz sensed the presence of another, he was scared because he suspected that a *sheid* (demon) came to visit him. However, when he felt that his visitor’s head had hair, he realized that she was human because, as the *Zohar* (*Bereishit* 54b) and *Sefer Chassidim* (§1155) explain, female demons do not have hair.

What are these *sheidim* (plural for *sheid*) we speak of? The Talmud (*Chagigah* 16a) relates that *sheidim* are similar to angels in some ways and similar to people in some ways: They are similar to angels in that they have wings, they fly about the world, and they have access to Heavenly information about the future. They are similar to humans in that they eat, drink, reproduce, and die like humans.

The Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 109a) further relates the punishment for some of the partcipants in the Tower of Babel was that they morphed into *kofim* (monkey), *ruchin*, *sheidim*, and *lilin* (plural for Lilith). What is the difference between *ruchin*, *sheidim*, and *lilin*?

Rashi explains: *sheidim* have a humanoid form and they eat and drink like humans; *ruchin* have no body or form; and *lilin* take on the shape of humans, but also have wings. [This implies that *sheidim* do not have wings, even though we mentioned above that the Talmud says they do have wings. Some answer that the type of *sheidim* into which the sinners at the Tower of Babel turned did not have wings, even if general *sheidim* usually do have wings.]

The Talmud (*Eruvin* 100b) maintains that one of the ten curses afflicted upon Eve in the aftermath of the Forbidden Fruit debacle was that she was doomed to growing hair like a Lilith. Rashi there explains that Lilith is a type of *sheid*. Rabbi Yehudah Fatiyah (1859–1942) further notes that Rashi wrote that a Lilith is a type of *sheid* (male demon) not *sheidah* (demoness) because female *sheidim* do not have hair (as we saw above), only male *sheidim* do.

Others explain that while female *sheidim* generally do not have hair on their head, there is one female *sheid* that does have hair on her head: Lilith. Lilith is the name of a certain *sheidah*—more specifically, the mother of all *sheidim*. The *Zohar* also says that Lilith is the consort to the Satan/Samael (whatever that means). According to this explanation, unlike other female *sheidim*, Lilith does have hair. Nonetheless, when Boaz felt his visitor’s hair, his fears of a demon were assuaged because Lilith generally visits men only when they are asleep (not when they are awake).

Although we have cited the Kabbalistic approach that understands that Lilith is the name of a specific demoness, others understand differently. They maintain that Lilith is a type of female demon—a category as opposed to a proper name. In truth, the word Lilith first appears in Isaiah 34:14 and the commentators there argue about its correct meaning: Rashi says it is the name of a female *sheid*, Ibn Ezra says it is a type of nocturnal bird, and Radak says it is some sort of nocturnal animal.

The first two of these explanations are supported by the Talmud (*Niddah* 24b) which speaks of a woman who miscarries a child in the form of a Lilith and it is evident from the ensuing discussion that the child had wings—so Lilith must be a demon or a bird. In that context, Rashi again explains that a Lilith is a female demon, but adds more description by noting that *sheidim* have the face of humans, but also have wings.

To sum up, a *sheid* is a demon, a *Lilith* is a female demon (possibly the name of a specific female demon), and a *ruach* (which literally means “wind” or “spirit”) is an incorporeal apparition. So when you’re staying up late Shavuot night learning Torah and you suddenly see the Jersey Devil or otherwise feel the need to call Ghostbusters, just remember that Boaz too was scared of demons and it turned out to be a great big nothingburger.[^289]
