# A Crown is a Crown

<!-- Bamidbar / Naso -->

Rabbi Avi Grossman: A keter, based on the classical literature, is a crown that is not part of the body that wears it, and even when placed, can be removed. Thus kings are coronated, and their crowns pass on to successors. A nezer is granted to the wearer, but can never be removed or passed on. Thus, priesthood and high priest hood are forms of nezer, and the nazirite's also, although his can be removed from a unique process not analogous to the removal of a keter. Similarly, certain temple appointment shad n'zarim of gold, that were fashioned and then attached, never to be removed. Atara refers to a "crown" that is a natural feature of an object. Thus, the joy of emancipated slaves or the glory of the righteous in the world to come are both referred to as ataroth, and also physically, when describing the male anatomy, or that of the sun.

Aaron Koller’s essay, "Learning from the *Tāg*: On a Persian Word for ‘Crown’ in Jewish Aramaic," Koller, A. J. Learning from the Tag: On a Persian Word for'Crown'in Jewish Aramaic. *Shoshannat Yaakov*, 237-246. provides a philological and cultural analysis of the word *tāg* (crown) to understand its significance in rabbinic literature, specifically in an aphorism attributed to Hillel the Elder. The essay's key points include: Etymology and Linguistic Context A Persian Loanword: The word *tāg* is a core borrowing from Persian into Aramaic. Koller notes that this is significant because Aramaic already possessed a common word for crown, *kālîl*, meaning the adoption of *tāg* was likely motivated by its specific "prestige" and cultural connotations. A "Babylonianism": The word is found in Eastern Aramaic dialects (Babylonian Jewish Aramaic and Mandaic) but is entirely absent from Western/Judean Aramaic sources. Its presence in the Mishnah (mAvot 1:13) in a saying by Hillel—who was a Babylonian immigrant—suggests either the historical authenticity of the quote or a sophisticated use of literary code-switching by the Mishnah's editors to match Hillel’s persona. The Symbolism of the Persian Crown Exclusivity and Power: In Persian culture, from the Achaemenid to Sasanian periods, the *tāg* was a distinctive, jagged, or crenelated crown that served as a marker of the monarchy's rarified existence.Aura of Remoteness: Koller cites traditions describing the Sasanian royal crown as so heavy it had to be suspended by a chain from the ceiling, creating a sense of awe and inaccessibility for visitors. This sense of the crown being "off-limits" explains why the word was perfect for Hillel's warning that "anyone who uses the crown (*tāgâ*) dies". Application to the "Crown of Torah" Sacred vs. Secular: By using a Persian term associated with the elite and restrictive nature of the Persian monarchy, Hillel’s aphorism emphasizes that the "crown of Torah" is sacred and must not be exploited for personal use. Shift in Meaning: While the term initially referred to a metaphorical crown of leadership or Torah in Hillel's time, it later evolved in Jewish tradition to refer to the physical crowns (*tāgîn*) placed on the letters of a Torah scroll, which were famously used by Rabbi Akiva to derive "piles and piles of laws". In summary, Koller argues that the choice of the word *tāg* was not accidental; it leveraged the symbolic weight of Persian imperial power to define the boundaries of religious authority and the sanctity of Torah study.

The Bible uses three different words to mean “crown”: *keter, atarah,* and *nezer*. Some explain that the word *keter* is a general term which refers to crowns, while the other two are specific types of crowns. However, most commentators assume that the three words represent three different types of crowns.

Malbim defines these three words by explaining the differences in their physical properties. He explains that an *atarah* is the type of crown which completely surrounds the circumference of one’s head and rises above the head’s height, while the *keter* is a crown which circles around one’s head, but does not rise above the head’s height (i.e. a coronet). A *nezer* is a type of crown which only encircles half of the wearer’s head (i.e. a tiara). Others explain that an *atarah* refers specifically to the type of crown which is covered on top, while the others refer to crowns which are not necessarily so.

The conceptualization of the physical aspects of *keter* and *atarah*—which surround the wearer’s head—are used metaphorically in the Bible to refer to any type of surrounding (similar to the English word *circlet*—a type of crown—which conjures the word circle). Similarly, the Aramaic word for “crown”, *klil* also refers to something which encompasses another. Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785–1865) reveals that *kallah,* the Hebrew word for bride, is derived from the Aramaic word for crown. He explains that just as a crown wholly surrounds the head of the one who wears it, a *kallah* is wholly surrounded with adornments and jewelry.

Dr. Michael Sokoloff's *Dictionary of Jewish Palestinian Aramaic of the Byzantne Period* defines the word *klil* as a garland (i.e. a wreath of leaves/flowers worn on the head). Based on this, Professor Steven Fine from YU claims that when the Shabbos liturgy describes Moses as wearing a *klil* on his head, this refers to a wreath of laurels rather than to an actual crown.

In addition to the physical characteristics which set apart these types of crowns from one another, Malbim explains that they also differ on a more abstract, thematic level. He explains that a *keter* specifically denotes a *royal* crown, while an *atarah* may denote any type of crown. In fact, all three times that the word *keter* appears in the Bible (Esther 2:17, 6:8, 1:11), it appears in the construct phrase *keter-malchut,* royal crown (like the soft drink!).

The word *nezer* is related to the word *zer* (diadem), which appears ten times in the book of Exodus, when describing the golden ornamental “crowns” which are to adorn some vessels in the *Mishkan* (Tabernacle). But, the word *nezer* connotes much more than that. For example, it serves as the root of the word *nazir,* Nazirite. The Torah explains that a Nazirite—one who has foresworn drinking wine, cutting his hair, and ritual impurity—is considered especially holy “because the *nezer* of G-d is upon his head” (Numbers 6:7). The physical manifestation of this “crown” is the Nazirite’s long hair, but the spiritual manifestation of the Nazirite’s uniqueness is the way that he separates himself from the common folk. The same expression is used when explaining the reason for the special laws concerning the Kohen Gadol (High Priest). About those laws, the Torah says, “Because the *nezer*—anointing oil—of his G-d is upon him…” (Leviticus 21:12). The Kohen Gadol is also separated from the rest of the nation in both physical (e.g., the priestly vestments) and spiritual ways. From this, we see that the word *nezer* connotes a crown specifically used as a means of demarcation or separation which shows one’s significance and sets him above the rest.

In addition to the three Hebrew words and the one Aramaic word mentioned above, there is a fifth word for crown used in rabbinic idiom: *tāg/tāga*. This word is commonly used when referring to the “crowns” drawn on top of certain letters in a Torah Scroll (known as *tāgin*). Linguists claim that *tag* is a Persian loanword that was later adopted by Aramaic. Interestingly, in light of our understanding of the role of a crown in marking its wearer as special, we can easily see how the Aramaic word *tāg* became the English word *tag*, which also serves as a marker of sorts.[^243]
