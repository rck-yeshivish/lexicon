# Listen up!

<!-- Devarim / Voeschanan -->

ציית

Rabbi Eliyahu benamozegh (eim le-mikra to gen. 38:17) says that הסכת is related to שתק beause ש=ס and כ=ק.

“Listen O heavens, and I will speak/and the earth shall hear the words of my mouth…” (Deut. 32:1). These are the opening words of a poetic song uttered by Moshe not long before his demise. In this passage, the word for “listen” is *haazinu*, while the word for “hear” is *tishma*. A form of the latter word is more famously used in the formula “Hear (*shema*) O Israel, Hashem our God—Hashem is one” (Deut. 6:4). While some Tosafists actually write that the two words are used interchangeably for poetic effect, most commentators reject the concept of synonyms in the Holy Language, and must therefore explain the words thusly. So, what is the difference between the word *shema* and *haazinu*? Furthermore, while Moshe uses *haazinu* for the heavens and *shema* for the earth, the prophet Yishaya uses the exact opposite formulation: “Hear O heavens, and listen O earth, for Hashem has spoken” (Isa. 1:2). In this context, Yishaya uses *haazinu* for the earth, and *shema* for the heavens. Why does Yishaya deviate from the norm already established by Moshe?

The Midrash (*Sifri* to *Parshat Haazinu*) explains that these two terms reflect two types of listening. One type of listening refers to hearing something from afar, while the other type of listening refers to hearing something nearby. When one listens to something from a distance, he must be especially attentive to the sound in order to properly concentrate, hear what should be heard, focus on its meaning. According to the Midrash, *shema* refers to listening from a distance, while *haazinu* refers to listening from close-range. (Other commentators, such as Chizkuni, Abarbanel, and Rabbi Yitzchak Arama, disagree with the Midrash and actually define the terms in the opposite way and explain the difference between Moshe and Yishaya accordingly. Rabbi Chaim Ibn Attar, in his work *Ohr ha-Chaim*, recognizes both ways of explaining the differences between these two words.)

Based on this, the Midrash explains that Moshe was closer to the heavens, so he used *haazinu* to refer to the heavens listening to him, while he was farther from the earth, so he used *shema* when referring to the earth listening to him. Conversely, Yishaya was closer to the earth, so he used *haazinu* for the earth, and only used *shema* for the heavens.

However, this explanation begs the question: If both Moshe and Yishaya were prophets of G-d, then why is Moshe considered “closer to the heavens” and Yishaya considered “closer to the earth”? The commentators offer several ways of differentiating between Moshe and Yishaya in this context. The first answer argues that because Moshe pronounced his epic song in the days before his death, he was considered “closer to the heavens” simply because his death was approaching and he already had “one foot” in the heavens; whilst the passage from Yishaya was at the start of his prophetic career, well before his death.

The second answer explains that although Moshe and Yishaya were two of the most important prophets, the importance of Moshe infinitely exceeds that of Yishaya. Moshe was the father of all prophets and attained a level of clarity in his prophecy unrivaled by any other prophet. As G-d Himself said of Moshe, “Mouth to mouth do I speak to him, in a clear vision and not in riddles…” (Num. 12:8). While Yishaya’s prophecies served to uphold the Torah, only Moshe’s prophecies *became* the Torah itself. For this reason, Moshe’s elevated spiritual existence rendered him closer to the heavens than to the earth. In contrast, Yishaya, for all that he continuously rebuked the Jews to keep the Torah, remained closer to the earth like an ordinary human being. Similarly, a third answer argues that Moshe was used to ascending to the heavens, as he ascended Mount Sinai multiple times for long stretches, so he is considered “closer to the heavens” than anyone else.

Other sources point to another distinction between the word *haazinu* and *shema*. The word *haazinu* is derived from the Hebrew word *ozen*, which means ear. As such, the verb of listening expressed by the word *haazinu* refers simply to the physiological function of the ear: hearing sound waves and relaying it to the brain. On the other hand, explains the Malbim, the word *shema* does not refer simply to the physical act of listening, rather it also denotes a certain degree of intellectual or emotional understanding of that which is being heard.

*Rokeach* explains that the word *shema* refers to hearkening in response to another’s call, while *haazinu* simply  refers to any type of listening. However, these explanations fail to account for the change in phraseology between the introduction Moshe’s song and Yishaya’s opening prophecy. Elsewhere, *Rokeach* writes that *shema* refers to listening to something which was stated explicitly, while *haazinu* refers to listening and inferring to something only said implicitly.

Rabbi Eliyahu Menachem Margulies (Emunas Eliezer, Yerach HaEisanim ch. 6) explains (based on the Vilna Gaon's comments to Prov. 5:1) that haazanah entails the listener paying closer attention to what is being said than shemiah does. Based on this, he explains why when Lemech asks his wives to reconcile with him, he beseeches them by saying: "Adah and Tzilah hear [shemiah] to my voice / O wives of Lemech, listen [haazanah] to my saying" (Gen. 4:23). In the second part of that verse where Lemech stresses his relationship to his wives, he uses a cognate of haazanah, which connotes a stronger form of "listening" because the relationship of between man and wife demands that closer listening than otherwise. But in the first part of that verse (where Lemech refers to his wives by their first names and does not explicitly refer to them being married to him), he uses the term shemiah, which is a weaker type of listening that does not require as much effort. similarly, when moshe called upon the heavens and the earth to listen to the Song of Haazinu, he used the term haazinu when referring to the heavens and shemiah when referring to the earth (Deut. 32:1), because moses was closer to the heavens, so it would be easier for the heavens to listen more closely (haazinu) than it would be for the earth. By contrast, when Isaiah similarly appealed to the heavens and the earth, he used the term haazinu in reference to the earth and shemiah in reference ot the heavens (Isa. 1:2) because Isaiah was closer to the earth than to the heavens, so it would be easier for the earth to listen more closely (haazinu) than it would be for the heavens (see also midrash tnachuma haazinu 1). Rabbi Margulies further expands onthe difference between these two terms by explaining that the word haazanah is related to the word moznaim ("scale") and as such connotes the listener weighing the merits and drawbacks of what is being said when more deeply considering what he hears. on the other hand, the term shemiah refers to the listener being more passive and simply hearing whatever is being said without contemplating it too much.

Taking this idea a step further, Rabbi Margulies uses the distinction between ha'azanah and shemiah to understand a nuance in the conclusion to the Shofarot prayer recited within the Rosh HaShanah Mussaf. The end of that prayer reads "...for You hear [shemiah] the voice of the shofar and you listen [ha'azanah] to the teruah, and none is like You..." As Rabbi Margulies explains it, "the voice of the shofar" refers to Hashem's trait of rachamim/chesed, which entails bestowing kindness on His creations with no questions asked. In that context, the term shemiah is more appropriate because there is no need to contmeplating or decide between multiple options, as His chesed is unrelenting. On the other hand, the term teruah refers to the sounding of the shofar that is broken up into segements (whether 3 or 9, or 3 and then 9). This breaking up the sound alludes to Hashem's trait of din/gevurah, which entails Him curtailing His kindness to only bestow that influx if/when His creations deserve it. In that modality, He is constantly "evaluating" and weighing whether one should receive His influx, which parallels the idea of ha'azanah which entails "listening" to something and considering/weighing what is being heard.[^304]
