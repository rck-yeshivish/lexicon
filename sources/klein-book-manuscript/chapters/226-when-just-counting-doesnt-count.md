# When Just Counting Doesn’t Count

<!-- Vayikra / Sefiras HaOmer -->

Pekidah – haksav vahakabbalah to ex. 38:21

ח"ש ערך מן: והנגזר עוד מן 'מן', המורה על הסדור - פעל 'מנה' לשון מנין: לִמְנוֹת אֶת-עֲפַר הָאָרֶץ גַּם זַרְעֲךָ יִמָּנֶה (בראשית יג, טז), מִי מָנָה עֲפַר יַעֲקֹב (במדבר כג, י) והוא עשיַת סך הכולל מיחידים רבים. שיש ג' מיני ספירה; ספירה פשוטה שהוא ספירת האחדים בזה אחר זה: א, ב, ג, ד וכו'. על זה הענין הונח פעל "ספר" כמו "וּסְפוֹר הַכּוֹכָבִים" (בראשית טו, ה), "עַד כִּי חָדַל לִסְפּוֹר" (בראשית מא, מט), "וְסָפְרָה לָהּ שִׁבְעַת יָמִים" (ויקרא טו, כח), "תִּסְפְּרוּ חֲמִשִׁים יוֹם" (ויקרא כג, טז). והסך הכולל העולה מן הפרטים יקרא "מספר", "מִסְפַּר הַיָמִים" (שמו"א כז, ז), "מִסְפַּר הַצֹאן" (במדבר לא, לו), "אֶת מִסְפַּר יָמֶיךָ אֲמַלֵּא" (שמות כג, כו).  וכשאין הפרטים שווים ורצופין בזה אחר זה וצריך ללקטם אחת לאחת כדי לעשות מקיבוצם סך כולל הונח על הענין פעל "פקד", והוא לשון זכירה ופקידה שפוקד קצת ומדלג קצת, כמו "אֶת רֹאשׁ בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל לִפְקוּדֵיהֶם" (שמות ל, יב), שלא היו נמנין רק מבן כ' ולמעלה והיה צריך לדלג מבן כ' לבן כ'.  וכן "פְּקוֹד כָּל בְּכוֹר זָכָר" (במדבר ג, מ), "פְּקוֹד אֶת בְּנֵי לֵוִי" (שם ג, טו), שלא היו ג"כ נמנים כי אם מבן חדש ומעלה, והפחות מבן חדש היה מדלג. ואומר "אֶת מִסְפַּר מִפְקַד הָעָם" (שמו"ב כד, ט) דהיינו הסך הכולל של מפקד העם.  ועל התקבץ כללים רבים ולעשות שוב מהם סך כולל, הונח פעל "מנה", והוא מלשון סדור שמסדר הכללים הקטנים תחת כלל אחד גדול, אמר "וְהֶחֱלִף אֶת-מַשְׂכֻּרְתִּי עֲשֶׂרֶת מֹנִים" (בראשית לא, ז) דהיינו עשרה כללים, כוללים עשר פעמים עשר, דהיינו ק' כמו שדרשו חז"ל (בראשית רבה, עד). ואמר "מוֹנֶה מִסְפָּר לַכּוֹכָבִים" (תהלים קמז, ד) שאחר שֶׁמְסַדְרָם לכללים פרטיים, עושה מן הכל שוב כלל אחד כולל.

The Torah commands us to count the days and weeks from Passover until Shavuot (Lev. 23:15–16). Interestingly, the Torah portion which includes this commandment is always read during that time of the year. Counting that forty-nine day/seven week period is known as *Sefirat ha-Omer* (“counting of the *omer*”) because it connects the annual barley sacrifice offered on the second day of Passover (known as the *Korban Omer*) to the annual wheat sacrifice offered on the festival of Shavuot. The word *sefirat* (“counting of”) is based on the verb *sofer* used by the Bible in this commandment to mean “count” (its noun form, *mispar*, means “number). Nonetheless, the Hebrew language has another word for counting: *moneh* (whose final number is called a *minyan*). What is the difference between *sofer/mispar* and *moneh*/*minyan* and why does the Torah choose to specifically use the word *sofer* when talking about *Sefirat ha-Omer*?

We can suggest some possible ideas, but none of these are hard and fast answers: Firstly, the Vilna Gaon (1720–1797) explains that *minyan* is a general count, while *mispar* refers to the specific number in the count. However, on the other hand, R. Wertheimer proffers evidence that *mispar* is the general number, while *mifkad* is the word for a specific count. Secondly, R. Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1740–1814) explains that the word *mispar* is related to the Hebrew word *sippur* (story) because just as the different parts of a story should flow in a natural and logical way, so do the numbers of one counting flow in a logical way (i.e. numerically). One does not count the contents of a set by saying random numbers, just as one does not tell a story by relating unrelated incidents. Nonetheless, this approach ostensibly does not account for how the term *mispar* differs from the term *minyan*.

Rabbi Yaakov Tzvi Mecklenburg (1785–1865) writes the word *moneh* is related to the word *maneh,* which means, “a [respectable] portion”. Thus, the connotation of the word *moneh* is that whatever is included in the set that is counted must be something respectable or important—something worth counting. In contrast, the word *mispar* also means “number,” but especially connotes the use of a number as a limit. For example, when discussing the halachic punishment of flogging, the Torah limits the amount of lashes to only a *mispar* (Deut. 25:2)—which tradition reveals is thirty-nine. For this reason, the Aramaic word for a nation’s border is *sfar* (which *limits* a nation’s territorial domain) and the Aramaic word for barber is *sapar* (because by giving his client a haircut, he limits the growth of his hair). By this rubric, R. Mecklenburg explains that *Sefirat ha-Omer* uses the word *sefirah* because the wording of that commandment reads: “until the morrow of the seventh week you shall count.” The verb “to count” in this context is *being limited* to forty-nine days, so *sefirah* is appropriate, as opposed to *moneh*.

Additionally, R. Mecklenberg suggests that the counting of *sefirah* differs from the counting of *moneh* in that the latter is simply the counting of numbers, while the former denotes something extra deliberately done or required to mark each unit. *Sefirah* is not just a count of quantity, but a count of quality as well. The goal of a *sefirah*-type counting is not to just count the raw numbers, but to also qualitatively improve oneself, to cleanse oneself of impurities. He connects the word *sefirah* to the Hebrew word *sapir* (“sapphire” in English) in that *sefirah* cleanses a person, just as a precious gem is free from impurities.

Rabbi Moshe Shapiro (1935–2017) also focuses on another aspect of "counting," that is, the act of taking something from its source to its highest most possible conclusion. Case in point: when counting one starts with a lower number and tries to reach as high as possible. Rabbi Shapiro explains that this is the intended meaning of the Kabbalistic concept of *Sefirot* (literally, "spheres"), which are different modes by which G-d interacts with the world. Each *Sefirah* is powered by an initial spark of Divine energy, which then rolls into a more concrete ramification. This too represents the idea of taking something from its source and bringing it to fruition. Similarly, the early rabbis are called *Sofrim* (see Babylonian Talmud *Kiddushin* 30a, Jeursalemic Talmud *Brachot* 1:4), which literally means "those who count," because the rabbis took the kernel of revelation found in the Written Torah and brought it to its highest possible conclusion by revealing to us the Oral Torah. *Sefirat HaOmer* thus refers to the process of taking the kernel of Divine Revelation that we saw and experienced in the Exodus and developing it into a more sophisticated understanding of G-d so that we could ready ourselves to accept the Torah, fifty days later.

From a halachic perspective, each day of the Omer may be considered a separate *Mitzvah*, so if an entire day is missed, one should continue counting (albeit without a *bracha*). Many have used the forty-nine day period between Passover and Shavuot to follow a forty-nine step scheme of character development or to focus their energies on the forty-nine ways of acquiring Torah (as listed in the Mishnah Avot ch. 6) or studying a forty-nine page tractate of the Talmud (like *Sotah* or *Shavuot*). Given this model, the purpose of counting the Omer is not necessarily just the destination to reach Shavuot, but the journey itself. If each day, one works on changing oneself into a better person, then not only does the final count have special significance, but each day unto itself has special significance. In this way, counting the Omer differs from, say, the quorum of ten men required for the recitation of certain especially holy parts of the prayers (known as a *minyan*) or the seven times that the Kohen Gadol sprinkles blood towards the Holy of Holies (whose counting the Yom Kippur liturgy describes as *moneh*, not *sofer*) in the final numbers are the only requirements and each individual unit on its own is not necessarily significant.

There is a third set of words for counting/number in the Bible and that is *poked/mifkad,* but we will have to leave that discussion for another time.[^235]
