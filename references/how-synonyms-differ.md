# How apparent synonyms differ

The method behind this guide, drawn from Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein, "Learning to
differentiate between apparent synonyms," in *Babel: The Language Magazine*,
May 2020, pages 30 to 33. What follows is a summary in our own words; the
article itself is his, and worth reading whole. His weekly column on Hebrew
synonyms is at
[ohr.edu](https://articles.ohr.edu/categories/whats-in-a-word), and he is a
founding editor of the [Veromemanu Foundation](https://biblicalhebrewetymology.com).

The question it answers is the one every entry here is an instance of. Hebrew
words are held to carry inherent meaning, yet Tanach, Mishnah and Gemara use
many words that look interchangeable. Either the tradition is wrong about
meaning, or the words are not in fact interchangeable. The rabbinic scholars
held to the second and worked out *how* such pairs differ.

Their answers, the article says, follow set formulae, and one who knows them can
build templates from them and apply those even to other languages. The article
does not number them. The eight headings below are ours, each with the
article's own examples; where an example comes from elsewhere in his column,
the text says so. Naming which one an entry is using makes the distinction
sharper, and keeps a writer from inventing a difference where the real answer
is one of these.


## The eight mechanisms

**1. General against specific (hypernym and hyponym).** One word is the
umbrella, the other a kind falling under it. The rock words show it on one of
the article's two views: *even* is the general word for rock, and *sela*, *tzur*
and *chalamish* name particular kinds. The other view has *even* a rock
detached from the ground and *sela* one still attached to it.

**2. The same thing seen from a different angle.** Both words denote one
referent but foreground different characteristics of it, so neither is wrong
and the choice carries emphasis. The article says this is true of verbs as well
as nouns.

**3. Degree along a scale.** The words rank. For verbs the article pairs degree
with intent: one act, done to different degrees or with different intentions.
The grammarians who make *even* the general word rank the other rock words by
hardness, *sela* below *tzur*, with the place of *chalamish* disputed; one view
of *churban* and *shemamah* makes *shemamah* the more severe destruction.

Rabbi Klein's essay on shame, not the article, takes this a step further.
Bedersi holds that where synonyms stand together in a pasuk, Tanach sets them
from least intense to most, so their order is itself evidence; Radak and
Rabbeinu Yonah read *bushah* before *kelimah* the same way. It is disputed:
Isaac Satanow and Rabbi Yehuda Leib Shapira-Frankfurter read that fixed order as
sequence in time, not intensity.

**4. Act against result.** The article gives three views of *churban* and
*shemamah* in Vayikra 26:31. On the third, *churban* is the destroying, a sort
of cognate of *cherev*, a sword, and *shemamah* is the desolation the destroying
leaves behind. The other two views put the same pair under other headings:
*shemamah* as the general word and *churban* the ruin of a city in particular
(general against specific), or *shemamah* as the graver destruction (degree).
One pair can fall under more than one mechanism, depending on whose view is
followed.

**5. Scope or setting.** The article counts eight Hebrew words for road or path.
Some explain *derech* as the intercity road and *rechov* as the road within a
town; others make *derech* the longer or wider road and *netiv* the shorter or
narrower; the Zohar makes *derech* open to the public and *netiv* private, for
select individuals. *Mesilah* is explained as a paved path, *mishol* a
footpath, *maagal* a path that runs in a circle, from *igul*, and *shvil* one
that runs steeply downhill, from *shafel*, low.

**6. Speaker's stance.** Two words render one English word while carrying
opposite attitudes. *Ulai* and *pen* both mean "maybe", but Hebrew grammarians
as early as the fifteenth century, in the mold of Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (Elias
Levita, 1469 to 1549), held that Hebrew has no neutral word for it: *ulai*
carries the speaker's acceptance of the possible outcome, *pen* the speaker's
rejection of it. *Ulai* is for the raffle you hope to win, *pen* for the illness
you insure against. English "maybe" is neutral, and the article says it does
not do justice to either.

**7. Different language.** *Eitz* is Hebrew, *ilan* is Aramaic; all six
biblical appearances of *ilan* are in the Aramaic of Daniel, between 4:7 and
4:23. They mean the same thing and are still not synonyms, because they belong
to different languages. Under Aramaic influence *ilan* became the ordinary
Rabbinic word for tree and *eitz* was kept for wood. The article adds that
Hebrew sometimes borrows a word from another language, most often a Semitic
one, to make a point or for poetic effect, and that the borrowed word may mean
exactly what the Hebrew word does.

**8. Loanword against native word.** Later Hebrew writing borrows from Greek,
Latin, German and even English. A borrowed word can stand beside a native one
as though the two were synonyms, and the article's point is that their origins
show they are not wholly the same. His essay on scribes gives a case, *lavlar*,
from Latin, beside *sofer*; whether those two differ in meaning as well as in
origin is disputed there.

A ninth case is not a distinction at all but belongs on the list: **historical
displacement**, where one word supplants another that meant the same, and the
older word may still turn up now and then beside the newer one. When that is the
true account, say so rather than manufacture a difference.

## The lexicographers

The article names seven rabbis who devoted whole works to synonyms, writing
lexicons that set out how words differ, the opposite approach to a thesaurus
such as J. I. Rodale's *The Synonym Finder*. It gives them as examples, not as a
closed list. Most entries here cite at least one of them, alongside a much wider
circle of commentators and grammarians.

## Using this

When drafting, ask which mechanism is at work before writing the distinction.
If none of them fits and no authority draws the line, the honest entry says the
sources do not separate the words, which is a real finding and not a failure.

Where the mechanism is clear, name it in the prose in ordinary words: *the
general term against three specific kinds*, *the act against what it leaves
behind*, *Hebrew against Aramaic*. The reader then has the shape of the answer
as well as the answer.
