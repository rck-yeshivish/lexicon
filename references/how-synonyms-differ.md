# How apparent synonyms differ

This page is a summary, in our own words, of Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's article
"Learning to differentiate between apparent synonyms," *Babel: The Language
Magazine*, May 2020, pages 30 to 33. The article itself is his, and worth
reading in full. Rabbi Klein writes the weekly column *What's in a Word?* at
[ohr.edu](https://articles.ohr.edu/categories/whats-in-a-word), and is a
founding editor of the [Veromemanu Foundation](https://biblicalhebrewetymology.com).

## The question

Jewish tradition holds that every Hebrew word carries a meaning of its own. Yet
Tanach, the Mishnah and the Gemara are full of words that seem to mean the same
thing. If each word has its own meaning, why would the language need two words
for one idea?

Many rabbinic scholars held that Hebrew has no true synonyms, and set out to
show how each such pair differs. Their answers tend to follow a few patterns.
Rabbi Klein's article goes through them with examples. He does not number them;
the list below is our arrangement of what he says.

## Eight ways the words differ

**1. One word is general, the other specific.** One word covers the whole
category, and the other names one kind within it. On one view in the article,
*even* is any stone, while *sela*, *tzur* and *chalamish* are particular kinds
of rock. Another view says *even* is a stone detached from the ground and
*sela* is rock still attached to it.

**2. The same thing, seen from different sides.** Both words name the same
thing, but each points to a different feature of it. This holds for verbs as
well as nouns.

**3. Stronger and weaker.** The words differ in degree. With verbs, two words
can describe the same act done to a different degree, or with a different
intention. Those who make *even* the general word also rank the other rock
words by hardness: *sela* is softer than *tzur*, and where *chalamish* falls is
disputed.

**4. The act, and what it leaves behind.** Vayikra 26:31 uses two words for
destruction, *churban* and *shemamah*, and the article brings three
explanations. On one, *churban* is the act of destroying (related to *cherev*,
a sword), and *shemamah* is the empty desolation left afterwards. The other two
explanations put the pair under other headings: *shemamah* as the general word
and *churban* as the destruction of a city in particular, or *shemamah* as the
more severe of the two.

**5. Different settings.** Hebrew has at least eight words for a road or path.
Some say a *derech* runs between cities and a *rechov* within one. Others say a
*derech* is longer or wider than a *netiv*. The Zohar says a *derech* is open
to everyone, and a *netiv* is private, for a few. A *mesilah* is explained as a
paved road, a *mishol* as a footpath, a *maagal* as a path that goes around in
a circle (from *igul*, circle), and a *shvil* as a path that slopes steeply
down (from *shafel*, low).

**6. The speaker's attitude.** *Ulai* and *pen* both mean "maybe", yet they are
close to opposites. Grammarians as early as the fifteenth century, like Rabbi
Eliyahu HaBachur (Elias Levita, 1469 to 1549), held that Hebrew has no neutral
word for "maybe". *Ulai* is said of an outcome the speaker accepts or hopes
for: "I'll enter the raffle; maybe I'll win." *Pen* is said of one the speaker
rejects and hopes will not happen: "I'll buy insurance; maybe I'll get sick."
English "maybe" is neutral, and the article says it does justice to neither.

**7. Two languages.** *Eitz* and *ilan* both mean tree, but *eitz* is Hebrew and
*ilan* is Aramaic. All six times *ilan* appears in Tanach are in the Aramaic
part of Daniel (4:7 to 4:23). Later, under Aramaic influence, *ilan* became the
usual word for tree in the Hebrew of *Chazal*, and *eitz* came to mean wood. The
article adds that Hebrew sometimes borrows a word from another language, often
a Semitic one, to make a point or for poetic effect, and the borrowed word may
mean exactly what the Hebrew word means.

**8. Borrowed words.** Later Hebrew writing also borrows from Greek, Latin,
German and even English. A borrowed word can stand beside a Hebrew word as if
the two were synonyms, but the article says their different origins show they
are not exactly the same.

**One more: an old word replaced.** Sometimes one word takes the place of an
older one that meant the same thing, and the older word still turns up now and
then beside the newer one. The two mean the same; one is older.

## The rabbis who wrote on synonyms

A thesaurus gathers words that mean the same thing. The article names seven
rabbis who wrote books on how such words differ. It gives them as examples, not
as a full list, and most entries on this site cite at least one of them.

| Rabbi | Work |
|---|---|
| Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini (1230 to 1300) | *Chosam Tochnis* |
| Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim of Breslau (1714 to 1814) | *Cheshek Shlomo*, *Yerios Shlomo* |
| Rabbi Eliyahu Kramer of Vilna, the Vilna Gaon (1720 to 1797) | Cited by Rabbi Klein from his commentaries, most often on Mishlei |
| Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel (1760 to 1828) | *Afikei Yehudah* |
| Rabbi Meir Leibush Weiser, the Malbim (1809 to 1879) | *Yair Ohr* |
| Rabbi Shimon Dovber Analak of Siedlce (1848 to 1907) | *Imrei Rashad*, in its section *Shem MiShimon* |
| Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer (1866 to 1935) | *Beiurei Shemos Nirdafim* |

The article names only Bedersi's book. The others are named in Rabbi Klein's
own writing elsewhere.
