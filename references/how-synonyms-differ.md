# How apparent synonyms differ

What follows is our summary of Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein's article "Learning to
differentiate between apparent synonyms," *Babel: The Language Magazine*, May
2020, pages 30 to 33. The article itself is his, and worth reading in full.
Rabbi Klein writes the weekly column *What's in a Word?* at
[ohr.edu](https://articles.ohr.edu/categories/whats-in-a-word), and is a
founding editor of the [Veromemanu Foundation](https://biblicalhebrewetymology.com).

## The question

As a boy in Los Angeles, Rabbi Klein wondered why one street was called an
avenue, another a boulevard, a third a way and a fourth simply a street. Who
decided which name went with which road? Hebrew raises the same question, and
more sharply. It has at least eight words for a road or path, and many other
sets of words that seem to mean one thing.

Jewish tradition sees Hebrew as a holy tongue whose words express the essence
of what they describe. The Kabbalists pointed out that the Hebrew for "word" and
for "thing" is the same, *davar*, reflecting the view that everything in
Creation is a Divine word crystallized into material form. But if every word
carries a meaning of its own, why would Tanach, the Mishnah and the Gemara need
several words for one idea? More than one would be redundant.

Many rabbinic scholars answered that Hebrew has no true synonyms at all. Words
that look alike turn out, on a closer look, to differ, and the ways they differ
follow a handful of patterns. Once we know the patterns, Rabbi Klein suggests,
we can bring them to apparent synonyms in any language. He does not number
them; the eight below are our arrangement of what he says, with his examples.

## Eight ways the words differ

**1. General and specific.** Sometimes one word is what linguists call a
*hypernym*, an umbrella term, and the other a *hyponym*, one kind that falls
under it. Take the words for rock: *even*, *sela*, *tzur* and *chalamish*. Some
say *even* is the general word for any rock, and the others name particular
kinds. Others say *even* is a stone detached from the ground, and *sela* is
rock still attached to it. See [even and sela](entries/rock-even-sela.html), and
[tzur and chalamish](entries/rock-tzur-chalamish.html).

**2. The same thing, seen from different sides.** Two words can name exactly
the same thing, while each draws attention to a different feature of it. This
happens with verbs as much as with nouns.

**3. Stronger and weaker.** Sometimes the difference is one of degree. With
verbs, two words may describe one act done to a greater or lesser degree, or
with a different intention. Those who make *even* the general word rank the
other rock words by hardness: *sela* is softer than *tzur*, and where
*chalamish* falls is disputed.

**4. The act, and what it leaves behind.** Vayikra 26:31 warns of destruction
with two words, *churban* and *shemamah*. Since the commentators assume they
cannot mean exactly the same, they explain them three ways. Some make *shemamah*
the general word and *churban* the destruction of a city in particular. Others
make *shemamah* the more severe of the two. A third view says *churban* is the
act of destroying (related to *cherev*, a sword), and *shemamah* is the empty
desolation it leaves behind. See [churban and
shemamah](entries/destruction-churban-shemamah.html).

**5. Different settings.** Back to the roads. Some explain that a *derech* runs
between cities, and a *rechov* runs within one. Others say a *derech* is longer
or wider than a *netiv*. The Zohar says a *derech* is open to everyone, while a
*netiv* is private, for a select few. A *mesilah* is a paved road, a *mishol* a
footpath, a *maagal* a path that goes around in a circle (from *igul*, circle),
and a *shvil* a path that slopes steeply downward (from *shafel*, low). See
[derech and orach](entries/path-derech-orach.html), and [the other
paths](entries/paths-netiv-mesilah-shvil-mishol.html).

**6. The speaker's attitude.** *Ulai* and *pen* both mean "maybe", yet in a
sense they are opposites. English "maybe" is neutral: it does not tell us
whether the speaker wants the thing to happen. Grammarians as early as the
fifteenth century, like Rabbi Eliyahu HaBachur (Elias Levita, 1469 to 1549),
held that Hebrew has no neutral word for "maybe". *Ulai* means the speaker
accepts or hopes for the outcome: "I'll enter the raffle, because maybe I'll
win." *Pen* means the speaker rejects it and hopes it will not happen: "I'll buy
insurance, because maybe I'll get sick." See [ulai and
pen](entries/maybe-ulai-pen.html).

**7. Two languages.** In most places Tanach calls a tree *eitz*, but sometimes
it says *ilan*. Look closely and all six times are in the Aramaic part of Daniel
(4:7 to 4:23). *Ilan* is Aramaic, not Hebrew, so the two are not truly
synonyms, even though they mean the same thing. After Tanach, Aramaic's pull on
Hebrew was strong enough that *ilan* became the usual word for tree in the
Hebrew of *Chazal*, and *eitz* came to mean wood. Hebrew also borrows a word
from another language now and then, often a Semitic one, to make a point or for
poetic effect, and such a word may mean exactly what the Hebrew word does. See
[eitz and ilan](entries/tree-eitz-ilan.html).

**8. Borrowed words.** Later Hebrew writing borrows from Greek, Latin, German
and even English. Such a word can stand beside a native Hebrew one as if the
two were synonyms, but their different origins show they are not quite the
same.

**One more: an old word replaced.** Sometimes history simply replaces one word
with another that means the same thing, and the older word may still turn up
now and then beside the newer one. Here the two words do mean the same; one is
just older.

## The rabbis who wrote on synonyms

Insights into apparent synonyms are scattered through rabbinic writing, from
the Talmud to our own day, and some rabbis wrote whole books on the subject.
Where a thesaurus such as J. I. Rodale's *The Synonym Finder* gathers words that
mean the same, these books show how such words differ. The article names seven
of these rabbis, and most entries on this site cite at least one of them:

- **Rabbi Avraham Bedersi HaPenini** (1230 to 1300), *Chosam Tochnis*
- **Rabbi Shlomo Pappenheim** of Breslau (1714 to 1814), *Cheshek Shlomo* and
  *Yerios Shlomo*
- **Rabbi Eliyahu Kramer of Vilna, the Vilna Gaon** (1720 to 1797), cited by
  Rabbi Klein from his commentaries, most often on Mishlei
- **Rabbi Yehuda Leib Edel** (1760 to 1828), *Afikei Yehudah*
- **Rabbi Meir Leibush Weiser, the Malbim** (1809 to 1879), *Yair Ohr*
- **Rabbi Shimon Dovber Analak** of Siedlce (1848 to 1907), *Imrei Rashad*, in
  its section *Shem MiShimon*
- **Rabbi Shlomo Aharon Wertheimer** (1866 to 1935), *Beiurei Shemos Nirdafim*

The article names only Bedersi's book; the others come from Rabbi Klein's other
writing.
