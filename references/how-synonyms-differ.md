# How apparent synonyms differ

The method behind this guide, drawn from Rabbi Reuven Chaim Klein, "Learning to
differentiate between apparent synonyms," in *Babel: The Language Magazine*,
May 2020, pages 30 to 33. What follows is a summary in our own words; the
article itself is his, and worth reading whole. His weekly column on Hebrew
synonyms is at
[ohr.edu](https://articles.ohr.edu/categories/whats-in-a-word), and he writes
for the [Veromemanu Foundation](https://biblicalhebrewetymology.com).

The question it answers is the one every entry here is an instance of. Hebrew
words are held to carry inherent meaning, yet Tanach, Mishnah and Gemara use
many words that look interchangeable. Either the tradition is wrong about
meaning, or the words are not in fact interchangeable. The rabbinic
lexicographers took the second view and worked out *how* such pairs differ.

Those ways form a short list. Naming which one an entry is using makes the
distinction sharper, and keeps a writer from inventing a difference where the
real answer is one of these.

## The eight mechanisms

**1. General against specific (hypernym and hyponym).** One word is the
umbrella, the other a kind falling under it. *Even* is the general word for
rock; *sela*, *tzur* and *chalamish* name particular kinds.

**2. The same thing seen from a different angle.** Both words denote one
referent but foreground different characteristics of it, so neither is wrong
and the choice carries emphasis.

**3. Degree along a scale.** The words rank. The rock cluster is ranked by
hardness; *bushah* and *kelimah* by intensity of shame. When a set ranks, the
order words appear in a pasuk is itself evidence, which is Bedersi's method.

**4. Act against result.** *Churban* is the destroying, cognate with *cherev*,
a sword; *shemamah* is the desolation that the destroying leaves behind.

**5. Scope or setting.** *Derech* is the intercity road, *rechov* the road
within a town; *derech* is public where *netiv* is private and for the few (the
Zohar's reading); *mesilah* is paved, *mishol* is a footpath, *maagal* runs in
a circle, *shvil* runs downhill, from *shafel*, low.

**6. Speaker's stance.** Two words render one English word while carrying
opposite attitudes. *Ulai* and *pen* both mean "maybe", but *ulai* accepts the
outcome and *pen* wards it off: *ulai* for the raffle you hope to win, *pen*
for the illness you insure against. English "maybe" is neutral and loses this
entirely, so a translation has to supply the stance some other way.

**7. Different language.** *Eitz* is Hebrew, *ilan* is Aramaic; every one of the
six biblical appearances of *ilan* sits in the Aramaic passages of Daniel. They
mean the same thing and are still not synonyms, because they belong to different
languages. Under Aramaic influence *ilan* became the ordinary Rabbinic word for
tree and *eitz* narrowed to wood.

**8. Loanword against native word.** Rabbinic Hebrew borrows from Greek, Latin,
German and later English. A borrowed word standing beside a native one may look
like a synonym while differing in register or in precise sense, as *lavlar*
does beside *sofer*.

A ninth case is not a distinction at all but worth naming: **historical
displacement**, where one word simply supplants another and the older survives
in fossilised phrases. When that is the true account, say so rather than
manufacture a difference.

## Using this

When drafting, ask which mechanism is at work before writing the distinction.
If none of them fits and no authority draws the line, the honest entry says the
sources do not separate the words, which is a real finding and not a failure.

Where the mechanism is clear, name it in the prose in ordinary words: *the
general term against three specific kinds*, *the act against what it leaves
behind*, *Hebrew against Aramaic*. The reader then has the shape of the answer
as well as the answer.
